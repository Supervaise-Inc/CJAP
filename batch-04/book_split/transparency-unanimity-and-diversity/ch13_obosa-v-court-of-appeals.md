# Chapter 13: Obosa v. Court of Appeals: Authority to Approve Bail Bonds in Appealed Cases

I will begin my discussion of Obosa v. Court of Appeals! with a synopsis of the facts, issues and ruling.

## Facts

Jose T. Obosa and three others were charged with the double murder of then Secretary of Local Governments Jaime N. Ferrer and his driver Jesus D. Calderon. Because murder was (and still is) a capital offense, Obosa was not allowed bail and was thus detained during the trial. At the time of the killing, he was an escapee from the National Penitentiary at Muntinlupa where he was serving a prison term for robbery. He was escorted out of prison by virtue of a subpoena illegally issued by a judge of the MTC of Sariaya, Quezon, who had purportedly needed his presence so that he could inquire on the whereabouts of the accused. While out of prison, Obosa was implicated in the ambush-slaying of Ferrer and Calderon.

In its May 31, 1990 Decision, the RTC of Makati found Obosa guilty of homicide, not murder, on two counts. On the same date, it granted his Manifestation to appeal and to post bail. Thereafter, on June 1, 1990, the lower court approved the bail bond and issued an Order of release. The prison authorities at the National Penitentiary promptly complied with the Order on June 4, 1990, notwithstanding that he was also serving a prison term for a separate case of robbery.

Acting on a Motion filed by the Office of the Solicitor General (OSG), a special five-person Division of the Court of Appeals (CA) issued a Resolution canceling the bail bond of Obosa, nullifying the trial court’s Order granting bail, and issuing a warrant for his immediate arrest. Hence, he elevated the matter to the Supreme Court.

## Issue

1. Did the trial court have jurisdiction to approve

the petitioner's bail bond?

2. Was petitioner entitled to bail as a matter of right pending review of his conviction for homicide?

## Ruling

1. No, the trial court had no such jurisdiction. It had the authority to grant bail at the time it did, but not to approve the bail bond and issue the Release Order after the appeal had already been perfected.

2. No, petitioner was not entitled to bail. True, homicide was a bailable offense, but the Information charged him with murder, which was non-bailable. At the time of his conviction, the rules on bail, prior to their amendment by Administrative Circular 12-94, did not favor his cause. In Quemel v. CA et al., the Court has held that an appeal in a criminal case opens for review of the whole case including the factual questions, and even the penalty which may be increased. Thus, the possibility of conviction for the original charge is ever present. Likewise, if the prosecution has previously demonstrated that the evidence of guilt is strong, as it had done in the present case, such determination subsists even on appeal despite conviction for a lesser offense.

While the grant of bail is initially at the discretion of the trial court, that discretion should be exercised with caution and only for strong reasons, with the end in view of upholding law and justice. It is thus best to lodge in the appellate court the discretion on whether to grant bail in appealed cases.

Although unanimously concurred in by the Supreme Court, my ponencia drew some flak, especially from the academe. The critics cited the general rule that gave the trial court discretion on whether to grant bail or not. They emphasized that if the records of the case had not yet been forwarded to the appellate court, the jurisdiction of the trial court would not end with the mere filing of a notice of appeal. Apart from purely doctrinal justifications, there was also a practical consideration behind this rule: while the records were still with the trial court, the appellate court would not be able to act, since it would have no documents on which to base its actions.

Upon the other hand, I had to weigh the special circumstances in this case. Here was an escapee from the National Penitentiary who had been charged with murder, but convicted of homicide only. If he was released on bail while his case was pending appeal, he could, as he had done already, commit other crimes. Worse, if the appellate court convicted him of murder and sentenced him to reclusion perpetua, he would in all probability jump bail and disappear. Thus, I thought that it would be a bad judicial policy to allow him bail on appeal.

Parenthetically, the trial judge (Nemesio Felix of the Makati RTC) could not be completely faulted for granting bail. Based on his judgment after trial, the accused was guilty, not of murder, but only of homicide which was a bailable offense. But the Court of Appeals, upon a quick review of the records, adjudged him unworthy of bail. After all, he had been accused of murder and the conviction for homicide was not yet final.

Given this peculiar set of circumstances, I thought it best, and the Court agreed, that the discretion on whether to grant bail in a case involving an appealed capital offense, in which conviction was rendered for a bailable crime, should be lodged in the appellate court, not the trial court.

The Supreme Court deliberated, from July to September 2000, on the proposed revisions in the Rules on Criminal Procedure. It decided to uphold my stand that the appellate court, not the court of origin, should be given the discretion on whether (0 grant bail to a person who had been accused of a non-bailable offense, but convicted by the lower court'of a bailable crime only. Hence, a new proviso was included in Rule 114, which reads as follows:

“SEC. 5. Bail when discretionary. — Upon conviction by the Regional Trial Court of an offense not punishable by death, reclusion perpetua, or life imprisonment, admission to bail is discretionary. The application for bail may be filed and acted upon by the trial court despite the filing of a notice of appeal, provided it has not transmitted the original record to the appellate court. However, if the decision of the trial court convicting the accused changed the nature of the offense from non-bailable to bailable, the application for bail can only be filed with and resolved by the appellate court.” (bold types supplied)

In hindsight, I believe the Court judiciously decided Obosa, if I may say so myself. The wisdom of this Decision was upheld by these two subsequent events:

a. On November 27, 1997, the Court of Appeals found Obosa guilty of double murder and sentenced him to two terms of reclusion perpetua.

b. On May 31, 2000, the Supreme Court, in People v. Obosa,! affirmed his conviction in toto.

Had he been granted bail and he thereafter absconded pending review of his appeal by the CA, the appeal would have been dismissed and the trial court’s Decision, holding him liable for homicide only, would have become final. In other words, by being granted and thereafter jumping bail, he would have made a mockery of judicial processes.
