# Reforming the Judiciary -- Ch. 4: Prerequisites for a Successful Reform Program

The Action Program for Judicial Reform (or APJR) is the most comprehensive judicial reform project ever undertaken by the Supreme Court of the Philippines. We realize, however, that its viability must be based on a thorough understanding of many variables that affect the problems and the possible solutions. Thus, in  initiating our current reform program, we took into account ten (10) prerequisites or key factors to assure its successful implementation. Allow me to discuss them one by one.

## Comprehensive Diagnosis

As a first step, we undertook a comprehensive review and diagnosis of the present state of the Philippine judicial system. With the help of in-house officials and external experts, we assessed our environment and our institutional capacity including our strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and the threats (SWOT) confronting us. The diagnostics revealed their nature, causes and effects. Thus, we were able to put into place a baseline from which to identify our goals and activities.

## 2. Review of Past Reforms

We also reviewed all past Philippine judicial reform efforts, so that we could learn from their successes and failures. A better understanding of the concepts formulated and the methods used certainly helped us develop a more effective program of action. Based on these studies, we adopted quite a number of recommendations that are useful to our present efforts.

## 3. Involvement of Stakeholders

In the reform program, it is likewise important to consider the consensus of the stakeholders - judges, court personnel, litigants, practicing lawyers, law professors and law students. These are the people who are inherently involved in the judicial system, and who must share common ground and vision. Around the country, we held workshops and dialogues that opened communications among them within the framework of the program. Thus, we can truly say that all affected parties had substantive involvement in developing and reviewing objectives and conceptual approaches; as well as in identifying and prioritizing projects, setting time frames and others. These are being further structured and refined through individual consultations and group discussions.

## 4. Facts Versus Perception

The diagnostics earlier mentioned provided the facts about the real or actual state of the judicial system. Early on, however, we realized that the key to developing a sound reform program was the ability to distinguish fact from perception. Thus, we also conducted perception surveys that provided an impression of how judges, court personnel and external stakeholders viewed the gravity of the problems. From such surveys we likewise gauged the level of public confidence in the existing judicial system. Accordingly, we were able to ground the reform program on a realistic assessment of the facts that would make our project goals more viable and consequently enhance the public image of the judiciary.

## 5. Consideration of the Global Perspective

Another important point we considered was the application of a global perspective to our program. We avoided costly reform pitfalls by drawing lessons from relevant international experiences and practices. Hence, a comparative analysis of the reform programs of other countries that had proven successful helped us develop our own. At the same time, we recognized that our judicial system had certain cultural peculiarities that might not fit into certain methodologies successfully used in other places. In short, we kept an open and dynamic approach while adopting relevant aspects of the global perspective.

## 6. Holistic Approach to Design and Implementation

Using the holistic approach, we identified and defined the essential components of the reform program: institution, policy procedure, and resources. The interdependent and mutually reinforcing relationships among these components were paramount considerations in our development and management of reforms. To promote an efficient coexistence among them, we tailored our methods or approaches with this end in mind.

## 7. Starting Points, Sequencing, Time Frames and Targets

Another important consideration in the implementation of our reform program was the proper or chronological execution of its various components in relation to sequencing targets as well as short-term and long-term goals. In this manner, we hoped to avoid backtracking, delay and waste of valuable resources.

## 8. Working Within Available Resources

Realistic goals that were attainable within the confines of available resources were set. We explored all possible avenues to generate funding support and invoked the assistance of agencies and other entities that were willing and capable of financing our reform program. Moreover, we developed a public investment program to organize the reform activities, schedule them, and calibrate the levels of resource infusions. By allocating specific funding for specific reform projects, we hoped to be able to avoid duplication and overlapping of activities and resources.

## 9. Clear Institutional Framework for Reform Management

Indispensable to a sound reform management process is the establishment of a project management setup with a definite structure, streamlined work flow and dedicated and skilled personnel. Thus, the Supreme Court created an independent Program Management Office (PMO) under the Office of the Chief Justice to oversee and supervise the judicial reform program.

## 10. Design and Implementation of a Monitoring and Evaluation System

The PMO has been tasked to monitor and evaluate the progress of reform activities and outputs based on the activities scheduled and resources allocated in a multi-year time frame. By defining performance indicators and milestone targets, the PMO will provide a vital data base for the evaluation of program accomplishments as well as for the implementation and management of forthcoming activities in anticipation of problems that may arise.

In a nutshell, the foregoing were the ten key factors that we have taken into account to assure ourselves of success in our reform program. The responsibilities have been great and the challenges daunting. But having acquired and harnessed internal and external expertise to undertake the tasks, and having ensured strong and sustained top management commitment, we have been blessed with a smooth flowing reform process. Indeed, we daresay that, with the Philippine judicial reform program on the right track, prospects for success are not too far ahead.

## Summary

Chapter 4 of 'Reforming the Judiciary' set out the ten prerequisites or key factors that the Supreme Court took into account in initiating the Action Program for Judicial Reform (APJR), which Justice Panganiban described as the most comprehensive judicial reform project ever undertaken by the Court, adding that its viability had to rest on a thorough understanding of the many variables affecting both the problems and the possible solutions. He listed them in order. First, a comprehensive diagnosis: with in-house officials and external experts, the Court reviewed the present state of the judicial system and assessed its environment and institutional capacity through a SWOT analysis, establishing a baseline from which goals and activities could be identified. Second, a review of all past Philippine judicial reform efforts so that their successes and failures could instruct the new program, with a number of their recommendations adopted. Third, involvement of stakeholders -- judges, court personnel, litigants, practicing lawyers, law professors and law students -- through nationwide workshops and dialogues, so that all affected parties had substantive involvement in setting objectives, prioritizing projects and fixing time frames. Fourth, facts versus perception: he maintained that the key to a sound reform program was the ability to distinguish the two, so perception surveys of judges, court personnel and external stakeholders were run alongside the factual diagnostics and also gauged public confidence in the system. Fifth, a global perspective, drawing lessons from successful foreign reform programs while recognizing that the Philippine system had cultural peculiarities that might not fit methodologies used elsewhere. Sixth, a holistic approach treating institution, policy procedure and resources as interdependent components. Seventh, proper sequencing, time frames and targets to avoid backtracking, delay and waste. Eighth, working within available resources, with a public investment program to schedule activities and calibrate funding and so avoid duplication. Ninth, a clear institutional framework: an independent Program Management Office under the Office of the Chief Justice. Tenth, a monitoring and evaluation system run by that office on performance indicators and milestone targets over a multi-year time frame. He closed by conceding that the responsibilities had been great and the challenges daunting, but reported that harnessed internal and external expertise and sustained top management commitment had produced a smooth flowing reform process, with prospects for success not too far ahead.
