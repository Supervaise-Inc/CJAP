The Supreme Court (SC) is basically a review court.21 This means that its work consists mainly of affirming, modifying or reversing decisions and

As a rule, the Court does not take initial cognizance of controversies. It merely reviews decisions of other tribunals which have acted on these controversies. In fact, it does not initiate such review. Its review authority must be triggered by the filing of a petition by a proper party. In this sense, the judiciary is a passive branch of the government. It cannot act on a controversy unless asked to do so.23

In trial courts,24 the proceedings are open to the public. But in collegiate appellate courts, particularly the Supreme Court, the internal deliberations of jurists are confidential; only their net results, as written in the form of decisions, resolutions, orders and opinions (concurring, dissenting and separate), are released to the public. Emphasizing the sacredness of collegial deliberations, Justice Florenz D. Regalado (who is retiring on October 13, 1998, shortly after my third anniversary) aptly said, "In trial courts, the rule is transparency; but in the Supreme Court, it is confidentiality."

## The Collegial Nature of Court Decisions and Actions

The members of the Court must act and vote independently of each other; yet they must at the same time exercise collegial rule, whether en banc or in division. Thus, all court actions and decisions are determined during sessions. Except in few very urgent instances, no justice, not even the Chief Justice, can act individually to bind the Court. Even simple motions for extension of time to file pleadings are calendared and acted upon by the Court as a collegial body.

The agenda of the Court (whether en banc or in division) usually consists of about 150 to 250 items per session. The Court patiently disposes of each of these items, but the backbreaking job is done in chambers and even at home, when justices study, read, reflect, pray and write on the matters assigned to them. Of course, prior to the session, they need also to read and study the written reports of their fellow justices,25 so that they can express concurrence or dissent, or at least intelligently discuss

Oral arguments are usually held in the cavernous and rather intimidating hearing hall of the Court located on the second floor of the Supreme Court main building on Padre Faura Street. Oral arguments, which are rare, are usually held only in cases involving difficult and complicated questions. The Court delineates the issues; and the lawyers for each side, who are expected to come extensively prepared, dwell on these issues within the allotted time, normally twenty minutes each. However, members of the Court may, and usually do, ask questions on any topic, issue or matter. Hence, the allotted time is usually extended.

In oral arguments on very important cases, the Court usually appoints amici curiae (friends of the court) to assist the justices in resolving difficult questions of law. An invitation to act as amicus curiae is a rare privilege and honor, granted only to lawyers26 of unquestioned stature, competence and lucidity.

After the oral arguments, the lawyers are usually required to follow up and close their cases with the filing of written memoranda.

## The Collegial Nature Aspects of Court Sessions

The Court regularly sits en banc (all members of the Court) and in division (five members each).27 To grant a motion or petition and to approve a decision or resolution, the concurrence of a majority of those taking part is sufficient.28 The distribution of the justices to the three Divisions is determined by the Chief Justice, who usually spreads the senior and the junior justices more or less evenly among the three groups.

Seniority based on the date of appointment is strictly observed in seating arrangements. En banc, the Chief Justice sits at the head of the table, with the most senior justice on his right and the next most senior on his left. The third most senior sits next to the first, the fourth most senior sits next to the second, and so on down to the fifteenth who sits at the foot of the table on the Chief Justice' left. ## The Three Rounds in the Court

It is said that, as in an amateur boxing match, there are three rounds in the Supreme Court. The first round usually begins upon the filing of a petition32 (or a motion for extension of time to file a petition) or a notice of appeal.

In general, the acceptance of any appeal or petition, as well as the grant of due course thereto, is addressed to the sound discretion of the Supreme Court. 33 With few exceptions like appeals of decisions imposing death or reclusion perpetua, the Court may dismiss motu proprio any petition or appeal before it. In such cases, the decision or order questioned in the petition is deemed upheld.34

### The "Second Round"

In case the Court is satisfied that the petition is prima facie meritorious and not procedurally or substantially defective, the "Second Round" begins. The Court will require the respondents to file their comment(s). After receipt of the comments, the Court may resolve to give due course to the petition, in which case it will normally require the parties to file their respective memoranda. Or the Court may direct the petitioners to submit a reply to the comment(s); the respondent(s), a rejoinder; and the petitioners, even a sur-rejoinder thereafter.

After this exchange of written arguments, the Court may grant due course to the petition and ask the parties to file memoranda. Alternatively, it may issue an extended resolution denying or dismissing56 the petition, usually unsigned by the justices but certified by the clerk of court or by his or her deputy who quotes the minute resolution from the minutes of the session.

As stated earlier, only the justices are present during the sessions.57 The minutes of the session are prepared by the Division chairman or, in case of en banc sessions, by the Chief Justice. Some lawyers and litigants wrongly believe that unsigned extended resolutions are composed by the clerk of

### The "Third Round"

The labor sector may ask: Will the assumption by the Court of Appeals (CA) of jurisdiction over NLRC cases just unduly prolong their adjudication, inasmuch as CA decisions are appealable to the SC anyway? No. First, the CA, which is composed of 51 members divided into 17 Divisions68 of 3 members each, can act on these cases much faster than the SC. Second, not all CA decisions are actually brought to the SC by the parties. Third, the SC places great persuasive value on CA decisions. There are no statistics, but based on my own experience and observation, about 60 percent of all petitions from CA judgments are denied through minute resolutions of the SC in the First Round. Of the balance, another 20 percent are thrown out during the Second Round. Thus, only about 20 percent of appealed CA decisions mature to penned decisions i n the Third Round. And of these, about one half are affirmed and the other half are reversed or modified. In other words, there is only a 10 percent chance that CA decisions may be reversed or modified by the SC.

At the earliest opportunity, the Office of the Clerk of Court, without knowing the identity of the ponente, schedules the case in the agenda of the Court. Although all the justices are given copies of the petition, it is usually the ponente who makes an indepth study of the case. During the First Round, he either (1) recommends the outright denial (or dismissal) of the petition due to some procedural or substantive flaw or (2) requires comment.

When recommending outright denial or dismissal, the ponente usually summarizes the facts and the issues, verbally or in writing, and points out why the case does not deserve any further consideration. In the Third Round, after the memoranda are received, the ponente prepares a written abstract of the case, citing the pertinent facts, issues and points of law and, finally, his recommended conclusions. Although there are disagreements in the Divisions once in a while, the real battles in the Court take place in the banc, where all the incumbent justices participate and where major controversies are resolved.
