# Transparency, Unanimity & Diversity -- Ch. 12: Republic v. Molina: Setting the Procedure for Nullifying a Marriage

The Family Code in Article 36’ provided an entirely new ground to void a marriage: “psychological incapacity.” Since the effectivity of the Code, our courts have been swamped with petitions to declare marriages void on this ground. Invariably, Article 36 was used, abused and misused as a convenient “divorce law,” not merely one that provided a new ground for declaring a marriage inexistent from the beginning.

In its Petition in Republic v. Molina? the Office of the Solicitor General (OSG) labeled Article 36 as the “most liberal divorce procedure in the world.” Hence, it asked the Court not only to decide the dispute between Spouses Reynaldo and Roridel Molina, but also to issue procedural guidelines for implementing this new way of voiding a marriage.

## Facts

Roridel Olaviano Molina filed in the Regional Trial Court (RTC) of La Trinidad (Benguet) a verified Petition praying for the declaration of nullity of her marriage. She stated therein that she had married Reynaldo Molina on April 14, 1985 at the San Agustin Church in Manila. As a result of the union, a son, Andre O. Molina, was born.

After a year of marriage, Reynaldo showed signs of “immaturity and irresponsibility” as a husband and father. He preferred to spend more time with his peers and friends on whom he squandered his money, depended on his parents for aid and assistance, and was never honest with his wife in regard to their finances. Consequently, frequent quarrels ensued between them.

Sometime in February 1986, Reynaldo was relieved of his job in Manila. Since then she became the breadwinner of the family. In October 1986, they had a very intense quarrel, as a result of which their relationship was estranged. Five months after, in March 1987, she resigned from her job in Manila and went to live with her parents in Baguio City. A few weeks later, Reynaldo left her and their child and since then abandoned them.

Roridel argued that her husband’s conduct showed he was psychologically incapable of complying with essential marital obligations. He was allegedly a highly immature and habitually quarrelsome individual, who thought of himself as a king to be served. She concluded that under Article 36 of the Family Code, it would be to the couple’s best interest to have their marriage declared null and void, in order to free them from what appeared to be an incompatible marriage from the start.

In his Answer, Reynaldo admitted that he and Roridel could no longer live together as husband and wife, but contended that their misunderstandings and frequent quarrels were due to (a) her strange behavior of insisting on maintaining her group of friends even after their marriage, (b) her refusal to perform some of her marital duties such as cooking meals, and (c) her failure to run the household and handle their finances.

The RTC rendered judgment declaring the marriage void. The Court of Appeals (CA) affirmed the RTC’s Decision. Disagreeing, the Republic of the Philippines through the OSG asked the Supreme Court to review the judgment of the lower courts.

## Issue

Should the marriage of a petitioner with her husband be voided on the ground of psychological incapacity?

## Ruling

No, the marriage should not be voided on such ground. The Court ruled that the meaning of psychological incapacity should be confined to the most serious cases of personality disorders indicating “utter insensitivity or inability to give meaning and significance to the marriage.” Such condition must be characterized by (a) gravity, (b) juridical antecedence, and (c) incurability. Furthermore, it must be shown to have already existed at the time the marriage was celebrated.

In the present case, there was no clear showing that the psychological defect cited was an incapacity. It appeared to be more of a “difficulty,” if not outright “negligence” in the performance of some marital obligations or “refusal” to perform them. A mere showing of “irreconcilable differences” and “conflicting personalities” did not in any way constitute psychological incapacity. It was not enough to prove that the parties failed to meet their responsibilities and duties as married persons; they must also be shown to have been incapable of doing so because of some psychological (not physical) illness.

The real significance of this Decision was not in the refusal to declare the Molina marriage void, but in the formulation of procedural rules for the evaluation of similar cases. Indeed, the function of the Supreme Court is to teach and to open new vistas. Hence, to guide the bench, the bar and the public in interpreting and applying “psychological incapacity” as a ground for declaring marriages void, the Court issued the following guidelines:

“(1) The burden of proof to show the nullity of the marriage belongs to the plaintiff. Any doubt should be resolved in favor of the existence and continuation of the marriage and against its dissolution and nullity. This is rooted in the fact that both our Constitution and our laws cherish the validity of marriage and unity of the family. Thus, our Constitution devotes an entire Article on the Family,’ recognizing it ‘as the foundation of the nation.’ It decrees marriage as legally ‘inviolable,’ thereby protecting it from » dissolution at the whim of the parties. Both the family and marriage are to be ‘protected’ by the state.

“The Family Code‘ echoes this constitutional edict on marriage and the family and emphasizes their permanence, inviolability and solidarity.

“(2) The root cause of the psychological incapacity must be: (a) medically or clinically identified, (b) alleged in the complaint, (c) sufficiently proven by experts and (d) clearly explained in the decision. Article 36 of the Family Code requires that the incapacity must be psychological — not physical, although its manifestations and/or symptoms may be physical. The evidence must convince the court that the parties, or one of them, was mentally or psychically ill to such an extent that the person could not have known the obligations he was assuming, or knowing them, could not have given valid assumption thereof. Although no example of such incapacity need be given here so as not to limit the application of the provision under the principle of ejusdem generis,’ nevertheless such root cause must be identified as a psychological illness and its incapacitating nature fully explained. Expert evidence may be given by qualified psychiatrists and clinical psychologists.

“(3) The incapacity must be proven to be existing at ‘the time of the celebration’ of the marriage. The evidence must show that the illness was existing when the parties exchanged their ‘I do’s.’ The manifestation of the illness need not be perceivable at such time, but the illness itself must have attached at such moment, or prior thereto.

“(4) Such incapacity must also be shown to be medically or clinically permanent or incurable. Such incurability may be absolute or even relative only in regard to the other spouse, not necessarily absolutely against everyone of the same sex. Furthermore, such incapacity must be relevant to the assumption of marriage obligations, not necessarily to those not related to marriage, like the exercise of a profession or employment in a job. Hence, a pediatrician may be effective in diagnosing illnesses of children and prescribing medicine to cure them but may not be psychologically capacitated to procreate, bear and raise his/her own children as an essential obligation of marriage.

(5) Such illness must be grave enough to bring about the disability of the party to assume the essential obligations of marriage. Thus, ‘mild characteriological peculiarities, mood changes, occasional emotional outbursts’ cannot be accepted as root causes. The illness must be shown as downright incapacity or inability, not a refusal, neglect or difficulty, much less ill will. In other words, there is a natal or supervening disabling factor in the person, an adverse integral element in the personality structure that effectively incapacitates the person from really accepting and thereby complying with the obligations essential to marriage.

“(6) The essential marital obligations must be those embraced by Articles 68 up to 71 of the Family Code as regards the husband and wife as well as Articles 220, 221 and 225 of the same Code in regard to parents and their children. Such non-complied marital obligation[s] must also be stated in the petition, proven by evidence and included in the text of the decision.

“(7) Interpretations given by the National Appellate Matrimonial Tribunal of the Catholic Church in the Philippines, while not controlling or decisive, should be given great respect by our courts. It is clear that Article 36 was taken by the Family Code Revision Committee from Canon 1095 of the New Code of Canon Law, which became effective in 1983 and which provides:

‘The following are incapable of contracting marriage: Those who are unable to assume the essential obligations of marriage due to causes of psychological nature.”

“Since the purpose of including such provision in our Family Code is to harmonize our civil laws with the religious faith of our people, it stands to reason that to achieve such harmonization, great persuasive weight should be given to decisions of such appellate tribunal. Ideally — subject to our law on evidence — what is decreed as canonically invalid should also be decreed civilly void.

“This is one instance where, in view of the evident source and purpose of the Family Code provision, contemporaneous religious interpretation is to be given persuasive effect. Here, the State and the Church - while remaining independent, separate and apart from each other — shall walk together in synodal cadence towards the same goal of protecting and cherishing marriage and the family as the inviolable base of the nation.

“(8) The trial court must order the prosecuting attorney or fiscal and the Solicitor General to appear as counsel for the state. No decision shall be handed down unless the Solicitor General issues a certification, which will be quoted in the decision, briefly stating -therein his reasons for his agreement or opposition, as the case may be, to the petition. The Solicitor General, along with the prosecuting attorney, shall submit to the court such certification within fifteen (15) days from the date the case is deemed submitted for resolution of the court. The Solicitor General shall discharge the equivalent function of the defensor vinculi contemplated under Canon 1095.”

My ponencia was unanimously carried by the full 15-member Court. In spite of this fact, the Decision is criticized by many lawyers for its alleged “strictness.” My standard reply is that Molina is strict only insofar as Article 36 is construed as a divorce law, but reasonable insofar as it was correctly deemed as a ground for declaring a marriage void, not “ended”; one that has never been, and therefore could not be, a“cut.”

I should add that three members of the Court, while concurring in the Decision, wrote Separate Opinions. Justice Padilla explained why he was concurring in this case, even if he had dissented in Santos v. Court of Appeals.’ On the other hand, Justice Romero clarified that “neither should the incapacity be the result of mental illness. For if it were due to insanity or defect in mental faculties short of insanity, there would be a resultant defect or vice of consent, thus rendering the marriage annullable under Article 45 of the Family Code.” She also explained the proceedings in the Civil Code Revision Committee which had drafted the Family Code and provided a “brief historical note on the Old Canon Law (1917).” Justice Vitug’s Concurring Opinion reiterated the tests for determining psychological incapacity, as laid down in Santos v. Court of Appeals which he had penned.

## Summary

Chapter 12 of 'Transparency, Unanimity & Diversity' recounted Republic v. Molina, the unanimous ponencia Artemio V. Panganiban wrote after Article 36 of the Family Code had swamped the courts with petitions and had been, in his words, used, abused and misused as a convenient divorce law -- the Office of the Solicitor General itself calling it the most liberal divorce procedure in the world and asking the Court for guidelines. Roridel Molina had won a declaration of nullity in the RTC of La Trinidad and in the Court of Appeals on the strength of her husband Reynaldo's immaturity, irresponsibility, squandering of money and eventual abandonment; the Republic sought review. The Court reversed, holding that psychological incapacity is confined to the most serious personality disorders indicating utter insensitivity or inability to give meaning and significance to the marriage, marked by gravity, juridical antecedence and incurability and existing when the marriage was celebrated. What Reynaldo showed was difficulty, negligence or refusal, and mere irreconcilable differences and conflicting personalities do not qualify. The real significance of the decision, CJP wrote, lay not in refusing to void the marriage but in the eight guidelines it issued, since the function of the Supreme Court is to teach and to open new vistas: the plaintiff bears the burden, with doubt resolved for the marriage the Constitution calls inviolable; the root cause must be medically identified, pleaded, proven by qualified psychiatrists or psychologists and explained in the decision; the incapacity must exist at the time of the celebration, be permanent or incurable, and be grave enough that mere characteriological peculiarities or emotional outbursts will not do; the obligations are those in Articles 68 to 71 and 220, 221 and 225; interpretations of the Church's National Appellate Matrimonial Tribunal, given Article 36's origin in Canon 1095, deserve great respect; and the Solicitor General must certify his position as the equivalent of a defensor vinculi. Answering critics of the ruling's strictness, he replied that Molina is strict only insofar as Article 36 is construed as a divorce law, and he recorded the concurring Separate Opinions of Justices Padilla, Romero and Vitug.
