# Chapter 6: Q & A During the “Chamber-to-Chamber” Dialogues

For many years now, many business leaders have criticized the judiciary for its alleged interference in the economic life of the country. They claim that judges tended to dampen business activity by deciding economic issues, over which they allegedly have no expertise.

To be sure, courts do not decide strictly economic issues. Whether the country should adopt the policy of deregulation or privatization or globalization is a question best left to the sound discretion of the political departments of government. Also, the issue of whether the peso should be devalued, or whether oil products should be taxed — and if so, by how much and in what manner — are not to be decided by the judiciary. Furthermore, whether the government should build another international airport or reclaim portions of Manila Bay — and if so, whether to undertake the project itself or to bid it out to private parties under specified legal terms and conditions — are questions that should be addressed to policymakers, not to judges.

But when the officials concerned violate the Constitution or the law or gravely abuse their discretion in the performance of their functions, the judiciary — upon proper petition of the proper parties — is duty-bound (0 step in and to uphold the Constitution and the law. In this sense, the judiciary settles and decides issues that are constitutional and legal, not economic or business. True, judicial decisions may affect the economy. But it is equally true that the courts merely discharge their duty to protect public interest by championing the rule of law.

To bridge the information gap between the judiciary and the business community, the Court conducted a novel project, the Chamber-to-Chamber Dialogues. In particular, the project sought to “(1) demystify the judiciary's often misunderstood role as the third branch of government; (2) inform the business sector of the ‘reform initiatives currently sweeping the judiciary; (3) apprise the judiciary of the business sector’s concerns as regards the administration of justice in the country; and (4) [conduct] a beneficial exchange of judgments, opinions, and beliefs on the inter-relationship between the business sector and the judiciary.”

The Dialogues were held in eleven key areas of the country; namely, the National Capital Region, Davao, Cebu, Laguna, Zamboanga, Baguio, Iloilo, Cagayan de Oro, Pampanga, Subic and Palawan. The project was assisted by a grant from The Asia Foundation.

In addition, considering the concentration of business activities in the NCR, special dialogues were conducted during the conferences of some groups, like the Employers Confederation of the Philippines (ECOP), the Legal Management Council of the Philippines (LMCP), and the Investor Services Unit of the Bangko Central ng Pilipinas.

The Dialogues were conducted in a question-andanswer format for simpler presentation and easier understanding. Many of the concerns raised were not strictly economic in orientation. They touched on the role of the judiciary in basic governance, as well as on the reform program of the Supreme Court (SC). The answers given by the SC panelists — including those by Jus: tices Leonardo A. Quisumbing, Antonio T. Carpio 2 Te Adolfo S. Azcuna; Deputy Court Administrator Jose P. Perez; Assistant Court Administrators Antonio H. Dujua and Ismael G. Khan Jr. — were couched in simple, non-legal language.

Many issues were repeated in various geographical locations. Hence, I will no longer indicate the city where the questions were raised. Suffice it to say that the following questions were asked and corresponding simple answers given.

## Vacancies

(Q) How many judicial positions are currently vacant?

(A) As of December 2003, out of 2,214 judicial positions, 710 (32.07 percent) were vacant.

(Q) Why are there so many vacancies in the lower courts?

(A) The basic problem is that there are few qualified applicants. The Constitution mandates the Judicial and Bar Council (JBC) to submit at least three nominations for every vacant position in the judiciary for the President’s consideration. If there are only two qualified applicants, their names cannot be considered because, again, the Constitution requires that there be at least three nominees.

(Q) Why are there only “few” applicants?

(A) The judiciary gets few applicants mainly because of low compensation and security risks in far-flung areas. Judges are paid less than new lawyers in the established law firms. MTC and RTC judges receive only about P25,000 to P30,000, while new entrants in big Makati law firms receive about P50,000 per month.

(Q) How can the problem of compensation be solved?

(A) A new law increased the compensation of judges a hundred percent. However, the increase will be given on a staggered basis — an increment of 25 percent every year for four years. On a long-term basis, the Supreme Court is asking Congress for exemption from the Salary Standardization Law in order to attract the best and the brightest to join the judiciary.

## Judicial Appointment

(Q) Does the SC have any power over the process of appointing judges?

(A) No, not directly. The Judicial and Bar Council (BC) is the constitutional body mandated to nominate persons for appointment as judges. Although the JBC is an attached agency of the SC and is chaired by the Chief Justice, normally the SC cannot override the nominations made by the JBC.

(Q) In order to insulate the appointment process from political pressure, can the SC — instead of the President — appoint judges?

No. The appointment process in the judiciary is constitutionally mandated: the JBC nominate and the President appoints.

(Q) Can any lawyer apply for nomination by the JBC?

(A) Before being considered for nomination, aspiring judges go through a mandatory pre-judicature program conducted by the Philippine Judiciary Academy (PhilJA). They have to pass the program. Otherwise, they will not even be considered for nomination. This serves as the pre-cleansing process in the nomination procedure.

## Delay in the Resolution of Cases

(Q) How much time is given to a judge to resolve a case?

The Constitution mandates that cases be resolved within three months by the lower courts, 12 months by the Court of Appeals, and 24 months by the Supreme Court — counted from the filing of the last pleading, brief or memorandum required by the Rules of Court or by the court itself."

(Q) Can you suggest some ways to decongest court dockets and thereby avoid delays?

(A) In order to avoid delays, parties are encouraged to submit their cases to Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR) mechanisms. During pretrial, they are asked to settle their cases amicably, whether partial or totally.

It must be noted that the President signed on April 2, 2004 a new law creating the Office of Alternative Dispute Resolution. With the law institutionalizing the different modes of alternative dispute resolution, the dockets of the courts could be unclogged.

Moreover, there is a proposal to create a two-judge system. In this system, a case would first be assigned to a mediator-judge who, after a thorough examination of the case, would explain the probable scenarios to the parties involved and then attempt to make them settle. If mediation fails, the case would then be assigned to a new judge who would try the case. This experimental system will be piloted in San Fernando (Pampanga) and Bacolod.

(Q) What are the different modes of ADR available to a party-litigant?

(A) The different modes of ADR are mediation, arbitration and conciliation. Mediation is a process in which a neutral third party facilitates communication between or among disputing parties to assist them in arriving at a mutually acceptable settlement. Arbitration is an adjudicatory process in which the parties submit themselves to a neutral third party, who hears/examines their positions and renders an award to either party. Conciliation is similar to mediation, but devoid of the subtleties that make the latter an art form. ‘The most widely used mode is mediation.

(Q) Why is mediation encouraged in the judiciary?

(A) Chief Justice Davide pushed for the development and the use of mediation in order to help unclog court dockets. The project was spearheaded in 2001 by the PhilJA, whose board of trustees he chairs. After training 400 potential mediators, a 10-day pilot settlement period resulted in an 81 percent success rate. For that period alone, the government saved approximately P54 million pesOS. The success of the project led to the designation of mediators in selected courts in Manila, Cebu and Davao.

(Q) About 65 percent of the cases filed in the Metropolitan Trial Courts refer to “bouncing checks.” Can mediation be applied to these litigations?

(A) Yes. Following the lead of the SC, the Department of Justice (DOJ) conducted its own mediation experiment. It tested the use of mediation at the stage prior to preliminary investigation. After training 60 prosecutors and private individuals in mediation, a settlement rate of 93 percent was achieved.

(Q) How can the SC efficiently monitor the prompt resolution of cases?

(A) The Court has recently piloted in Pasay City a computer-assisted system of Case-Flow Management (CFM). This system allows the instant monitoring or tracking of cases filed and pending before trial courts in that city. For instance, as soon as a case is filed, information regarding the case is en-coded in an on-line computer. All succeeding developments are also encoded. ‘Thereafter, the CFM system makes a report on these developments, and this report is electronically transmitted to the Office of the Court Administrator (OCA). The OCA could then easily monitor the cases for any undue delays.

(Q) What are the other innovations being employed by the judiciary to speed up the delivery of justice?

(A) As part of the computerization program of the entire judiciary, a number of computer-assisted transcription machines have been distributed to the lower courts. These will allow stenographers to prepare transcripts in real time and will thereby help reduce delays in adjudication.

Another innovation is the Computer-Aided Legal Research (CALR). It involves setting up an electronic library that will systematize legal research. This is now in an advanced stage of development.

## Infrastructure Improvement

(Q) What efforts have been made to improve the present state of courthouses?

(A) The SC Committee on Halls of Justice oversees the construction and maintenance of courtrooms throughout the country. But while designs and plans have already been made, lack of funding remains a problem. At present, the judiciary is dependent on local governments for housing and furnishing courts.

However, the judiciary will soon launch a foreign-assisted project that will create model courthouses in Angeles City, Lapulapu City and Cagayan de Oro City. These courthouses are envisioned to be modern, with all the information technology (IT) components in place and all the necessary facilities installed.

(Q) How can the judiciary address the problem of certain areas that have no courthouses?

(A) Following the success of Guatemala, the judiciary plans to introduce the use of “mobile courts.” For this purpose, the judiciary will convert a bus into a mobile courthouse that will be used by the designated judge to go around areas where there are no accessible regular courthouses.

## Monitoring the Behavior of Legal Practitioners

(Q) How does the SC monitor the behavior of legal practitioners?

(A) Normally, the SC does not act on any misbehavior unless a complaint is filed. However, when its attention is called to some direct misdeed, it in1-tiates an investigation through the Office of the Bar Confidant (OBC).

(Q) How does the SC act on a complaint against a lawyer?

(A) When it receives a complaint against a lawyer, it normally requires a comment from the respondent. It then refers the matter to the Integrated Bar of the Philippines (IBP) for investigation and recommendation. Ninety percent of the time, the SC accepts the recommendation of the IBP. This is called the IBP’s “self- disciplining process.”

(Q) How many lawyers have been disciplined recently?

(A) In 2003, the SC disciplined about 58 lawyers.

## Evaluation of Judges

(Q) Is there an established evaluation system to assess the performance of judges regularly?

(A) Yes, there is. Judges are required to submit monthly reports containing information on the number of cases filed in their courts, the nature of these cases, the number of cases disposed, and other related information. Soon, this process will be automated through the Court Administration Management Information System (CAMIS) now being developed.

(Q) How are complaints against judges acted upon?

(A) A verified complaint must first be filed before the SC can act on charges against erring judges. However, if an anonymous complaint comes with verifiable documents, the OCA will conduct a discreet investigation. If the charges are found true, the OCA itself will file a complaint against that particular judge.

## Nullification of Business Contracts

(Q) Of the many cases filed for the nullification of contracts, how many are actually voided by the SC?

Only about 2 percent of the contracts are declared void by the SC.

(Q) Mega-contracts involving foreign investments were recently declared void by the SC. On what grounds were the contracts nullified?

(A) The cases involved essentially the principle of “leveling the playing field.” The contracts were voided because they violated the basic elements of fair play. For instance, in the automation case involving the Commission on Elections (Comelec), the contract was awarded in violation of the bidding law and the bidding rules of Comelec itself. In the Piatco case, the Contract awarded was not the one implemented. In the Amari case, the SC reiterated that private entities could not own fore shore land. ‘The fact that investors lose money as a consequence of the nullification of contracts 18 a business risk.

(Q) How can entrepreneurs avoid this business risk?

(A) First, consult a good lawyer or a reputable law firm before entering into a contract, and not after getting into trouble. Second, avoid shortcuts. Short cuts are dangerous because the law will be upheld by the courts regardless of the consequences. Third, do not bribe judges. Do not believe a lawyer who says that a judge must be bribed in order to get a positive decision. The SC has dismissed some corrupt judges, but they are very few compared to the many ethical judges in our country.

## Supervision over Judges in the Autonomous Region of Muslim Mindanao (ARMM)

(Q) Does the Supreme Court have supervision over judges in the ARMM?

(A) Yes. The SC has administrative supervision over all judges in the Philippines.

## Labor Cases

(Q) Do trial courts have original jurisdiction over labor cases?

(A) No. It is the National Labor Relations Commission (NLRC) under the Department of Labor and Employment (DOLE) that has original jurisdiction over labor disputes. However, cases decided by NLRC can be raised on certiorari to the Court of Appeals and then appealed to the SC.

## Department of Agrarian Reform Adjudication Board (DARAB) Cases

(Q) Can a DARAB decision be appealed?

(A) Yes, but within the period for appeal.

(Q) Can a DARAB decision be elevated to the SC? What is the flow of the appeal?

(A) Cases decided by DARAB may be appealed to the Office of the President, then to the Court of Appeals and finally to the SC.

## Overcrowded Jails

(Q) How can the SC address the problem of overcrowded jails?

(A) The judiciary has no jurisdiction over the management of jails. City jails are under the jurisdiction of the local city government, while municipal jails are under the Bureau of Jail Management and Penology. The National Bilibid Prison and the Correctional Institute for Women are under the Executive Department (Bureau of Corrections).

At present, the Integrated Bar of the Philippines (IBP) is spearheading an ongoing project entitled “Balik Laya. A Jail Decongestion Project in the Manila City and Pasay City Jails,” under the Legal Accountability and Dispute Resolution (LADR) Program. The project provides overstaying prisoners adequate legal representation and facilitates their release. A similar project is also ongoing in Quezon City and Pasig City.

## Tribal Justice System

(Q) How can the judiciary give adequate service to areas where there exists a tribal justice system?

(A) The Action Program for Judicial Reform (APJR) recognizes the special needs of tribal communities. It is for this reason that efforts have been made to train judges on the Indigenous People’ Rights Act (IPRA) through a project entitled “Reforming the Administration of Justice through Coalition Advocacy (RAJCA): Judicial Seminar on Indigenous People’s Rights.” The seminar was conducted by the PhilJA with the assistance of USAID.

## Creation of Special Courts for Business Cases

(Q) Is it possible to create special courts for business litigations or for estafa and “bouncing checks” cases?

(A) The matter needs careful review. However, as early as November 21, 2000, the SC already issued a Resolution designating certain branches of regional trial courts to try and decide cases formerly cognizable by the Securities and Exchange Commission. Cases involving bouncing checks constitute about 60 percent of cases in certain metropolitan and municipal trial courts. The Supreme Court calls on the business community to help police these cases. For instance, banks should not accept accounts from habitual bouncing check writers.

## Epilogue

The “Chamber-to-Chamber” project may seem to be a small detail in the extensive reform program of the judiciary. However, its importance is reflected in the challenges that the business sector has impressed upon the judiciary. While the APJR has already covered an extensive area of reform, the Chamber-to-Chamber Dialogues have proved that there is so much more that needs to be done in order to attain the quest for equal justice. Some may not agree with certain decisions made on specific cases, but the Supreme Court hopes to achieve, in the long term and with the full implementation of the APJR, a justice system that is fair and efficient as well as predictable and stable.
