# Leveling the Playing Field -- Ch. 15: Francisco v. House of Representatives: Constitutionality of the Davide Impeachment

I believe that the most controversial case decided by the Supreme Court during my ninth year in office was Francisco v. House of Representatives.¹ Here the Court effectively stopped the House of Representatives from acting on the second impeachment Complaint against Chief Justice Hilario G. Davide Jr. Fortunately for constitutionalism and the rule of law, the House — during its session on November 10, 2003, the very day the Decision was promulgated² — voted 115 to 77 to accept the ruling of Speaker Jose C. de Venecia “to respect the Decision of the Supreme Court.”

This case attracted daily nationwide attention and full media focus for several months. Had Congress ignored the Supreme Court Decision and proceeded to impeach the Chief Justice, a constitutional crisis of googolplex proportions would have ensued.

The very foundation of the government, and the premacy of the Constitution as interpreted by the Su-sme Court, would have been shaken beyond imagination. The Senate, especially Senate President Franklin Drilon, displayed a becoming regard for constitutionalism when it opted not to interfere. And of course, much of the credit for the triumph of the rule of law should go to President Gloria Macapagal Arroyo, who steadily supported the authority of the Court as the final arbiter of all constitutional disputes. Here are the details of the case.

## The Facts

On July 22, 2002, the House of Representatives adopted a Resolution sponsored by Representative Felix Wiliam D. Fuentebella, directing the Committee on Jus-‘ “to conduct an investigation, in aid of legislation, on manner of disbursements and expenditures by the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court of the Judiciary Development Fund (JDF).”

Almost a year after, on June 2, 2003, former Present Joseph E. Estrada filed the first impeachment Coming against Chief Justice Hilario G. Davide Jr. and seven Associate justices of the Court for “culpable violation of the Constitution, betrayal of the public trust and other high crimes.” ‘The Complaint — endorsed by Representatives Rolex T. Suplico, Ronaldo B. Zamora and Didag Piang Dilangalen — was referred to the House Committee on Justice on August 5, 2003. The Committee ruled on October 13, 2003 that the Complaint was “sufficient in form,” but voted on October 22, 2003, to dismiss it for being “insufficient in substance.”

On October 23, 2003, a day after the House Committee on Justice voted to dismiss the first impeachment Complaint, a second one was filed with the secretary general of the House by Representatives Gilberto C. Teodoro Jr. and Fuentebella against the Chief Justice on founded on the alleged results of the legislative inquiry initiated by the House Resolution sponsored the prev ous year by the latter congressman. This second impeachment charge was accompanied by a “Resolution of Endorsement/Impeachment” signed by at least one third of all the members of the House of Representatives.

Subsequently, eighteen separate Petitions were filed before the Court, similarly contending that the second impeachment Complaint was unconstitutional because it violated Section 5 of Article XI of the Constitution, according to which “[n]o impeachment proceedings shall be initiated against the same official more than of within a period of one year.”

Respondent House of Representatives, through Speaker Jose C. de Venecia Jr. by way of special appearance, submitted a Manifestation asserting that the larger House of Congress was an independent and a coequal branch of government under the Constitution; and that, hence, the Court had no jurisdiction to hear the case, much less prohibit or enjoin the House from the performance of its constitutionally mandated duty to initiate impeachment cases.

For its part, the Senate of the Philippines through Senate President Franklin M. Drilon filed a Manifestation stating that insofar as it was concerned, the Petitions were plainly premature and had no basis in law or in fact. The Senate added that as of the time of the filing of the Petitions, no justiciable issue had been presented before it, since (1) its constitutional duty to constitute itself as an impeachment court would commence only when it received the Articles of Impeachment, which it had not; and (2) the principal issues raised by the Petitions pertained exclusively to the proceedings in the House of Representatives.

Senator Aquilino Q. Pimentel Jr. filed, on his own behalf, a Motion to Intervene (Ex Abudante Cautela) and (.Comment, praying that the consolidated Petitions be dismissed for lack of jurisdiction over the issues affecting the impeachment proceedings. He contended that the sole power, authority and jurisdiction of the Senate to try and decide impeachment cases — including those in which the Chief Justice was the respondent — be recognized and upheld pursuant to the provisions of Article XI of the Constitution.

On November 5-6, 2003, this Court conducted an Oral Argument, during which it heard the views 6 eight amici curiae whom it had invited.

During the Oral Argument, the following questions were discussed: whether the certiorari jurisdiction of the Supreme Court could be invoked, who could invoke it, on what issues and at what time, and whether it should be exercised by the Court at the time. Specif cally, the following issues were touched upon:

Locus standi of petitioners

Ripeness (prematurity or mootness)

Political question/justiciability

The House’s “exclusive” power to initiate cases of impeachment

The Senate’s “sole” power to try and decide cases of impeachment

The constitutionality of the House Rules on Impeachment vis-a-vis Section 3(5) of Article XI of the Constitution

Judicial restraint

## The Court’s Ruling

## Judicial Review

Speaking through Justice Conchita Carpio-Morales, the Court initially delved into “the threshold and novel issue of whether or not the power of judicial review extends to those arising from impeachment proceedings.”

Citing Justice Laurel, the Decision reiterated that inherent in all courts was the “moderating power” to “determine the proper allocation of powers” of the different branches of government and “to direct the course of government along constitutional channels.” Such “moderating power” was a necessary consequence of judicial power itself, which was “the power of the court to settle actual controversies involving rights which are legally demandable and enforceable.”

As ruled in Angara v. Electoral Commission, judicial review is an integral component of the system of checks and balances which, together with the corollary principle of separation of powers, forms the bedrock of our te-publican form of government.

To ensure the potency of judicial review in curbing grave abuse of discretion by “any branch or instrumentalities of government,” Section 1 of Article VIII of the Constitution — for the first time in its history engraves in block-letter law the so-called “expanded certiorari jurisdiction” of the Court.

## Tools of Constitutional Construction

In determining the merits of the issues raised in the Petitions, the Court turned to well-settled principles of constitutional construction.

First, verba legis. Wherever possible, the words used in the Constitution must be given their ordinary meaning except where technical terms are employed. Second, where there is ambiguity, ratio lexis est anima. The words of the Constitution must be interpreted in accordance with the intent of its framers. Finally, ut magis valeat quam pereat. The Constitution is to be interpreted as a whole, not by independent parts.

## Political Question

Respondents Speaker de Venecia et al. argued that impeachment was a political action that could not assume a judicial character. Hence, any question, issue or incident arising at any stage of the impeachment proceedings was beyond the reach of judicial review.

There is, however, a plethora of cases in which the Court has exercised the power of judicial review over congressional actions. In Santiago v. Guingona Jr, ruled that it was well within its power and jurisdiction to inquire whether the Senate or its officials committed a violation of the Constitution or gravely abused their discretion in the exercise of their functions and prerogatives. seeking to nullify an act of the Senate on the ground that it was unconstitutional, Tañada v. Angara held that when an action of the legislative branch was seriously alleged to have infringed the Constitution, it became not only the right but in fact the duty of the judiciary to settle the dispute.

Bondoc v. Pinedal declared null and void a Resolution of the House of Representatives withdrawing the nomination and rescinding the election of a congressman as a member of the House of Representatives Electoral Tribunal for being violative of Section 17 of Article VI of the Constitution. Coseteng v. Mitra Jr. ruled as subject to judicial review the resolution of whether the House representation in the Commission on Appointments should be based on proportional representations: of the political parties, as provided in Section 18 of Article VI of the Constitution. Daza v. Singson held that the act of the House of Representatives in removing the petitioner from the Commission on Appointment was subject to judicial review.

Indeed, as affirmed in Tañada v. Cuenco, while legislative power is vested by the Constitution exclusively Congress, this fact does not detract from the power of the courts to pass upon the constitutionality of acts Congress.

As clearly stated in Angara v. Electoral Commission, the courts’ power of judicial review over constitutional issues is subject to these limitations: (1) there must an actual case or controversy; (2) the person challenging the act must have “standing” — a personal and stantial interest in the case — such that he or she has sustained, or will sustain, direct injury as a result of enforcement; (3) the question of constitutionality must be raised at the earliest possible opportunity; and (4) the issue of constitutionality must be the very lis mota of the case.

## On Standing

The Court opted to grant standing to most of the petitioners, given their allegation that any impending transmittal of the Articles of Impeachment to the Senate and the ensuing trial of the Chief Justice would necessarily involve the expenditure of public funds. In addition, legislators are allowed to sue for the purpose of questioning the validity of any official action that allegedly infringes the prerogatives, powers and privileges vested by the Constitution in their office.

The Petitions also advanced constitutional issues, whose seriousness, novelty and weight as precedents deserved the attention of the Court. Thus, opting to resolve the issues presented, it relaxed the rules on standing.

## On Ripeness

For a case to be considered ripe for adjudication, “it is a prerequisite that something has by then been accomplished or performed by either branch before court may come into the picture.”' Only then may the courts pass on the validity of what was done, if and when the matter is challenged in an appropriate legal proceeding.

The instant Petitions raised the issue of the validity of the filing of the second impeachment Complaint against the Chief Justice in accordance with the House Impeachment Rules (adopted by the 12th Congress), the constitutionality of which was also questioned here. The assailed acts had already been carried out; that is, the second impeachment Complaint had been filed with the House of Representatives, and the 2001 Rules promulgated and enforced. Hence, there was compliance with the prerequisite that the alleged unconstitutional act should have been accomplished or performed before the suit.

## On Justiciability

With respect to the justiciability of the issues, the Court reiterated that judicial review was not only a power! but also a duty that could not be abdicated by the met specter of the so-called political question doctrine. It hastened to clarify, however, that Section 1 of Article VI had not done away with “truly political questions.”

The distinction between a real political question and a non-justiciable political question lies in whether there are constitutionally imposed limits or conditions on the powers or functions conferred upon political bodies. If so, then courts are duty-bound to examine whether any branch or instrumentality of government properly acted within such limits or conditions.

The Court held that the following inextricably linked issues constituted the very lis mota of the instant controversy: (1) whether Sections 16 and 17 of Rule V of the House Impeachment Rules adopted by the 12th Congress violated the provisions of Section 32 of Article XI of the Constitution; and (2) whether, as a result, the second impeachment Complaint was barred under Section 3(5) of Article XI of the Constitution.

## On Judicial Restraint

The Court emphasized that the exercise of judicial restraint over justiciable issues was not an option Adjudication could not be declined, because the Court was not legally disqualified. Neither could jurisdiction be renounced, as there was no other tribunal to which the controversy could be referred.” Otherwise, the Court would be shirking from the duty vested in it under Section 1(2) of Article VIII of the Constitution. More than being clothed with authority, the Court was duty-bound (0 take cognizance of the Petitions.

Even in cases in which it is an interested party, the Court cannot inhibit itself but must rule upon the challenge, because no other office has the authority to do so. On this occasion when it was an interested party (to the controversy before it, it acted upon the matter, “not with officiousness but in the discharge of an un-avoidable duty and, as always, with detachment and fairness.”

## Constitutionality of the House Rules on Impeachment

During the Oral Argument, Amicus Curiae Florenz Regalado — who had sponsored Section 3(5) of the Constitution while he was a constitutional commissioner — affirmed that the meaning of initiate was “to file, and that the act of initiating included taking initial action on the complaint. Bolstered by his affirmation, the Court held that indeed the word initiate, as it twice appeared in Sections (3) and (5) of Article XI of the Constitution, meant “to file a complaint and take initial action on it.”

Based on the records of the Constitutional mission, as reiterated in the amicus curiae Briefs of two former constitutional commissioners, the Court also concluded that the framers had intended initiation to start with the filing of the complaint.

The Court, thus, ruled that Sections 16 and 17 of Rule V of the House Impeachment Rules — according to which impeachment proceedings were “deemed initiated" in either of three ways stated therein — had clearly contravened Section 3(5) of Article XI of the Constitution by giving the term initiate a meaning different from “filing.”

The Court reiterated that no House rule must contravene the Constitution. Arroyo v. De Venecia held that while it was empowered by the Constitution to det mine its rules of proceedings, the House may not by rules ignore constitutional restraints or violate fundamental rights; further, there should be a reasonable relation between the mode or method of proceeding established by the rule and the result that was sought to be attained.

## Validity of the Second Impeachment Complaint

From the foregoing interpretation, once an impeachment complaint has been initiated by its filing and referral to the House Committee on Justice, another one may not be filed against the same official within a one-year period in accordance with Section 3(5) of Article XI of the Constitution.

The first impeachment Complaint was filed by former President Estrada against Chief Justice Hilario G. Davide Jr. (along with seven associate justices of this Court) on June 2, 2003, and was referred to the House Committee on Justice on August 5, 2003. Considering this fact, the second impeachment Complaint against Chief Justice Davide — filed by Representatives Teodoro and Fuentebella with the Office of the Secretary General of the House of Representatives on October 23, 2003 — violated the constitutional provision barring the initiation of impeachment proceedings against the same impeachable officer within a one-year period.

Hence, the Court ruled as unconstitutional Sections 16 and 17 of Rule V of the Rules of Procedure in Impeachment Proceedings — rules that had been approved by the House of Representatives on November 28, 2001. Consequently, the second impeachment Complaint was deemed barred under paragraph 5, Section 3 of Article XI of the Constitution.

## The Separate Opinions

Several justices submitted Separate Opinions. shall summarize them in the order of the seniority of their authors.

## The Bellosillo Opinion

Justice Josue N. Bellosillo anchored his thesis on “the dictates of judicial restraint and reasoned hesitance, He found no urgency for judicial intervention. Although conscious of the transcendental implications and importance of the issues that confronted the Court, he nonetheless opined that this was not the proper hour or the appropriate circumstance for it to perform its duty.

‘True, he said, the Court was vested with the power to annul the acts of the legislature when tainted with grave abuse of discretion. Even so, this power sho not be lightly assumed or readily exercised. The doctrine of separation of powers imposed upon the courts proper restraint in striking down the acts of the legislature unconstitutional. Such restraint was born of the nature of their functions and of their respect for the other departments. Verily, the policy should be a harmonious blend of courtesy and caution.

All avenues of redress in the instant cases must perforce be conscientiously explored and exhausted, he opined, not within the hallowed domain of the Court but within the august confines of the legislature. He cited the following reasons.

First. The matter of impeachment was a political question that must rightfully be addressed to a political branch of government, which was the Congress of the Philippines.

Second. At all times, the three departments of government —being coequal, coordinate and coextensive - must accord mutual respect to one another under the principle of separation of powers. Hence, as a measure of judicial comity on issues properly within the sphere of the legislature, the judiciary must defer to the wisdom of the Congress in the exercise of the latter's power under the impeachment clause of the Constitution.

Third. It was incumbent upon the Court to exercise judicial restraint in rendering a ruling in this particular case, in order to preserve the principle of separation of powers and to restore faith and stability in our system of government.

Despite his advice on judicial restraint, Justice Bellosillo found the second impeachment Complaint filed against the Chief Justice to be constitutionally infirm.

## The Puno Opinion

Justice Reynato S. Puno concurred with the majority that the issue posed was “justiciable”; nonetheless he opined that the Court should defer exercising its jurisdiction until after the House and the Senate would have acted on the impeachment Complaint.

In Tañada v. Cuenco, the Court held that the term political question connoted what it meant in ordinary parlance; namely, a question of policy. Indeed, the te refers to “those questions which, under the Constitution, are to be decided by the people in their sovereign capacity; or in regard to which full discretionary authority has been delegated to the legislative or executive branch of government. It is concerned with issues dependent upon the wisdom, not legality, of a particular measure.” In Sanidad v. Comelec, the Court further hi that “political questions are not the legality of a particular act. Where the vortex of the controversy refers to the legality or validity of the contested act, the matter! definitely justiciable or non-political.”

Justice Puno pointed out that the power of impeachment was textually committed to Congress, a political branch of government. The right to accuse was granted exclusively to the House of Representatives; and the right to try and decide, to the Senate. Only when the President was under impeachment would the Chief Justice have a part, a limited one, in the process — (0 preside, but without the right to vote.

Justice Puno saw a comixture of political and judicial components in the re-engineered concept of impeachment. He classified an impeachment proceeding as sui generis — indigenous, a kind of its own.

The Petitions at bar concerned a nonpolitical issue: whether the impeachment Complaint against Chief Justice Davide involving the JDF was already barred by the one-year rule under Section 3(5) of Article XI of the Constitution, By any standard, this was a justiciable issue.

Between judicial activism and judicial restraint, Justice Puno’s preference was the contextual approach of the “coordinacy theory.” This theory took into consideration the constitutional allocation of the decision-making authority; the constitutional judgments of the relative risks of action and inaction by each branch of government; and the fears and aspirations embodied in the different constitutional provisions. The contextual approach paid better attention to the specific character of particular constitutional provisions and accordingly calibrated deference or restraint on a case-to-case basis. In so doing, it allowed the legislature adequate leeway to carry out its constitutional duties, and at the same time ensured that no form of abuse would undermine important constitutional principles.”

The correct calibration of these constitutional values compelled Justice Puno to conclude that the Court should defer the exercise of its ultimate jurisdiction over the Petitions at bar, out of prudence and respect for the initial exercise by the legislature of its jurisdiction over impeachment proceedings.

He opined that the Court had jurisdiction, but the wrong timing of the exercise thereof could negate — with catastrophic consequence — the very existence of this jurisdiction.

In summary, he voted in favor of the following:

1. Grant the locus standi of the petition considering the transcendental constitutional issues presented.

2. Hold that it was within the power of this Court to define the division of powers of the branches of government.

3. Hold that the alleged violation of Section 3(5) of Article XI of the Constitution, providing that “no impeachment proceedings shall be initiated against the same official more than once within a period of one year,” was a justiciable issue and hence within the competence of this Court to decide.

4. Hold that the coordinacy theory of constitutional interpretation — as well as prudential considerations — demanded that this Court defer the exercise of its certiorari jurisdiction over the issue of the alleged violation of the Constitution, until after the remedies against impeachment still available to both the House of Representatives and the Senate would have been exhausted.

In this light, he voted to dismiss the Petitions.

## The Vitug Concurrence

Justice Jose C. Vitug said that as a proceeding, impeachment might be described, first, as legal and political in nature; and, second, as sui generis — neither as a criminal nor as an administrative proceeding, but as partaking of hybrid characteristic of both, while retaining the requirement of due process basic to all proceedings.

The political nature of impeachment, he stressed, was apparent from its function as a constitutional measure designed to protect the State from official delinquency and malfeasance, the punishment of the offender being merely incidental. Although impeachment was intended to be nonpartisan, the power to impeach was nevertheless lodged in the House of Representatives whose members were highly responsive to political am

Where the House — through its conduct or its rules — transgressed in any way the detailed procedure prescribed in the Constitution, it was Justice Vitug’s opinion that the issue became far removed from the sphere of a “political question” and was then transformed into a constitutional issue falling squarely within the jurisdictional ambit of the Supreme Court as the interpreter of the fundamental law.

When constitutional limits or proscriptions were expressed, Justice Vitug pointed out, discretion was effectively withheld. Thus, issues pertaining to who the impeachable officers were, the number of votes necessary to impeach, and the prohibition against the initiation of impeachment proceedings twice against the same official in a single year — provided for in Sections 2, 3, 4 and 5 of Article XI of the Constitution — verily were subject to judicial inquiry. Any violation or disregard of those explicit constitutional mandates could be struck down by the Court in the exercise of judicial power.

Justice Vitug seconded the view that the term initiate should be construed as the physical act of filing the complaint, coupled with an action by the House taking cognizance of it; that is, referring the complaint to the proper Committee. Evidently, the House of Representatives took cognizance of the first Complaint and acted on it.

Justice Vitug noted that despite having conceded the locus standi of petitioners and the jurisdiction of the Court, some would call for judicial restraint. He entertained no doubt that the advice was well-meant and understandable. But the social unrest and division that the controversy had generated and the possibility of a worsening political and constitutional crisis did not appear to sustain that idea.

Indeed, in his view, the circumstances could well be compelling reasons for the Court to put a lid on an impending, simmering ferment before it erupted. The (Court had to do its task right, then, if it was to maintain its credibility, dependability, and independence. It was probably weak, but it did not have to be a weakling. The keeper of the fundamental law could not afford to be a bystander — passively watching from the sidelines — lest events overtake it, make it impotent, and seriously endanger the Constitution and what it stood for.

## My Concurring Opinion

When these consolidated Petitions were first taken up by the Court, I immediately inhibited myself, because among the petitioners was one of my former law part nets. Also, one of the eight amici curiae was former Senate President Jovito R. Salonga, with whom I had a deeply rooted personal and professional relationship.

There was also the lingering thought that the judg: ment I would make in those consolidated cases might present a conflict of interest because of the following considerations:

1. My judgment on the cases might personally benefit me, considering that I was one of the eight justices who had been charged by former President Jose! Ejercito Estrada in the first impeachment Complaint thus, a ruling barring the initiation of the second plaint within one year from the first would also present any future indictment against me within the same period.;

2. As a member of the Court, I had used some facilities purchased or constructed with the Judies Development Fund (JDF).

3. I had voted in favor of several unanimous en banc Resolutions of the Court affirming JDF expenditures recommended by some of its committees.

However, the Court, in its Resolution dated October 28, 2003, “directed [me] to participate,” because the Petitions presented novel and transcendental constitutional questions that necessitated the participation of all the justices.

During the Oral Argument on November 5, 2003, “Amicus Joaquin G. Bernas shed some light on my question regarding my conflict-of-interest problem. He explained that in Perfecto v. Meer, the Court had ruled that justices may participate and vote in a judgment that benefited its members because, inter alia, “jurisdiction may not be declined”; and the issue “involved the right of other constitutional officers x x x equally protected by the Constitution.”

Also cited was Nitafan v. Commissioner of Internal Revenue, in which the Court — upholding the intent behind Section 10 of Article VIII of the Constitution - had in fact ruled in a manner adverse to the interest of its members. This fact showed that in taking action over matters affecting them, justices were capable of ruling against their own interests when impelled by law and jurisprudence.

Furthermore, in Abbas v. Senate Electoral Tribunal (SET), the Court held that “the proposed mass disqualification (of the senators who were members of the SET), if sanctioned and ordered, would leave the ‘Tribunal no alternative but to abandon a duty that no other court or body can perform, but which it cannot lawfully discharge ‘if shorn of the participation of its entire membership of senators.” The Court further explained:

“To our mind, this is the overriding consideration — that the Tribunal be not prevented from discharging a duty which it alone has the power to perform, the performance of which is in the highest public interest as evidenced by its being expressly imposed by no less than the fundamental law.”

Estrada v. Desierto also held recently that “to disqualify any of the members of the Court, particularly the majority of them, is nothing short of pro tanto depriving the Court itself of its jurisdiction as established by the fundamental law. x x x. It affects the very heart of judicial independence.”

In the instant cases, the judgment would affect not just Supreme Court justices, but also other high officials like the President, the Vice-President and the members of the various constitutional commissions. Besides, if the six other justices — who, like me, were named respondents in the first impeachment Complaint — were also to inhibit themselves, the Court would be left without a majority (only seven would remain) and thus be deprived of its jurisdiction. In Perfecto, the Court opined that “judges would indeed be hapless guardians of the Constitution if they did not perceive and block encroachment upon their prerogatives in whatever form.”

## The Court’s Assumption of Jurisdiction Mandated by the 1987 Constitution

Unlike the 1973 and the 1935 Constitutions, the 1987 Constitution imposes upon the Supreme Court the duty to strike down the acts of “any branch or instrumentality of the government” whenever these are perweed “with grave abuse of discretion amounting to lack or excess of jurisdiction.”

Unlike the US Constitution, Section 1 of Article VIII of our Constitution is very specific as to what our courts must do: not only to settle actual controversies involving legally demandable and enforceable rights, but also to determine whether there has been grave abuse of discretion amounting to lack or excess of jurisdiction on the part of any branch or instrumentality of the government.”

This provision was crafted precisely to remedy judicial cop-outs that characterized the Martial Law era during which the Court had invariably found its hands tied (or had conveniently avoided involvement) when faced with questions that were allegedly political in nature. As a result, the Court at the time was unable to check all the constitutional excesses of the executive and the legislative branches of government.

Former Chief Justice Roberto Concepcion, who had authored Section 1 of Article VIII of the Constitution, explained that the Supreme Court could not under any circumstance evade its duty to settle disputes involving grave abuse of discretion.”

In effect, even if the question posed before the Court appeals to be political in nature, the Court may still resolve the question if it entails a determination of grave abuse of discretion or unconstitutionality. The question becomes justiciable when the Constitution provides conditions, limitations or restrictions on the exercise of a power vested in a specific branch or instrumentality. When the Court resolves the question, it does not judge the wisdom of an act of a coequal department, but merely ensures that the Constitution is upheld.

In contrast, the US Constitution does not impose upon its judiciary a similar duty to strike down grave abuse of discretion on the part of any government agency. The magistrates are thus given the luxury of choosing between being passivists or activists when confronted with “political questions.”

Thus, the US Supreme Court under Chief Justice Earl Warren was characterized as an activist for having actively intervened in political, social and economic concerns of the state. It issued decisions favoring the poor and the underprivileged; and overhauled jurisprudence on the Bill of Rights to protect ethnic minorities, eliminate racial segregations, and uphold the civil liberties of the people. In contrast, the present Court under Chief Justice William Rehnquist is generally conservative or passivist, taking mostly a hands-off stance on similar issues and largely deferring to the discretion of the political branches of government in most policy questions brought before it.

By imposing upon our judges the duty to intervene and to settle issues of grave abuse of discretion our Constitution has thereby mandated them to be activists. A duty cannot be evaded. The Supreme Court must uphold the Constitution at all times. Otherwise, it would be guilty of dereliction, of abandonment, of if solemn duty; and would repeat the judicial cop-outs that our 1987 Constitution abhors.

## The Court's Duty Intervene in Impeachment Cases That Infringe the Constitution

The Supreme Court has jurisdiction even over questions involving the impeachment of public officials. The Constitution imposes on it the plenary and extensive duty to rule on unconstitutional acts of “any” branch or instrumentality of government. While the Court is” authorized to pass upon the wisdom of an impeachment, it is nonetheless obligated to determine whether any incident of the proceedings violates any constitutional prohibition, condition or limitation imposed thereon. As the protector and interpreter of the Constitution, this Court is duty-bound to intervene and “to settle” the issue.

Furthermore, I pointed out the following:

1. The Constitution likewise grants the electoral tribunals of both Houses of Congress the authority to be the “sole” judges of all contests relating to the election, the returns and the qualifications of their respective members. Still, the Supreme Court reviews the decisions of these tribunals on certiorari.”

2. The Constitution has granted many powers and prerogatives exclusively to Congress. However, when these are exercised in violation of the Constitution or with grave abuse of discretion, the jurisdiction of the Court has been invoked; and its decisions thereon, respected by the legislative branch.

In Avelino v. Cuenco, the Court ruled on the issue of who was the duly elected President of the Senate, a question normally left to the sole discretion of that chamber; in Santiago v. Guingona, on who was the minority floor leader of the Senate; and in Daza v. Singson and Caseteng v. Mitra Jr., on who were the duly designated members of the Commission on Appointments representing the House of Representatives. The Court held in the latter two cases that it could intervene, because the question involved was “the legality, not the wisdom of the manner of filling the Commission on Appointment as prescribed by the Constitution.”

In the instant cases, the main issue was whether in initiating the second impeachment Complaint, the House of Representatives violated Section 3(5) of Article XI, which provided that “[n]o impeachment proceedings shall be initiated against the same official me than once within a period of one year.” The interpretation of this constitutional prohibition or condition cleat involved the “legality, not the wisdom,” of the acts of the House of Representatives. Thus, the Court had to “settle it.”.

## Observance of Due Process

The observance of the due process clause shrined in our fundamental law is a conditio sine qua non that cannot be ignored in any proceeding — administrative, judicial or otherwise. It is deemed written into every law, rule or contract, even though not expressly stated therein. Hence, also unconstitutional are the House rules on impeachment, insofar as they do not provide the charged official with (1) notice and (2) an opportunity to be heard prior to being impeached.

Let me just stress that in taking jurisdiction over the case and in exercising its power of judicial review, the Court was not pretending to be superior to Congress or to the President. It was merely upholding the supremacy of the Constitution and the rule of law.

Let me quote in full the Epilogue of my Opinion:

“T was initially tempted to adopt the view of Amici Jovito R. Salonga and Raul C. Pangalangan, who both maintain that although the Court has jurisdiction over the subject matter and although the second Impeachment Complaint was unconstitutional, the Court should nonetheless ‘use its power with care and only as a last resort’ and allow the House to correct its constitutional errors; or, failing in that, give the Senate the opportunity to invalidate the second Complaint.

“This Salonga-Pangalangan thesis has some advantages. It preserves the availability of judicial review as a ‘last resort’ to prevent or cure constitutional abuse; it also observes interdepartmental courtesy by allowing the seamless exercise of the congressional power of impeachment. It also enriches the doctrine of primary jurisdiction by enabling Congress to exercise fully its ‘exclusive’ authority to initiate, try and decide impeachment cases. In short, it gives Congress the primary jurisdiction; and the Court, ‘appellate’ certiorari power, over the case.

“Furthermore, the proponents of this deferential position add that the Senate may eventually rule that the second impeachment Complaint is unconstitutional, and that the matter may thus be settled definitively. The parties may be satisfied with the judgment of the Senate and, thus, obviate the need for this Court to rule on the matter. In this way, the latter would not need to grapple with any conflict of interest problem.

“With due respect, I believe that this stance of ‘passing the buck’ is not consistent with the activist duty imposed by the Constitution upon this Court.

“I close this Opinion with the truism that the judiciary is the ‘weakest’ branch of government. Nonetheless, when ranged against the more powerful branches, it should never cower in silence. Indeed, if the Supreme Court cannot take courage and wade into ‘grave abuse’ disputes involving the purse-disbursing Legislative Department, how much more deferential will it be when faced with constitutional abuses perpetrated by the even more powerful, sword-wielding Executive Department?

“I respectfully submit that the very same weakness of the Court becomes its strength when it dares speak through decisions that rightfully uphold the supremacy of the Constitution and the rule of law. The strength of the judiciary lies not in its lack of brute power, but in its moral courage to perform its constitutional duty at all times against all odds. Its might is in its being right.”

I voted to declare the second Impeachment Complaint unconstitutional and time-barred by Section 3(5) of Article XI of the Constitution.

## The Santiago Opinion

Justice Consuelo Ynares-Santiago concurred with the ponente that the Court had the power of judicial re view, which had been expanded by the Constitution not only to settle actual controversies involving rights that were legally demandable and enforceable, but also to determine whether there was grave abuse of discretion amounting to lack or excess of jurisdiction on the part of any branch or instrumentality of government. She opined that the Court was under mandate to assume jurisdiction over and to undertake judicial inquiry 1 even political questions, provided that grave abuse 0 discretion — the sole test of justiciability of purely poll cal issues —- was shown to have attended the contested act.

Justice Santiago explained that the Court checked the exercise of power of the other branches of government through judicial review. It was, she said, the arbiter of disputes involving the proper allocation and exercise of the different powers under the Constitution When it reviewed the constitutionality of the acts” Congress, it did not thereby assert its superiority over coequal branches of government. It merely asserted solemn and sacred obligation under the Constitution and affirmed constitutional supremacy.

In the resolution of the principal issue in the Petitions, Justice Santiago saw the need to draw a distinction between the power of the members of the House of Representatives to initiate impeachment proceedings, on the one hand; and, on the other, the manner in which they exercised that power. It was clear that the House had the exclusive power to initiate impeachment cases; and the Senate, the sole power to try and decide those cases. But the Court, upon a proper finding that either chamber had committed grave abuse of discretion or violated any constitutional provision, could invoke its corrective power of judicial review.

The 2001 House Rules on Impeachment, insofar as the initiation of impeachment proceedings was concealed, had unduly expanded the power of the House by restricting the constitutional time bar only to complaints “approved” by the House Committee on Justice. However, the one-year bar was a limitation set by the Constitution itself, which Congress could not overstep.

Moreover, the second impeachment Complaint was filed by only two complainants; namely, Representatives Gilberto G. Teodoro Jr. and Felix William B. Fuentebella. The rest of the members of the House, whose names appeared on the attachments, merely signed endorsements of the Complaint.

Mere endorsement by the House members, albeit embodied in a verified Resolution, did not suffice; such endorsement did not constitute a filing of the impel ment Complaint, as this term was plainly understood. For a verified complaint to be deemed to have been filed by at least one third of the members, all of them must have been named as complainants therein and must have signed the main complaint.

The foregoing defect was a vital issue in determining whether the House should transmit the Complaint to the Senate; and if it did, whether the Senate should entertain it.

The impeachment Complaint suffered from another serious flaw. As Amicus Curiae and former Senate President Jovito Salonga pointed out, the signing the impeachment Complaint purportedly by one n of the congressmen had been done without due p cess. The Chief Justice had not been served notice of the proceedings against him.

Notwithstanding the constitutional and procedural defects in the impeachment Complaint, Justice Santiago dissented from the majority in its decision to resolve the issues at that “premature” stage. In her opinion, the process of impeachment should first be allowed to run its course. The power of this Court as the final arbiter of justiciable questions should come into play only when the procedure as outlined in the Constitution had been exhausted.

Justice Santiago emphasized that the impeachment power should remain at all times and under all circumstances with the legislature, in which the Constitution had placed it. The common-law principle of judicial restraint would serve the public interest by allowing the political processes to operate without undue interference.

## The Gutierrez Concurring Opinion

The basic issue posed by the instant Petitions — whether the second impeachment Complaint against Chief Justice Hilario G. Davide Jr. violated the constitutional provision that “no impeachment proceedings shall be initiated against the same official more than once within the period of one year” — was a justiciable issue. Chief Justice Davide should not be subjected to a second impeachment proceeding. On the face of the Petitions, he had a right to be protected by the courts. So opined Justice Angelina Sandoval-Gutierrez.

Confronted with an issue involving constitutional infringement, should this Court shackle its hands under the principle of judicial self-restraint? It was not for the Court to withhold its judgment, because to do so would be a futile exercise of authority. It should do its duty to interpret the law.

There being a clear constitutional infringement, this was an appropriate occasion for judicial activism. To allow such a transcendental issue to pass into legal limbo would be a clear case of “misguided” judicial self-restraint. This Court had assiduously taken every opportunity to maintain the constitutional order, the distribution of public power, and the limitations of that power. Certainly, this was no time for a display of judicial weakness.

The House rules deviated from the clear language; of the Constitution and the intent of its framers. The rules infused into the term “initiate” a meaning more than what it actually connoted. The term “initiate” pertained to the initial act of filing the verified Complaint — not to the finding by the Committee on Justice that the Complaint and/or/or Resolution was sufficient in s sub: stance; or to the obtention of the vote of one third 0f all the members of the House as provided by the House rules.

Section 3(5) of Article XI, also referred to as the “anti-harassment clause,” had the dual objective of allowing the legislative body to concentrate on its tion, which was lawmaking, and of protecting public officials from harassment.

If the term “initiate” was to be construed as f ferring to the obtention of the vote of one third of the House members or to the date when the Committee on Justice ruled that the Complaint was sufficient in substance, would not one lose sight of the fact that much time had already been wasted by the House? Getting hold of the vote of one third was almost the last step necessary for the accused officer to be considered successfully impeached. The process would almost be complete insofar as the House was concerned.

The same was true with respect to the proceedings in the Committee on Justice. The hearing, the voting and the reporting in the House would definitely take much of the members’ precious time. If impeachment complaints would be deemed “initiated” only during those phases, then the object of allowing the legislature to concentrate on its functions could not really be achieved. Instead of acting as a legislative body, the House would be spending more time as a prosecutorial body.

In addition, Justice Gutierrez said, to let the accused official go through the above phases would be to subject him to additional harassment. As the process progressed, the greater the harassment of the official. One glaring illustration was the instant case.

Another constitutional objection to the second impeachment Complaint raised by petitioners was the fact that only Congressmen Teodoro and Fuentebella had signed it, in violation of Section 3(4) of Article XI of the Constitution. The requirement was that the complaint or resolution must at the time of filing be verified and sworn to before the secretary general of the House by each of at least one third of all the members thereof.

Indeed, the suits involved matters of first impression and of immense importance to the public, considering that — as previously stated — this was the first time a Chief Justice was being subjected to an impeach= ment proceeding that petitioners thought was prohibited by the Constitution. Obviously, if such a proceeding was not prevented and nullified, public funds amount: ing to millions of pesos would be disbursed for an illegal act. Undoubtedly, this was a grave national concern involving public interest that was of paramount importance.

## The Callejo Concurring Opinion

Justice Romeo J. Callejo Sr. opined that the construction of the word “initiate” would be determinative of the resolution of two issues: whether Sections 16 and 17 of Rule V of the 2001 House Rules of Procedure in Impeachment Proceedings violated Section 3(5) of Article XI of the Constitution; and whether the October 23, 2003 Impeachment Complaint violated Section 3(5) of Article XI of the Constitution, proscribing impeachment proceedings initiated against the same respondent more than once within a period of one year. The questions were of necessity within the Court's jurisdiction to resolve.

The prematurity of the Petition and the need for judicial restraint were discussed at length by Justice Calle In his view, there was no doubt that the Petitions at bar had seasonably been filed against Speaker Jose de Venecia and his co-respondents. In Pimentel v. Aguirre, this Court had ruled that upon the mere enactment of the questioned law or the approval of the challenged action, the dispute would ripen into a judicial controversy even without any other overt act.

With respect to the Respondent Senate, the Petitions were premature, as the issues before the Court related solely to the proceedings in the House.

Justice Callejo rejected the call for judicial restraint for being unacceptable on the following grounds:

First. The complainants and the endorsers of the Complaint, and even the House of Representatives through Speaker Jose de Venecia, were bent on transmitting the impeachment Complaint to the Senate without delay.

Second. The courts should take cognizance of and resolve actions involving issues within the competence of tribunals of special competence. The former should do so without a need for the latter to resolve such issues, if the respondents had acted with grave abuse of discretion, arbitrariness and capriciousness.

Third. Another important issue raised in the Petitions at bar was the constitutionality of Sections 16 and 17 of Rule V of the 2001 House Rules of Procedure. The resolution of the first question was anchored on and inextricably interrelated with the resolution of this second issue. Furthermore, decisive of both issues was the Court’s construction of the word “initiate” in Sections 3(1) and (5) in relation to Section 3(3) of Article XI of the Constitution.

Fourth. The Senate had no jurisdiction to resolve the issue of the constitutionality of Sections 16 and 17 of Rule V of the 2001 House Rules of Procedure, in the same manner that the House of Representatives had no jurisdiction to rule on the constitutionality of the Impeachment Rules of the Senate. The Senate and House of Representatives were coequal.

Fifth. The doctrine of primary jurisdiction would come into play in the Senate, only upon transmittal of the impeachment Complaint to it.

Sixth. The resolution of whether the October 2 2003 Complaint was time-barred did not require the application of a special skill or technical expertise on the part of the Senate.

Justice Callejo called attention to the fact that the word initiate was twice used in Section 3; first in paragraph 1, and again in paragraph 5. “Initiate” in paragraph 1 was followed by the phrase “all cases of impeachment," while the word “initiated” in paragraph 5 of the same Section was preceded by the words “no impeachment proceedings shall be.” On the other hand, the word “file” or “filed” was used in paragraphs 2 and 4 of Section 3.

There was a clear distinction between the words “file” and “initiate.” Under the Rules of Civil Procedure, complaints are filed when they are delivered into the custody of the clerk of court or the judge — either by personal delivery or by registered mail — and the payment of docket and other fees therefore. In criminal cases, the information or criminal complaint is considered filed when it is delivered to the court — whether for purposes of preliminary investigation or for trial, as the case may be.

Distinction must be made, said Justice Callejo, between the phrase “the case” in Section 3(1) and the word “proceedings” in Section 3(5). “The case” referred to an action commenced or initiated in the Senate by the House of Representatives’ transmittal of the articles of impeachment (or complaint for impeachment) for trial. The word “proceeding” meant “the regular and orderly progression of a lawsuit including all acts and events been the time of commencement and the entry of judgments; an act or step that is part of a larger action; an act (one by the authority or direction of the court, express or implied; it is more comprehensive than the word ‘action’ but it may include in its general sense all the steps taken or measures adopted in the prosecution or defense of an action including the pleadings and judgment.” The word initiate meant “to begin with or get going; make a beginning; perform or facilitate the first action.”

Based on the foregoing definitions, the phrase "initiate all cases of impeachment” in Section 3(1) referred to the commencement of impeachment cases by the House of Representatives through the transmittal of the complaint for impeachment or articles of impeachment to the Senate for trial and decision. The word initiated in Section 3(5), on the other hand, referred to the filing of the complaint for impeachment with the Office of the Secretary General of the House of Representatives, either by a verified complaint by any member of the House or by any citizen — upon a resolution of endorsement by any member of the House and a referral of the com plaint to the Committee of Justice and Human Rights for action; or upon the filing, by at least one third of members of the House, of a verified complaint or resolution of impeachment that shall constitute the Articles of Impeachment.

The House of Representatives distorted and ignored the plain wording of Section 3(1) of Article XI the Constitution, when it provided in Section 16 of Rule V that a complaint of impeachment would be “deem initiated” in the House of Representatives “on the the committee of justice finds that the said verified complaint and/or resolution against such official, as the case may be, is sufficient in substance or on the date the House votes to overturn or affirm the finding of the said committee that the verified complaint and/or resolution, as the case may be, is not sufficient in substance.”

Consequently, the House of Representatives also distorted the computation of the one-year time bar under Section 3(5) of Article XI of the Constitution. The House ruled that the time bar was to begin only “on the day this Committee on Justice finds that the verified complaint and/or resolution against such official is sufficient in substance or on the date the House votes to overturn or affirm the finding of the said Committee that the verified complaint and/or resolution, as the case may be, is not sufficient in substance.”

Rule V of the 2001 Rules of Procedure was contrary to the Constitution; thus it was void.

## The Azcuna Concurrence

Justice Adolfo S. Azcuna said that there could be no serious challenge to the petitioners’ locus standi. Eight were members of the House of Representatives, with direct interest in the integrity of its proceedings. Furthermore, as taxpayers they had sufficient standing, in view of the transcendental importance of the issue at hand. ‘The case went beyond the fate of Chief Justice Davide, as it shook the very foundations of our system of government and posed a question as to our s as a democratic polity.

There was, moreover, an actual controversy involving rights that were legally demandable, thereby leaving no doubt as to the justiciability of the Petitions.

Worth noting, however, was that the Constitution placed the provision on impeachment — not in Articles VI, VII and VIII on governmental powers - but in Article XI on accountability of public officers.

This placement was clearly intentional and meant to signal the importance of the accountability of public officers; and impeachment was an instrument of enforcing or securing that accountability, and not simply a method of checks and balances by one power over another.

Article XI divided the power of impeachment - the first part, or the power to “initiate,” was given exclusively to the House of Representatives; the second part, the power to try and decide, was granted solely to the Senate.

It is clear that unlike in other countries, our Constitution opted textually to commit the sole or exclusive power to this or that department or branch of government, but provided specific procedures and equally textually identifiable limits to the exercise of those powers.

Thus, as regards the filing of a complaint for impeachment, it provided for in detail who may file and — after being filed — what shall be done to the complaint; the referral of the complaint to the proper committee; the hearing, the voting and the reporting thereon in the House; the action of the House on the complaint; and the time frame for every step (Subsection 2).

Similarly, the required number of votes to affirm or override a favorable or contrary resolution is stated (Subsection 3).

In like manner, for the trial by the Senate to proceed forthwith, our Constitution specifically laid down what was needed for a complaint or resolution of impeachment to constitute the Articles of Impeachment ~- a verified complaint or resolution of impeachment must have been filed by at least one third of all the members of the House (Subsection 4).

Justice Azcuna held that when the Constitution not only gave or allocated a power to one department or branch of government, be it solely or exclusively, but at the same time specifically provided certain limits to its exercise, then this Court — which belonged to the department called upon under the Constitution to interpret its provisions — had the jurisdiction to do so.

The Court’s jurisdiction, he said, was not so much a power but a duty, as clearly set forth in Section 1 of Article VIII of the Constitution.

The second impeachment Complaint, according to the respondent, was not barred, because under the House Rules of Procedure in Impeachment Proceedings} the first Complaint filed four months easier was not yet “deemed initiated,” since it had not been reported the floor of the House of Representatives. Justice Azcuna opined that this interpretation would stretch the meaning of “initiate” and defeat the purpose of the Constitution. Such a view would allow considerable harassment from multiple complaints filed within one year against the same official. It would tie up the legislature, particularly the House of Representatives, in too frequent and too many complaints of impeachment filed before it, leaving it little time to attend to its principal task of legislation.

The respondent also contended that Section 3(5) of Article XI referred to impeachment proceedings:) the Senate, not in the House. This interpretation, 0 ever, would do violence to the carefully allocated division of power found in the said provision. Precisely, the part of the power — that of initiating impeachment was lodged in the House. What the House initiated in the Senate was an impeachment CASE, not PROCEEDINGS. The proceedings for impeachment preceded the impeachment case and took place exclusively in the House. What was to take place in the Senate was a trial to culminate in a decision.

Justice Azcuna had earlier called attention to placement of the power of impeachment, not in the Articles on governmental powers, but in the Article on accountability. This approach indicated that the power was not essentially legislative in character or primarily intended as a check by the legislative department on the other branches. Its main purpose under our Constitution was to provide accountability, but without detriment to the governmental power of legislation under Article VI.

## The Tinga Concurrence

Justice Dante O. Tinga explained that the 1987 Constitution had introduced conditionalities and limitations on the power of impeachment. While these limitations were intrusive on rules of parliamentary practice, they could not take on a merely procedural character, because they were mandatory impositions made by the highest law of the land. As such, they could not be dispensed with upon the whims of the legislative body.

The determination of compliance with a constitutional prescribed rule, requirement or limitation on the exercise of a power that was delegated by the Constitution itself to a body or an official was invariably a justiciable controversy, according to Justice Tinga.

He explained that the term “judicial supremacy” had previously been used in relation to the Supreme Court’s power of judicial review,” yet the phrase wrong connoted the bugaboo of a judiciary supreme to all other branches of the government. Whenever the Court mediated to allocate constitutional boundaries or invalidated the acts of a coordinate body, what it upheld was not its own supremacy, but that of the Constitution.” When this supremacy was invoked, it compelled the errant branches of government to obey, not the Supreme Cow but the Constitution.,

There was another unique, albeit uneasy, issue 0 standing that should be discussed, said justice Tinga. ‘The party who could most palpably demonstrate injury, and whose rights had been most affected by the actions of the respondents, was the Chief Justice. Precisely because of that consideration, he was unable to file the Petition for himself. In a situation in which it would be difficult for the person whose rights were being asserted to present a grievance before any court, the U.S. Supreme Court held in Barrows v. Jackson that the rules on standing would be outweighed by the need to protect those fundamental rights; in such a situation, standing may be granted. There was no reason why this doctrine could not be invoked in this jurisdiction, Justice Tinga pointed out.

In his opinion, the Senate did not have the jurisdiction to determine whether the House Rules of Impeachment violated the Constitution. Impeachment was not an inherent legislative function, although it was traditionally conferred on the legislature.

He pointed out that the Senate could not be expected to declare void the Articles of Impeachment or the offending Rules of the House, based on which the latter completed the impeachment process. He suggested that the Senate could look beyond the Articles of Impeachment. While it acted as an impeachment court for the purpose of trying and deciding impeachment cases, its impeachment powers were not residual; hence, its “transformation” into such a court did not vest it with any of the powers inherent in the judiciary. Whatever powers the Senate could acquire as an impeachment court were limited to what the Constitution had provided and could not extend to and assume a semblance of judicial review of the acts of coequal components of government, including those of the House.

Justice Tinga observed that any attempt on the part of the Senate to invalidate the House Rules of Impeachment was obnoxious to inter-chamber courtesy. If the Senate were to render those Rules unconstitutional it would set an unfortunate precedent that might engender a wrong-headed assertion that one chamber of Congress could invalidate the rules and regulations promulgated by the other chamber. ‘The duty to pass upon the validity of the House Rules of Impeachment was imposed by the Constitution, not upon the Senate, but upon the Court.

Simplistic notions of rectitude were likely to cause a furor over the decision of the Supreme Court, even if it was the right one, noted Justice Tinga. Yet it had to decide this case, because the Constitution had dictated that it do so. The most fatal charge that could be levied on the Court was that it did not obey the Constitution.

In many cases, according to Justice Tinga, the Court deviated from its self-imposed policy of prudence and restraint. Such deviations were expressed when, despite its pronounced distaste for cases that apparently catered to the ostensibly self-serving concerns of the Court as whole or of its individual members, it proceeded anyway to resolve issues involving the interpretation of the Constitution and the independence of the judiciary.

## Summary

At bottom, Justices Vitug, Panganiban, (Quisumbing, Gutierrez, Carpio, Martinez, Corona, Morales (ponente), Callejo, Azcuna and Tinga voted to grant the Petitions; while Justices Bellosillo, Puno and Santiago opted to dismiss the Petitions — resulting in a decisive 11-3-1 vote. Chief Justice Davide took no part.

## Summary

Chapter 15 of 'Leveling the Playing Field' is CJP's account of Francisco v. House of Representatives, which he called the most controversial case decided by the Supreme Court during his ninth year in office. The Court effectively stopped the House from acting on the second impeachment Complaint against Chief Justice Hilario G. Davide Jr., and on November 10, 2003, the day the Decision was promulgated, the House voted 115 to 77 to respect it, averting what he described as a constitutional crisis of googolplex proportions; he credited the Senate's restraint and President Arroyo's support for the outcome. He recounted the facts -- the 2002 House Resolution probing the Chief Justice's disbursement of the Judiciary Development Fund, Estrada's first impeachment Complaint of June 2, 2003 referred to the Committee on Justice on August 5 and dismissed as insufficient in substance on October 22, and the second Complaint filed the next day by Representatives Teodoro and Fuentebella -- and then the Court's ruling, delivered through Justice Carpio-Morales: judicial review extends to impeachment proceedings; standing was relaxed and the issue was ripe and justiciable; and, on the authority of amicus Florenz Regalado who had sponsored the provision, 'initiate' means to file a complaint and take initial action on it, so that Sections 16 and 17 of Rule V of the 2001 House Impeachment Rules contravened Section 3(5) of Article XI and the second Complaint was time-barred. He then summarized each Separate Opinion in order of seniority, from Bellosillo's and Puno's calls for restraint and Santiago's view that the process should first run its course, through Vitug's, Gutierrez's, Callejo's, Azcuna's and Tinga's concurrences. In his own Opinion he disclosed that he had inhibited because of a former law partner, a close friendship with amicus Salonga, and his own exposure in the first Complaint, but was directed to participate; citing Perfecto v. Meer, Nitafan, Abbas and Estrada v. Desierto, and noting that mass inhibition would strip the Court of a quorum, he argued that jurisdiction may not be declined. Because the 1987 Constitution, authored on this point by Chief Justice Concepcion to end Martial Law-era judicial cop-outs, commands judges to strike down grave abuse of discretion, he concluded that they are mandated to be activists, rejected the deferential Salonga-Pangalangan thesis as passing the buck, and closed that the judiciary's weakness becomes its strength, for its might is in its being right. The vote was 11-3-1, with Chief Justice Davide taking no part.
