# Leveling the Playing Field -- Ch. 7: A Unique Mode of Nurturing Democracy

A lawyer-friend of mine was diagnosed to have malignant cells in his brain. He was advised by his surgeon that the operation to excise the cancer would surely be successful. “How can you be sure that I will survive the surgery?” my friend anxiously asked.

“I am certain because a survey shows that nine out of my ten patients die on the operating table,” answered the doctor. “That’s no assurance,” the lawyer complained.

“Don’t worry,” the surgeon comforted him. “Nine of my last patients have already died. You are the tenth in line. Based on the survey, you will surely live!”

This may be a bizarre way of starting a talk on polling and opinion research before the world’s experts in these fields. But I assure you, my friend survived the operating table and lives to this day to testify persuasively on the validity and the accuracy of surveys, at least those of the surgical variety.

## Credible Indices of Voter Preferences

Levity aside, I am deeply honored at being invited by Dr. Mahar Mangahas to be your guest speaker during the Fourth Asian Regional Conference of the World Association of Public Opinion Research (WAPOR) headed by President Kathleen Francovic, polling director of CBS News of the United States, who is in the audience today. You are undoubtedly aware that your Conference is being held amidst a full-blown electoral campaign in the Philippines. Although polling and opinion surveys started #8 research tools to analyze the usage, attitude and image (UAT) of the marketing programs of commercial establishments and products,’ they have become — in the hands of competent organizations like the Social Weather Stations (SWS) — credible indices of the electoral preferences of our voters.

The conduct and the publication of electoral polls (during the last decade have become normal staples of the campaign menus of Philippine elections. Opinion polls have done very well in prognosticating election results. Our people have come to believe in them, sometimes even more than in the official count. In fact, one wag naughtily suggested the abolition of expensive elections and, in their place, the institution of what have proven to be accurate poll surveys.

Ladies and gentlemen, I am pleased to know that WAPOR has a policy of sharing research information amongst its members in over 50 countries. Surfing the: Internet, I gathered that the publication of the results of election opinion polls is a subject that has caused much debate and emotion around the world.

## Legality of Polling in Selected Countries

For instance, in Singapore, the dissemination of information through the Internet is restricted. Election surveys and exit polls are banned. In Mexico, it is a criminal offense to release — within eight days before an election — a poll predicting an election winner.’

On the other hand, in India, the petition to “ban” the publication of the results of preelection and exit polls covering the 13th Lok Sabha elections was dismissed by its Supreme Court. Similarly, the Canadian Supreme Court ruled in 1998 that restricting the publication of election surveys was unconstitutional and a violation of the freedom of expression.’ In the United Kingdom, the approach its uniquely British: on the day of the elections, they voluntarily refrain from reporting the results of their opinion polls.

In recent years, there has been a trend towards liberalization in the publication of election polls. In South Africa, there used to be a six-week period during which polls could be conducted, but their results could not be released. Now, poll results can freely be published up to the day before an election.’ France has reduced the embargo from seven days to the day before an election.

In a number of countries, despite a local embargo, poll survey results have been published on foreign websites and thus made accessible to the locals. Some 35 percent of surveyed individuals in countries with an embargo said they would consider using the Internet to evade restrictions, compared with 31 percent who would not and 34 percent who did not know.’

## Exit Polls, a Part of Free Expression

In the Philippines, public opinion polls are viewed as an emerging paradigm of free expression.” In two landmark Decisions, our Supreme Court has definitively ruled that the conduct of these opinion polls, as well as the public dissemination of their results, is part of the right to free expression and is thus constitutionally protected as a unique mode of nurturing democracy.

It was on January 28, 2000, that the Philippine Supreme Court had the first occasion to discuss public opinion polls — particularly exit polls — in the case ABS-CBN Broadcasting Corporation v. Commission on Elections, a Decision I had the honor of writing,

In this case, ABS-CBN, a major local television network, assailed a Resolution issued by the Commission on Election (Comelec), the government agency that regulates elections. ‘This Comelec Resolution restrained the Social Weather Stations (SWS) from conducting exit polls during the 1998 presidential elections; and the I network, from broadcasting the results. The elector commission believed that the exit polls might conflict with the official ballot count and thus cause confusion.

The Comelec justified the ban on the ground that “an exit poll has the tendency to sow confusion considering the randomness of selecting interviewees, which further makes the exit poll highly unreliable. ‘The probability that the results of such exit polls may not be in harmony with the official count made by the Comelec x x x is ever present. In other words, the exit poll has a clear and present danger of destroying the credibility and integrity of the electoral process.”

In short, the Court was faced with the issue of whether the Comelec, in the guise of protecting the sanctity of elections and the secrecy of the ballot, might totally prohibit the holding of exit polls and the dissemination of their results through the mass media. The Decision answered this question with a firm “No.” Exit polls formed part of free expression and were entitled to constitutional protection.

The Supreme Court ruled that the arguments of Comelec were purely speculative and untenable. First, interviewees or participants in this survey were seed at random, so that the results would as much as possible be representative and thus be reflective of the general sentiment of the community polled. Second, the survey results were not meant to replace or be on a par with the official Comelec count. These were merely the opinion of the polling group as to who had probably been opted for, based on the limited data gathered from polled individuals. Finally, the credibility and integrity of the election, which was an exercise separate and independent from the exit polls, was not at stake in the case. Therefore, the holding and the reporting of exit polls could not have undermined the elections.

Freedom of expression is a fundamental principle of every democratic government, a preferred right standing on a higher level than substantive economic freedom or other liberties. The lessons of history, both political and legal, teach us that freedom of speech is an indispensable condition for nearly every other form of freedom.

Free speech, which includes a free press, is liberty to discuss publicly and truthfully any matter of public interest without censorship or punishment. It is the right of the people to inform and be informed, to unhampered reportage, and to access channels of information and opinion.

The guarantees of free expression were history intended to provide some assurance that governments would remain responsive to the will of the people, in line with the republican principle that sovereignty resides in — and all government authority emanates from them. The viability of a truly representative government depends upon the effective protection and exercise of the rights of the people to think freely, to discuss independently, and to assemble for redress of their grievances without fear of reprisal. These rights underlie the mechanisms of peaceful change in a democratic polity.

Freedom of expression is a means of assuring in-woul self-fulfillment, of attaining the truth, of secure people’s participation in social and political diction n-making, and of maintaining the balance between ability and change.'’ It represents a profound commitment to the principle that debates on public issues should be uninhibited, robust and wide open. It means more than the right to approve existing political beliefs or economic arrangements, to lend support to official usurer, or to take refuge in the existing climate of non on any matter of public consequence. Paraphrasing, the eminent Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr., Supreme Court stressed that this freedom encompasses the thought we hate, no less than the thought we agree with.”

It is also important to note that while all poll organizations equally enjoy the right to publicize their surveys, they do not enjoy equal public credibility. Just as all newspapers, radio and TV stations have an equal right to express their opinions, they do not enjoy the same degree of public acceptability.

## Unconstitutionality of Poll Embargo

The second landmark Decision on polling was issued by our Supreme Court on May 5, 2001 in Social Weather Stations v. Comelec.

In this case, SWS and the Manila Standard, a newspaper of general circulation, sought to prohibit the Comelec from enforcing Section 5.4 of Republic Act No 9006 (otherwise known as the Fair Elections Act.) This law banned the publication, within fifteen days prior an election, of surveys affecting national candidates and within seven days, of surveys affecting local candidates. The Court held that the embargo constituted an unconstitutional abridgement of the freedom of expression for the following reasons:

First, the prohibition constituted prior restraint, which was anathema to free speech.

Second, the powers granted by the Constitution to the Comelec were limited, and the authority to enforce the embargo was not among the powers entrusted to the Commission.

Third, citing the US Supreme Court in United States v. O 'Brien, our Court said that the embargo was an unreasonable regulation that sought to advance a government interest at the expense of free speech.

The right to free expression is constitutionally guaranteed to journalists and broadcasters to enable them to disseminate their individual opinions. Why then should the publication of opinion polls that reflect the views of the general population be banned?

## Limitations on Public Opinion Polls

It must be stressed that, like any other right, the freedom to conduct public opinion polls and to disseminate the information gathered from them may be regulated, not to stifle or diminish it, but in fact to safeguard it; and, at same time, to ensure that it does not collide with or over the rights of others. There must, therefore, be a continuing effort to ensure that public opinion polls remain true 1 struments of freedom of expression as declared by the Supreme Court.

In ABS-CBN v. Comelec, the Court made some recommendations to arrive at this end, such as designating specific limited area for conducting exit polls; allowing only professional survey groups to conduct the polls; and require ing pollsters to keep a reasonable distance from the vo in center, as well as to wear distinctive clothing to set them apart from election officials.

Furthermore, the research organizations and the government should educate the public on the factors surrounding the conduct of public opinion polls. Rest must be published with explanations of such terms as “margin of error,’ “sample size,” and “demographics of respondents.” There must also be transparency in the methodology used by the groups conducting the polls, so that the public can judge the accuracy of the results.

## SWS Surveys on the Supreme Court

The great passion of SWS in catalyzing jurispru-(dance on opinion research is not limited to bringing to the Supreme Court and eventually winning the two landmark cases I mentioned. From 1985 up to the present, »W has been periodically polling public perception of major government offices including the Supreme Court. Like any democratic institution, the Court is sensitive to how the public perceives it, especially its credibility in deciding public interest cases.

True, cases are won or lost, not because of popular palatability, compromise, friendship or political accommodation — not to mention pecuniary consideration ~» but because of reasoned argument flowing from legal principles and precedents. Let me repeat: the Supreme Court values its independence and integrity. It will resist intrusions by any agency of government, whether it be the executive or the legislative; pressures from lobby groups and vested interests; and threats from (mob, crackpots and military adventurers. It will decide cases with cold impartiality on the basis of the law and the facts, after meticulously observing due process. Fiat justitia ruat coelum. Justice shall be done though the heavens may fall.

Having stressed this basic parameter, let me add that the Supreme Court nonetheless realizes that it can be effective only as long as it retains the long-term support of the public. The Tribunal knows that public acceptance of its actions and decisions — especially those that go against public opinions and perceptions — is possible only if it enjoys the people’s residual respect and esteem. Without the support of the public, it becomes useless, because by itself it cannot enforce its own pronouncements. It does not command the military or the police or, for that matter, “the raging mob”; neither does it have the money to grease political patronage.

But there is a difference between the shifting wind: of public emotion and the long-term trust in the institution. While critics, mobs, disgruntled litigants and lobby groups may rant from time to time against some of its decisions, the Court will always steadfastly and courageously uphold the rule of law. Indeed, its real strength lies not in its might, but in its being right.

In this context of developing residual public trust, the Court finds tremendous value in the SWS survey As a result of these polls, our Court has become ever more sensitive to the need for long-term public acceptance of its actions and decisions.

Consequently, it has embarked on a program of transparency and accountability. It inaugurated its Public Information Office; opened an Internet homepage; issued more reader-friendly decisions; held regular dialogues with specific sectors of society; established some rapport with media; adhered strictly to ethical standards; and made public information a cornerstone of its ongoing, judicial reform program.

## Conclusion

In summary, may I reiterate my sincere thanks to SWS and WAPOR for this privilege of addressing you and of breaking bread with you this noon. SWS has been the catalyst of two landmark cases involving opinion research and polling. Equally important, it has been instrumental in giving scientific and accurate feedback on how the public perceives the Supreme Court. It has thereby stressed the need for the Court to respond with reform measures to improve its long-term acceptance as a democratic institution worthy of public trust and conlicense.

I thank you.

## Summary

Chapter 7 of 'Leveling the Playing Field' records CJP's address to the Fourth Asian Regional Conference of the World Association of Public Opinion Research, opened with a joke about a surgeon who assured a patient he would survive because nine of his last ten patients had already died. Thanking Mahar Mangahas and greeting WAPOR president Kathleen Francovic, he observed that polling had grown from a marketing research tool into a credible index of voter preference in the hands of organizations like Social Weather Stations, believed by Filipinos sometimes more than the official count. He surveyed how other countries treat election polls -- bans in Singapore and Mexico, judicial protection in India and Canada, voluntary restraint in Britain, and liberalization in South Africa and France -- noting that embargoes are evaded through foreign websites. He then explained two landmark Decisions. In ABS-CBN v. Comelec, which he wrote, the Court rejected as speculative Comelec's claim that exit polls posed a clear and present danger to the electoral process, holding that respondents were randomly chosen, that results were not meant to replace the official count, and that exit polls formed part of free expression. In Social Weather Stations v. Comelec, the Court voided Section 5.4 of the Fair Elections Act as prior restraint, beyond Comelec's powers, and an unreasonable regulation under United States v. O'Brien. He restated the doctrine that free speech is an indispensable condition of other freedoms and that debate should be uninhibited, robust and wide open, encompassing the thought we hate, while conceding that polling may be regulated to safeguard it through designated areas, professional pollsters and disclosure of margin of error and methodology. He closed on the Court's own standing: it decides with cold impartiality and resists mobs and lobbies, yet commanding neither army nor money it needs residual public trust, its real strength lying not in its might but in its being right -- which is why SWS surveys prompted its transparency reforms.
