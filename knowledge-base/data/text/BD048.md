Strictly speaking, case management refers to the manner in which a particular case — from its filing to its disposition — is handled by a trial court at its level. Case management is therefore appropriately employed in the trial of cases as a strategy to facilitate their hearing and early disposition. It involves organizing case events or stages thereof by adding necessary events that are allowed under the law or the rules of procedure and by eliminating those that are unnecessary and cause undue delay.

Effective case management also includes determining the number of witnesses needed to establish the cause of each party; rationalizing the presentation of evidence, both objective and testimonial; and exercising firm judicial control over the calendaring of case events and hearings — all with the end in view of achieving an early disposition of the case, without running roughshod over the substantial and the procedural requirements of due process.

## Mediation Before Pretrial

In the Philippines, the manner in which a case is to be tried is determined during pretrial, which is mandatory in both civil and criminal cases. However, the case is first referred for mediation whenever appropriate. (Philippine law provides instances when this recourse is not allowed.) If mediation is unsuccessful, the case returned to the trial court and scheduled for pretrial proper. The latter proceeding involves, in civil case simplifying the issues; amending the pleadings, if warranted; obtaining stipulations or admissions; limiting the number of witnesses; referring technical issues to a com: missioner for a preliminary conference; and such other actions as may aid in the prompt disposition of the case.

In criminal cases, matters taken up on pretrial a plea bargaining, stipulation or admission of facts, ing of exhibits for purposes of identification, waiver objections to the admissibility of evidence, and other actions that would promote a reasonably swift resolution of the case. The only caveat here is that the ri of the accused must not be violated.

Before Philippine trial courts, bio-cases — civil and criminal — are managed along the same line as non-bio-cases and are similarly determined during the pretrial stage. The only difference is that extensive judicial intervention is required here, considering the novelty of the scientific issues involved. After pretrial, the court issues ‘ pretrial order that serves as the trial guide for the court, the litigants and their lawyers.

## Scientific Evidence Essential to a Bio-Case

As scientific evidence is an essential component of a bio-case, the testimonies of experts become the primary means of establishing or disproving a cause of action. The most common cases involving biological issues concern the application of forensic DNA analysis to immigration, marital infidelity, the identification of lost or stolen children, the determination of paternity, and the identification of human sources of biological samples taken from crime scenes. Forensic scientists are experts who convey special knowledge that goes beyond firsthand observations. Thus, they are allowed to draw inference from facts and to give expert opinion — something that even judges, investigators or other fact finders may not be able to do.

During the pretrial of a bio-case, the number of expert witnesses and their qualifications, as well as the nature and the relevancy of their testimonies, may already be determined and agreed upon in order to simplify the issues and shorten trial time. Parenthetically parties may want to present their own hired “experts.” Inevitably, a clash of opinions may ensue and affect; impartial judicial assessment of the issue. One proposal to forestall this situation is for the litigants to agree that the court appoint the expert witness, but that they themselves shoulder the expenses for the laboratory tests at the expert testimony.

Initially, Philippine courts were not inclined to accept DNA profiles as evidence because of insufficient knowledge of the technology, lack of laboratory fact ties, and contamination of specimens by inexperienced investigators, among others. But then came the Supreme Court Decision in People v. Vallejo (GR No. 144656, May 9, 2002), affirming the finding of the lower court th the DNA profile of the accused matched that of the semen obtained from the rape victim’s body. With that ruling, it is now settled that DNA profiles are admissible as evidence in judicial proceedings. The Court said that “in assessing the probative value of the DNA evidence, therefore courts should consider among other things, the following data: how the samples were collected, how they were handled, the possibility of contamination of the samples, the procedure followed in analyzing the samples, whether the proper standards were followed in conducting the tests, and the qualification of the analyst who conducted the tests.

## The Relevancy Test Vis-à-vis the Frye Rule

The above-quoted pronouncement follows the “relevancy” test under Section 2, Rule 128 of the Rules) Court of the Philippines. This test is perceived to be more liberal, as compared with the Frye rule of assessing and construing evidence. The cited provision is bolstered by Section 49 of Rule 130, also of the Rules of (Court, on expert testimony. The latter section provides that the court may receive in evidence the opinion of a witness on a matter requiring special knowledge, skill, existence or training that such witness has been shown to possess.

Problems, however, may arise in accessing expert testimony on account of high financial costs to the parties or the trial court itself The technology has only recently been developing in the Philippines and, under-‘standby, there exists a dearth of DNA experts in areas such as laboratory testing and protocols, molecular biology, chemistry, genetics, and even population statistics.

If there are experts, their services are expensive and beyond the reach of a great number of the Philippine populace. This problem of cost gives rise, in turn, to) constitutional issues of due process and equal protection by the law. For instance, the accused may not be accorded a level playing field in court, simply because they cannot afford to hire their own experts.

Trials that are managed effectively allow the presentation of admissible and relevant evidence in an orderly manner, with a minimum of redundant or non essential testimony. Avoided in this way is wastage — the time and resources of the court and of expense on the part of the litigants.

During the trial, a judge will also have to contend with some constitutional issues involving the rights” the accused, like the right against self incrimination, especially in the manner in which DNA samples are obtained; the right against unreasonable searches and seizures; and the right to privacy and to due process. Thus, in managing a bio-case, the trial judge acknowledges the right of the accused to be given enough time to prepare for trial.

For calendaring hearings, the applicable formula consists of seeing to it that the time intervals between hearings or other case events should be long eno give the accused or the defendant time to prepare., short enough to encourage preparation. The judge must adhere to a strict “no continuance or adjournment” po The parties will also be accorded the right to access and examine all DNA-related documents in order to be able, if they so wish, to refer these to another expert for a review or an assessment.

## Pilot Test of Case Flow Management

The Philippines is currently pilot-testing effective case flow management in the trial courts of one of the cities of Metro Manila. Tracking systems — fast, standard, complex, and holding tracks — have been designed to) serve as pathways for a case to proceed reasonably swiftly from the moment of its filing up to its disposition, whether by settlement or by a full-blown trial by the court.

One has to consider the nature and the complexity of the issues in a bio-case; the constitutional rights of ' accused; the current state of technology; the level of knowledge that judges, prosecutors and practicing lati have about DNA testing — not to mention the kind of evidence to be presented in court. A modified complex track will thus have to be formulated to accumulate these factors and still maintain a case flow pros that will effectively reduce, if not eliminate, undue delay in case processing and management.
