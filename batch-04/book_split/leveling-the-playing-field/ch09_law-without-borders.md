# Chapter 9: Law Without Borders

by Ismael G. Khan Jr.

This commentary was printed in the May 30, 2004 issue of the Philippine Daily Inquirer. It is a fitting summary and evaluation of the five speeches/lectures I delivered on the biosciences in Chile on March 1-5, 2004, during two international conferences; namely, the Global Forum on Biotechnology and the Judicial Institute on the Biosciences. The five speeches/lectures are included in this book, as Chapters 9 to 13. Atty. Ismael G. Khan Ir, the writer of this introductory, is assistant court administrator and chief of the Supreme Court’s Public Information Office.

The long-arm of the law - a figure of speech that best characterizes the administration of justice - has developed into a global concept whose far-reaching significance literally leaps across international boundaries. The United States, acting as the world’s policeman, is the foremost exponent of the extra-territorial enforcement of its laws best exemplified by its resolute application of pre-emptive self-defense against terrorism which itself respects no frontiers.

This sea-change in the philosophy and practice of the law is the linchpin in what Supreme Court Justice Artemio V. Panganiban calls “judicial globalization.” He posits the view that the advances in telecommunications, the movement of people from one country to another, the rapid change in technology and the new scientific realities of our times have all made our world smaller and smaller. He has observed that even as the pronouncements of a court are supposed to be restricted to confines of a country, domestic courts can in reality solve international cases under exceptional circumstances or extraordinary situations. This is in fact the thesis of his most recent book “The Bio-Age Dawns on the Judiciary.”

The “dawn of the bio-age” in his view has effectively effaced artificial boundaries between and among nations as they grapple with legal problems and issues wrought by scientific breakthroughs in human engineering, e.g., stem cell research, in vitro fertilization, and cleaning. These are issues which include “not only the enhancement of health and longevity; but also the alternation and even the manipulation of human behavior, especially three higher forms with genetic roots: those linked to intelligence, crime and sexuality.” Justice Panganiban is thereby alerting us to “a scientific and technological revolution that started in humanity’s ability to decode DNA; and is careening towards the more mind-boggling power to manipulate human life, health and behavior, well being and happiness,” resulting from the successful and comprehensive mapping of the human genome, a gargantuan project completed way ahead of schedule in the year 2000.

That in fact is the message delivered by Justice Panganiban halfway across the world — in Chile, where he delivered five speeches before two international conferences: the Global Biotechnology Forum sponsored by the United Nations Industrial Development Organization (UNIDO) and the Government of Chile; and the Judicial Institute on the Biosciences under the auspices of the Supreme Court of Chile and the Einstein Institute for Science, Health and the Courts (EINSHAC). Keynoted by no less than the President of Chile, they were attended by some 800 delegates from 82 countries.

In his main address entitled “The Globalization 1 Justice” (now re-titled “Borderless Justice” in this book), tice Panganiban talked about the preparation judicial-) must make “in resolving bioscientific and biotechnical les that will surely reach the courtrooms within this decade of the twenty-first century.” He urged judges to develop new competencies and update their scientific wedge if they are to cope successfully with the novel challenges of the bio-age. He observed that courts around the world have increasingly been faced with disputes over bro ownership and disposition; and complex questions relating to fertility, abortion, reproductive rights, paternity and heirship. Moreover, people have begun to ask who should have access to the different forms of reproductive technology and — the more urgent ethical question — whether we should clone human beings.

In a parallel forum sponsored by the Chilean Supreme Court and EINSHAC, Justice Panganiban talked about the peculiar difficulties confronting the Philippine justice system in adjudicating bio-cases. He shared with the participants, most of them Chilean judges, the Philippine experience regarding DNA evidence. “Initially Philippine courts were not inclined to accept DNA pro files as evidence because of insufficient knowledge of the technology, lack of laboratory facilities, and contamination of specimens by inexperienced investigators, among others. But with the ruling of the Supreme Court if People v. Vallejo (GR No. 144656, May 9, 2002) affirm the finding of the lower court that the DNA analysis 0f the semen of the accused obtained from the rape victim body matched that of his DNA profile, it is now settle that DNA profiles are admissible as evidence in judicial proceedings.” Just a week ago, the Supreme Court reaffirmed that ruling when it upheld the death penalty of a rapist whose DNA was found on his victim.

Justice Panganiban proposed that common problems brought about by these “paradigm shifts in law should be resolved uniformly by various national tribunals to avoid the evils of forum shopping. On the issue of whether globalization is antithetical to nationalism, he averred, “There is just the need to de-emphasize the concept of sovereignty rooted in artificial borders. National identity is never homogeneous; segments of a country’s population easily find various political, spiritual, cultural, moral or economic values shared with other countries. Such values, however, do not necessarily include the entire national identity.” A good example is the Philippine membership in the World Trade Organization (WTO). In a landmark decision, the Supreme Court ruled that the Senate did not violate the economic nationalism or "Filipino First” provisions of our Constitution when it ratified the multilateral treaty designed to knock down all economic and trade barriers among nations.

The Philippine Ambassador to Chile, Consuelo Puyat-Reyes, confirmed the high regard in which our Supreme Court is held around the world by jurists who have learned and been impressed by its Action Program for Judiciary Reform. Justice Panganiban’s comments and opinions were extensively covered and reported by the media in Latin America, especially by El Sur which featured him on its front page and devoted an entire page to his interview.
