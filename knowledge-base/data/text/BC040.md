# Transparency, Unanimity & Diversity -- Ch. 6: e-Values for the e-Age and Beyond
Date:       2000-11-01
Publisher:  Supreme Court Printing Services
Source:     Transparency, Unanimity & Diversity, Supreme Court Printing Services — ch. 6
By: Artemio V. Panganiban

I thank Dr. Alejandro R. Roces, CAP chairman, for inviting me to join this journey to honor President Sergio Osmena. Truth to tell, however, I believe that the guest speaker today should be Dr. Roces, who has devoted a lifetime teaching the values that make life sublime. I first met him over forty years ago, when he was dean of the Institute of Arts and Sciences of Far Eastern University (FEU), where I was a student of law at the time.

Although I did not have the privilege of being his classroom pupil, nonetheless, my youthful aspirations for a place in society were influenced by his words and example. You see, he was a dominant figure in the FEU where he espoused his ideals of the truly educated person — one bred in the humanities, anchored on values, and moved by principles. In other words, to him the educated person is the cultured person.

Dr. Roces was my benefactor and coach in my several battles for campus leadership. In 1957, I invited him to be adviser of the National Union of Students (the largest organization of students in the Philippines up to now) which, along with a number of others, I ‘organized. As a team, we collaborated to reform student movements in the country. Indeed, I so esteemed Dr. Roces that my wife and I asked him to be our wedding sponsor in 1961. Since then, I have had the honor of calling him Ninong Anding. —

Being my ninong, mentor and friend were not to be his only appellations. He likewise caught the admiration of then President Diosdado Macapagal, who named him his secretary of education. But high office did not end our relationship; he fortified it by appointing me his legal counsel. I shall always be grateful to him for entrusting delicate responsibilities to me as early as then, even if I had just passed the bar examinations during the previous year. I can go on and on reminiscing about Ninong Anding, but I will reserve that for another occasion when, perhaps, the theme of my address would be the life and times of CAP Chair Alejandro R. Roces.

## Fundamental Questions of Being and Living

In the meantime, let me tell you of another interesting man. About six weeks ago, I watched a televised biography of Aristotle Onassis over the Hallmark Entertainment Network, Channel 38 on Sky Cable TV. Onassis, the Greek shipping tycoon, was considered the world’s wealthiest person during the early 1970s. Divorced from his first wife, he later married the world’s best loved and most popular beauty at that time, Jacqueline Kennedy, the widow of the late US President John F. Kennedy. Aside from her, however, he publicly cavorted with several other women, among them the famous Soprano Maria Callas. As a mega-businessman, he brushed shoulders with many heads of state who openly solicited investments and favors from him and his companies. And as a power broker, he enjoyed all the banalities and excitements that possessions, positions and perversions could bring.

In spite of all these worldly trappings, he died a broken man in 1975, because his material treasures and pleasures did not bring him love, peace, fulfillment or a sense of accomplishment that he longed for; he did not find the answers to the fundamental questions that all of us are confronted with; namely, Who am I? Why was I born? Where am I headed for? How do I get there?

Ladies and gentlemen, all of us at one time or another have asked ourselves these basic questions of life, and unless we can satisfy them, we will feel empty, listless and purposeless. Our lives will be endless journeys of ups and downs, but not of vision and direction; of hunger and gluttony, but not of wellness; of drunkenness and lucid intervals, but not of sobriety; of injury and recovery, but not of healing; of trying to be, but never of being; of always chasing carnal pleasures, but never catching lasting joy; and of forever wanting something, but never being fulfilled. It is my purpose today to lead you to some answers to these basic quests. Let me begin with something fundamental.

Humans are rational beings. They are cousins to animals in their needs to procure food and feed themselves, to enjoy sex and procreate, and indeed to exploit nature and obtain sensual pleasures. But all these activities — eating, having sex and obtaining pleasure — merely satisfy their animal and not their rational or divine natures. The stark reality is this: the satisfaction of their ultimate longings depends on the fulfillment of their rationality and divinity, not of their carnality and sensuality.

Stated otherwise, the truth is that as long as we work and live only to eat, to have sex and to enjoy sensual pleasures, as long as we pursue only the things that matter to our carnal nature, we will always be afflicted, broken and crushed. But when we aspire to reach our rational and divine nature, we find ultimate joy, peace and fulfillment. Indeed, what we generally refer to as values are the traits that elevate us from mere existence to life on high, from frustration to satisfaction. They are called values because they are born of man’s higher self, of his valuable rationality and divinity. And more than that, values transform people and make them attain real happiness, greatness, heroism and even sainthood.

What then are these values that we must develop and cultivate? Let me discuss what I consider the most important three: excellence, ethics and eternity.

## Excellence Is Competence, Dedication to Duty, and Hard Work Put Together

The first is excellence. This connotes not merely competence, but also dedication to duty, and hard work put together. It means not only being skillful, but also being able to cultivate our talent or skill to the highest degree possible.

Excellence is not confined to scholarly pursuits. It applies to everything we say and do. God has gifted us with various skills and talents — literary, musical, athletic and artistic. Some of us are teachers, soldiers, or lawyers; others are computer programmers; many are plain ordinary workers. But whatever we may be, our responsibility to ourselves and to one another is to develop to the maximum whatever skill or talent the Lord has given us.

Let me illustrate. Every weekend I try to reward my tired body with a good massage, and I always go to a blind masseur named Larry, because he has mastered the art and knows how to press the right muscle with the right pressure. Whenever I want a haircut, I go to my favorite barber; by the same token, I patronize my old tailor all the time. They are not exactly the cheapest in town, but I go to them anyway and give them generous tips besides. It is not just the monetary payment that gets them going, so says my barber and my tailor, but the esteem I give them because of their expertise. The same can be said of doctors, lawyers, teachers, economists, businessmen and public servants. By becoming excellent in their chosen lines of work, they become self-confident and self-assured. Excellence in everything they say and do becomes their source of self-dignity, self-worth and self-fulfillment.

For the truly educated, excellence demands an understanding and appreciation of the arts, history, literature and the humanities in general, and the mastery of a particular discipline or specialized branch of knowledge. In short, excellence in the humanities and specialization in a career are the earmarks of the educated person.

Hard work characterizes all successful men and women, of ages past and present, in the Philippines and around the world. We all have heard of our own Toribio Teodoro, of India’s Mohandas Gandhi, and of America’s Thomas Edison who said: “Genius is ninety-nine percent perspiration and one percent inspiration.”

In a recent interview with Asiaweek?, Diosdado “Dado” Banatao, 54-year-old native of Iguig, Northern Luzon, who is acclaimed as one of California’s most successful entrepreneurs in the computer industry and who, according to UP President Francisco Nemenzo, is now richer than the venerable Ayala family, revealed his success secret in two words: “hard work.” He further explained, “For every success, there are 100 or 200 failures.” But the success-driven person is not discouraged. He simply plods on and on. He strives even harder with each failure.

Truly, there is no substitute for devotion to excellence; no replacement for hard work and high performance, no alternative to believing in ourselves and in our capacity to ride over tragedy and defeat. Some of us lose heart after a few disappointments and failures: a failed subject in school, a frustrated love affair, a lost job, a missed opportunity, Many times, however, adversities and misfortunes are really preludes to greater victory. Let me illustrate that with a little story.

During the Middle Ages, there lived a farmer, his wife, and their only son who was twenty years old. One day the farmer’s horse ran away to the nearby mountains. So, his neighbors went to him and told: him, “Bad luck for you, your horse is gone. Now you and your family will starve because you can no longer till the fields.” The farmer replied, “Good luck? Bad luck? Who knows? I will just keep on doing my best.” Three days later, the horse returned with nine other wild horses from the mountain. The villagers again trooped to the farmer and said, “Good luck for you. Now you have ten horses.” The farmer replied, “Good luck? Bad luck? Who knows? I will just keep trying my best.” A few days later, the farmer’s only son mounted one of the wild horses which, however, threw him off. Alas, he broke his left leg. Again the villagers told the farmer, “Bad luck for you. With a broken leg, your son is useless in your farm.” The farmer replied, “Good luck? Bad luck? Who knows? I will just keep doing my best!” A week later, the kingdom went to war and the king conscripted all the able-bodied young men in the village except, of course, the disabled son of the farmer. The kingdom lost the war, and all the young men from the village died. And all the villagers again visited the farmer and chorused, “Good luck for you. You are the only villager with a son to inherit your farm.” And the farmer replied, “Good luck? Bad luck? Who knows? I will just keep on doing my best.”

Friends, like our farmer, we are besieged by events outside our control. But let us not be frustrated, let us not give up, let us not be overtaken by them. Let us be like the farmer: no matter what happens, whether good or bad, we will continue doing our best, continue to be excellent, continue to persevere in our good work, continue to believe in ourselves, continue to believe in our ultimate victory.

Let us be like St. Paul who inspired Christian communities with his determination to rise above tragedy and his faith in his ultimate victory. Let me quote from his Second Letter to the Corinthians:

We are afflicted in every way possible, but we are not crushed; Full of doubts, we never despair. We are persecuted but never abandoned; We are struck down but never destroyed.

## Ethics Is Rooted in Integrity, Honesty and Truth

The second value I recommend is ethics which is rooted in personal integrity. By integrity I refer not only to honesty but also to truth. A person of integrity acts in accordance with what is true and honest regardless of personal consequences. To act with integrity is to act with moral courage. This involves not only theoretical bravery in battle but, a priori, a purity of spirit and a clarity of conscience. For only one who is pure of heart and clear of mind can act with integrity.

A good guide on how to act with integrity is our professions’ codes of ethics, or canons as we prefer to call them in the legal profession. These codes are based on “old-fashioned” principles like courage, humility, fidelity, and prudence — basic norms that must be internalized as the primary sources of personal success and enduring happiness.

For government leaders, the ethical standard is contained in Republic Act No. 6713 entitled “An Act Establishing a Code of Conduct and Ethical Standards for Public Officials and Employees x x x.”

This law begins with a declaration of policy that “public officials and employees shall at all times be accountable to the people and shall discharge their duties with utmost responsibility, integrity, competence and loyalty, act with patriotism and justice, lead modest lives and uphold public interest over private interest.”

## Double Standards, Duplicity and Pretension Degrade Self and Society

In our world of shortcuts and make believe, society has bastardized ethics into public relations techniques. Instead of emphasizing the Ethical Standards Law, our so-called leaders display double standards — one for kin and friends, another for the rest of mankind; engage in double speak or talk of one thing and do quite another; and employ so-called “spin doctors” who try to create an image of public acceptability for them without their changing old habits and ways. Hence, they are projected as great orators, when they merely memorize ghost-written speeches they barely understand; as charitable persons giving alms to the poor, when in truth they are merely dishing out crumbs from ill-gotten money; and as model spouses or parents, when all the while they cohabit with several lovers with whom they have several children.

Such duplicity is fundamentally flawed. Instead of being a beacon of success and happiness, pretension is really a source of anxiety and frustration, because the subject knows that the public image is false or dishonest. It does not represent reality. Yes, duplicity is the opposite of integrity. It is a malaise that befuddles the inner fabric of society and is the cause of many national ills. The game of pretend has been used as a substitute for integrity. Asa result, our character as a people has been subverted.

It is interesting to note that more than 90 percent of our people are Christians who profess to know, to follow and to serve Jesus Christ, the God who preached that the truth shall set us free; the God who condemned violence, adultery, selfishness and corruption. Stated in more secular language, we note that our core institutions in the Philippines are theoretically unassailable as they are well-rooted. Thus, our people are devoted to democracy, our media is free, Our non-government groups are militant, our economy liberalized and our judiciary independent.

Why, then, is our country one of the poorest, most graft-ridden and most violent in this part of the world? The Catholic Church’s answer to this question is that Christians lead dichotomized lives: they are Christians only in their thoughts, but not in their actions; Christians only in their faith, but not in their deeds; Christians only while worshipping inside the churches, but not while working in their offices.

In secular language, our leaders live duplicitous lives — lives engendered by public relations techniques, not by ethics; by pretension, not by truth; by deceit, not by integrity. The result is not only personal disillusionment of the individual, but degradation of society as well.

The solution? A return to ethics — to the “old-fashioned” ethical values of integrity, courage, hard work, humility, honor and rectitude. To change society, to change our government, what we need is not just a change of leaders during periodic elections, but a change in leaders from the inside out. The change we are looking for embraces the whole person — head, heart, hands and feet: head, so as to know which traits and habits have to be reformed; heart, so as to have the will and the courage to change; and hands and feet, so as to actually implement the reforms willed.

## Eternity Is our Final Destiny

Finally, my third recommended value is faith in eternity as our final destiny. Never for a moment think that our lives on earth are the only ones we have been gifted with. Rather, believe that there is life after death, that there is eternity where our final destiny lies.

Just as there are human and physical laws, there are also spiritual laws. And just as there are consequences for violations of human and physical laws, there are penalties for the transgression of God's commands. For example, civil laws protect contracts, and a party who breaks them may be liable for damages. In the same manner, one who injures another violates criminal laws and is jailed as a consequence. So, too, a violation of the laws of physics impels consequences; hence, when one jumps out of a window, one falls in accordance with the law of gravity.

By the same token, spiritual laws operate on our spirits, whether we like it or not. When we transgress the Ten Commandments or the Beatitudes proclaimed by our Lord Jesus, our spirits suffer the necessary consequences by being damned in eternity. Just as there are reparations for violations of human and physical laws, there is also salvation from our sins — by repentance, restitution and reformation.

The Ten Commandments are negative directions given to us. They represent what should not be done, like “You shall not have strange gods before me” or “You shall not steal” or “You shall not covet your neighbor’s wife.” On the other hand, the Beatitudes* are our Lord Jesus’ positive pronouncements on what we should be, like being “poor in spirit,” becoming “peacemakers,” or “hungering for holiness.”

I shall not go into details of these commands of the Christian faith, because this address is not about Christianity. In fact, 1am addressing the same plea to our Muslim, Jewish and other God-loving brothers and sisters to follow their own faiths and credos. The point I am driving at is that every religion recognizes an eternal Power greater and mightier than ourselves, who watches over all of us, silently listens to everything we say, and is the quiet witness to everything we do.

In the words of a former president of the Czech Republic, Vaclav Havel, who is regarded as one of the foremost intellectuals of our time, “the whole history of the cosmos, and especially of life, is mysteriously recorded in the inner workings of human beings. This history is projected into man’s creation and is, x x x something that joins us together far more than we think.” He explained, and I quote from him again:

“x x x [S]omewhere in the foundation of most religions and cultures, though they may take a thousand and one forms, [we find] common elements [like] respect for what transcends us, whether we mean the mystery of Being or a moral order that stands above us; certain imperatives that come to us from heaven, or from nature, or from our own hearts, a belief that our deeds will live after us; respect for our neighbors, for our families, for certain natural authorities; for human dignity in general and for nature; and a sense of solidarity and benevolence toward guests who come with good intentions.”°

In other words, humankind is bound together by faith in a Transcendental Being, without which one is bound to forget that one is not God. Without this belief in transcendence, civilizations and cultures will collapse. Humans would be no different from animals which rule by might and not by right.

Let me firmly assure you, ladies and gentlemen: God is present with us today, here and now in this very auditorium. He is amongst us, above us to inspire us, before us to lead us, beside us to guide us, behind us to protect us, and within us — in our hearts and our spirits — to possess us and bring us to His everlasting Kingdom.

My friends, I have discussed thus far what I consider the three most important values in life: excellence, ethics and eternity. I should however add that there is a fourth one, and this is transparency. It is particularly important for government leaders. In public service, there is no such thing as a private deal, no such thing as an under-the-table transaction. The Constitution and the law subject to scrutiny all transactions involving public interest and impose transparency as a desirable standard of public service. But because most of us in this Forum are not public servants, I will not take up transparency any further.

## Epilogue: e-Values Are Immutable

Ladies and gentlemen, let me now put an epilogue to my message today. As we cross to the twenty-first century and the third millennium, civilization is also at the threshold of a new age — the electronic age or simply the e-age. In everything we do, everywhere we go, we encounter the tremendous changes wrought by the e-revolution. The agricultural and the industrial ages are being phased out, and the e-age is here. We now live in a new world — the world of e-commerce, e-health, e-media, e-medicine and even e-law. Truly, the personal computer, the Internet, and the dotcom mania are changing our way of living, relaxing and working.

Even while I welcome the e-age of new technologies and learning, at the same time I advocate the old e-values of excellence, ethics and eternity. Unlike the e-paradigms of the electronic age, these e-values have been with mankind since time began and have worked wonders since then. In this e-age of changing habits and standards, I say that the e-values are timeless. They are still relevant today as they were yesterday. In fact I dare say that they will be with us for all time; indeed, they are the e-values for the e-age and beyond. Let them be our constant and continuing guides on how to attain a life worth living for the here and now, and for the hereafter and forever.

As I end this address, let me go back to its beginning. At the start of this talk, I expressed the hope of being able to help you answer four basic questions: Who am I? Why was I born? Where am I headed for? How do I get there? By this time, you must already know the answers, but I will verbalize them for all of us in this manner:

Who are we? We are all children of one and the same God, one and the same Almighty Eternal Power and Transcendental Being.

Why were we born? We were all born to love, honor and obey Him, who created us in His own image. Where are we headed for? We are all headed for a reunion with Him, our Lord and Creator. And how do we get there? We will all reach our destiny by believing, internalizing and practising daily, throughout our temporal lives on earth — at home, in

our offices, in our leisure, everywhere and all the time — the e-values of excellence, ethics and eternity.

I thank you.
