# Reforming the Judiciary -- Ch. 12: What I Learned at Far Eastern University
Date:       2002-12-01
Publisher:  Supreme Court Printing Press
Source:     Reforming the Judiciary, Supreme Court Printing Press — ch. 12 (batch-04 split)
By: Artemio V. Panganiban

I thank Dr. Edilberto C. de Jesus, president of Far Eastern University (FEU), for inviting me as your graduation speaker. May I also say “Salamat po” to the entire FEU community, led by Dr. Lourdes Reyes Montinola, chair of the FEU board of trustees, for conferring upon me today the degree of Doctor of Laws, honoris causa.

I received my first doctoral degree - also honoris causa - five years ago in 1997 from the University of Iloilo. Now, with this second doctorate, I hope that, finally, my wife Leni who is present today - and who is a professor and former associate dean at the Asian Institute of Management, as President de Jesus used to be - can equate my academic achievements with those of our only son, Jose Artemio, who has two PhDs from  Stanford University. Heretofore, I had been suffering from a venial inferiority complex, because I was the only one in my family who did not have a graduate degree. My wife has two master's degrees and our five children — four girls and one boy — all obtained graduate diplomas in Ivy League schools in the United States, including Harvard and Stanford. But thanks to Dr. Montinola and to Dr. de Jesus, I can now be the academic equal of my wife and children.

## Graduation Memories

Levity aside, this commencement ceremony brings back poignant memories of my own graduation from the FEU Institute of Law 42 years ago in 1960. As some of you may know, I come from a very poor family. A mere high school graduate, my father was an underpaid but overworked government employee. With his meager income, he had to look after not only his four children of whom I was the youngest, but also his seven siblings who looked up to him for paternal help after their own father had died at an early age. In taking care of his brothers and sisters, my Tatay, the eldest child, took on the responsibilities of his own father.

Even if he did not finish a college degree, he emphasized to his children and siblings that education was the key to success and fulfillment in life. True enough, all his children finished their college courses but, alas, I, his youngest son, nearly did not graduate because he died even before I completed my preparatory law course.

But FEU came to my rescue and provided me with a scholarship. To further support myself, I sold bibles to my professors and textbooks to my classmates so I could own a copy as my commission. My eldest sister and only brother, who were already employed when my father died, provided me shelter and meals. But tragedy struck again when my mother died during my senior year at law school.

That is why I congratulate all of you, dear graduates. I know that, like me, most of you come from poor struggling families who place their hopes in their FEU education. Since its founding as the Institute of Accounting in 1928 and as a university in 1933, FEU has always provided "relevant education to men and women at a cost within the reach of the middle and working class." I also felicitate all the parents here who I know labored long nights and hard days to help their children finish their education. Fellow graduates, you are lucky to have parents who are still alive to share your joy today — unlike me whose parents died before my graduation.

During the formal conferment ceremony of my doctoral degree, Atty. Gianna R. Montinola recited, to my discomfort, a rather complimentary presentation. While I am thankful for such generosity, I must stress that I could not have been where I am today, enjoying such a heart-warming accolade and receiving an honorary doctorate, without the basic education and the value formation I learned at Far Eastern University. Therefore, I would like to recall and relate to you, as my theme today, what I as a student imbibed at FEU. What, indeed, did our alma mater teach me? Basically, four things:

1. A desire for academic excellence

2. A craving for a well-rounded education

3. A search for my Catholic faith

4. A hunger for leadership

## Academic Excellence

Let me discuss each of these. First, a desire for academic excellence. Truth to tell, I originally had no intention of studying at FEU. I wanted to enroll at the University of the Philippines, where most of my classmates at Mapa High School were going. While UP granted me a tuition scholarship, I could not study there because I could not afford the then 15-centavo bus fare from our apartment in Cataluña Street, Sampaloc, Manila; to Diliman, Quezon City, where the UP campus was located. So, at the behest of my parents, I grudgingly enrolled at FEU which was a walking distance from our home.

Initially, I felt disgruntled because I was enrolling in a school I had not chosen. However, I must confess that from my Day One at FEU, I had been intellectually challenged. I found my professors unusually strict and demanding. That was why I had to study well to keep my scholarship. In my preparatory law course, I remember very well Arts and Sciences Dean Francisco M. Africa who was my professor in argumentation and debate, Dr. Benito F. Reyes who expounded on philosophy, Prof. Angelica P. Mendoza who explained botany, and Prof. Rosario P. Melchor who taught me English. Up to now, more than four decades later, I still remember and use the basic knowledge they imparted to me. I still use the elementary rules of logic I learned from Dean Africa and those of basic grammar from Prof. Melchor.

In the Institute of Law, Dean Jovito R. Salonga became my role model in pursuing my legal career. From him I acquired not only UP-style education but Harvard and Yale techniques as well, because he taught his students what he learned from all these three schools where he had finished his bachelor's, master's and doctoral degrees.

As a student, I was very active in extracurricular activities, having become president of the FEU Central Student Organization (FEUCSO) and of the National Union of Students of the Philippines (NUSP). Because of these activities, I tended to give secondary importance to my academic studies. But Dr. Salonga constantly reminded m e that my first duty was to excel in academics.

As my professor in several subjects, he would call on me to recite when student activities were at their height — perhaps to test whether I was adhering to my promise never to forget my academic preparation in the midst of adulation for my leadership activities. Because of his constant monitoring of my grades, I managed to graduate with honors and to be among the bar examination topnotchers.

Dr. Salonga also recruited into the law faculty the best and the brightest lawyers like Neptali A. Gonzales, who later became Senate President; Edgardo L. Paras and Salvador Esguerra, who both later became Supreme Court justices; Arsenio Solidum, Antonio Lucero, Luis . Reyes, Angel Mojica and Jose Leuterio, who all ended B up in the Court of Appeals; plus young Ivy League graduates like Juan Ponce Enrile, Manuel Montecillo, Reynaldo Alejandro and Augusto Caesar Espiritu. All of them demanded excellence, diligence and hard work from their students. Small wonder, during my college days, FEU law graduates always landed among the topnotchers in the annual bar examinations.

Indeed, my professors both in the Institute of Law and in the Institute of Arts and Sciences required my best performance all the time. The excellence I learned from FEU has made me self-confident and self-assured. It is now my source of self-dignity, self-worth and self-fulfillment.

## Well-Rounded Education

Let me now explain how I imbibed a craving for a well-rounded education. While I was a law student, a new dean of the Institute of Arts and Sciences was appointed: D.r Alejandro R. Roces. He was young, outspoken, intelligent and dynamic. More importantly, he stressed the need for liberal education. Upon assuming office, he declared: "My purpose is to produce capable and cultured men and women - individuals who are human beings before they are doctors, lawyers and n in the accountants. If I can make them moral and capable individuals, they will become moral and capable practitioners of their professions."

Tirelessly, he espoused his ideals of a truly educated person — one bred in the humanities, anchored on values, and moved by principles. His battlecry was "Education for Culture." Under his leadership, FEU became the center for the arts, drama, music, folk dance, folklore and literature. I was so impressed that I cultivated his friendship and asked him to be my adviser when I was elected president of the National Union of Students of the Philippines. Eventually, he became our Ninong when Leni and I got married in 1961.

In no time, the dynamism and the fame of Dean Roces overflowed into the national consciousness — he was recruited into then President Diosdado Macapagal's cabinet as the youngest person to be named secretary of education.

The then President of FEU, Dr. Teodoro Evangelista, was himself outstanding and single-minded in his educational policies. He believed that "education in the right sense of the word aims to prepare for work, not as an end in itself but as a means to the enjoyment of life of the mind and spirit."

In brief, I learned from FEU that it was not enough to be an expert in one's profession; one should also have a more than passing understanding of the arts, history, literature, ethics and the humanities in general. Indeed, I was taught that excellence in liberal education and specialization in a career were the marks of the truly educated person.

Translated into legal lore, this educational philosophy means that it is no longer enough for judges and lawyers to be walking encyclopedias of the Constitution, the codes and judicial doctrines. It is essential that they must also have working knowledge of the humanities, economics, biotechnology, accounting, medicine, world history, computers, telecommunications, digital sciences, mathematics and physics. Indeed, excellence in law is not enough; a lawyer must also have a good grasp of the arts and sciences.

Because of this realization early on, I have continued my search for knowledge and wisdom in all branches of human endeavor. This has given me, as a member of the Supreme Court, the confidence to discuss and decide litigations of all types, even those involving avant-garde sciences, new economic paradigms and ultramodern technology.

## Fundamentals of Catholicism

The third important matter I learned from FEU was my Catholic faith. You see, I was a product of public elementary and high schools where religion was not taught. While I was baptized a Catholic, I did not really know my faith. In short, I had no catechetical background.

True, FEU prides itself in being nonsectarian. But during my FEU days, I met the brilliant and compassionate Fr. Michael Nolan, the then FEU chaplain, under whose watch the FEU chapel was built. He took a personal interest in me and in what I did, recruited me into the FEU Student Catholic Action and taught me the rudiments of Catholicism. In short, he introduced me to God.

In FEU, I encountered another engaging Columban priest, Fr. James Sheehy, who insisted that a good Catholic must not only be personally pure, but must also lead others to purity; in short, be a Christ for others. That was enough for me to make further efforts to learn g y, and experience my faith. My initial contact with religion in FEU impelled me to search for God more deeply and passionately all my life.

Now I know and I believe. Just as there are human and physical laws, there are also spiritual laws. And just as there are consequences for the violations of human and physical laws, there are penalties for the violations of God's commands. For example, civil laws protect contracts, and a person who breaks them may be liable for damages. In the same manner, one who injures another violates criminal laws and is jailed as a consequence. So, too, a violation of physical rules impels consequences. When one jumps out of a window, one falls in accordance with the law of gravity.

By the same token, spiritual laws operate in our spirits, whether we like it or not. When we transgress the Ten Commandments or the Beatitudes proclaimed by our Lord Jesus Christ, our spirits suffer the inevitable consequences by being damned in eternity. Just as there are reparations for violations of human and physical laws, there is also salvation from our sins by repentance, restitution and reformation.

Let me firmly assure you, fellow graduates, God is present with us today, here and now in this very plenary hall. He is amongst us to celebrate our graduation, above us to inspire us, before us to lead us, beside us to guide us, behind us to protect us, and within us — in our hearts and our spirits — to possess us and bring us to his everlasting kingdom.

## Hunger for Leadership

The fourth important thing I learned from FEU was a hunger for leadership, for activism. When I was a young boy, I used to be very shy and timid. I kept mostly to myself. That was why my high school classmates who knew me as an introvert were surprised to learn that I was elected president of the FEU Central Student Organization (FEUCSO) during my sophomore year; and then became co-founder and president of the National Union of Students of the Philippines (NUSP), the largest student movement in our country. This was because FEU had a huge student population who looked up to me as their leader. Equally important, its owners and administrators encouraged student leadership.

I fondly remember Don Angel Palanca, the then chairman of the board of trustees, who took a liking for me. Though he was wealthy, Don Angel had a genuine concern for the poor students in his school. He personified decency, good breeding and right manners. It is indeed appropriate that, in his honor, the Peace Education program was included in the academic curriculum. I also remember Mr. Hector del Rosario - "Mang Etoy" as we affectionately called him —the FEU treasurer, who always lent me an ear whenever I asked him to assist the FEUCSO with its financial concerns.

With these four things I learned at FEU - academic excellence, well-rounded education, Catholic faith and leadership — I realized early on that I was the equal of the UP, the Ateneo or the La Salle student, and that I could in fact compare and compete favorably with the best and the brightest from any school in the country and in the world.

## Present FEU Policies

In preparing my address today, I must admit that I researched the present milieu in FEU. After a careful study, I am fully convinced that the present owners, administrators and leaders of our alma mater have continued —and, I daresay, have even tried to improve on — policies, traditions and teachings of the FEU I knew.

On March 9, 1996, during his formal installation as president of this great university, Dr. de Jesus declared that he was continuing the excellent policies of his predecessors. In fact, he went even deeper and culled from the vision of FEU's founder, Dr. Nicanor Reyes Sr., in pledging to work for "a committed and conscientious faculty," whose performance will be measured "not in terms of the information they unload on the students but on the critical thinking they provoke among them." Indeed, it is what President de Jesus calls the faculty's ability to teach students "to learn on their own, the capacity for continuous learning," on which the greatness of this university and the success of its alumni will depend.

Already, President de Jesus has revived the FEU auditorium as the center for the performing arts in our part of the country. He is slowly restoring FEU's prime role in advancing the country's cultural development. In the Institute of Law, I know that Dean Andres Bautista has begun his program to upgrade the standards of legal education. In connection with this program, I am proud to inform you that the championship in the nationwide essay writing contest sponsored by the Supreme Court last year was won by an FEU student, Margarita Eugenia F. Victorino.

And so long as we have dedicated and selfless benefactors - like Dr. Lourdes Reyes Montinola — supporting these benevolent policies, I know that FEU graduates, like all of us today, will always be luminous stars in the galaxy of successful men and women in our Republic.

## Epilogue

As I close my address, let me recapitulate. I learned four things at FEU: academic excellence, well-rounded education, Catholic faith and leadership. I will now summarize these four lessons in one teaching, which I would like to leave to you as my final message. Indeed, if my presence today and my 20-minute address should have any relevance at all, it is this one-sentence summary of what I learned at FEU, as articulated by my most esteemed mentor, Dean Jovito R. Salonga: While it is desirable to acquire the many necessities that money can buy — like adequate food on the table, sufficient clothing and shelter for the family, sports and recreation for the body, travel and leisure for the spirit and even a car and a house for our comfort - never we ever forget that it is far more important to aspire for the things that money cannot buy, like integrity, honor, dignity and a reverential regard and love for God as the source of all that is true, good and beautiful. I thank you.
