# Transparency, Unanimity & Diversity -- Ch. 22: Firestone Ceramics v. Court of Appeals: Sauce for the Poor Goose and the Rich Gander
Date:       2000-11-01
Publisher:  Supreme Court Printing Services
Source:     Transparency, Unanimity & Diversity, Supreme Court Printing Services — ch. 22
By: Artemio V. Panganiban

The Constitution, in Article VIII of Section 4, expressly authorizes the Supreme Court to “sit en banc or, in its discretion, in divisions of three, five, or seven Members.” Pursuant to this mandate, the present Court has opted to sit en banc and in three Divisions composed of five members each. The Chief Justice presides over the Banc and over one Division. The two most senior justices! -- _ those who have served the Court longest among the remaining fourteen -- chair the two other Divisions. Each of the remaining twelve, in alternating seniority, is assigned a membership in one of the three Divisions.

The Banc or full Court of 15 members and each Division of 5 deliberate on and decide cases independently of each other. Decisions, whether en banc or in division, are coequal judgments of the Supreme Court of the Philippines. The full Court of fifteen is not an appellate body that reviews, modifies or reverses the Divisions. Subject to the constitutional caveat that “no doctrine or principle of law laid down in a decision rendered en banc or in division may be modified or reversed except by the court sitting en banc,” a decision of a Division is as good a judgment as one rendered by the full Court. In this sense, some people say that there are really four Supreme Courts: the Banc and the three Divisions. Indeed, there have been complaints that sometimes the Divisions promulgate decisions that allegedly conflict with one another.

Further, the Constitution, in Article VIII of Section 4 (2), enumerates the instances which must be heard and decided en banc:

1. Those “involving the constitutionality of a treaty, international or executive agreement, or law”

2. “[AJll other cases which under the Rules of Court are required to be heard en banc, including those involving the constitutionality, application, or operation of presidential decrees, proclamations, orders, instructions, ordinances, and other regulations”

By implication, the cases not included in the above enumeration may be heard and resolved by a Division. Indeed, a vast majority of cases are decided by the Divisions more than by the Banc. Statistics in the Annual Reports of the Supreme Court show that about 85 to 90 percent of all Supreme Court decisions are rendered by the Divisions and only the remaining 10-15 percent, by the Banc.

In actual practice, how are cases assigned to the Banc and to the Divisions? As soon as a petition or an appeal is filed, the SC’s Raffle Committee immediately peruses it and makes a determination as to whether, on the basis of the issues raised, the case should go to the Banc. Such determination is preliminary in nature, and the Division to which the case has initially been raffled may still refer it to the banc.

Referral to the Banc of cases previously assigned to a Division is governed by Circular 2-89, as amended by a full Court Resolution dated November 18, 1993, in which the following “were considered to be en banc cases”:

“1. Cases in which the constitutionality or validity of any treaty, international or executive agreement, law, executive order, or presidential decree, proclamation, order, instruction, ordinance, or regulation is in question;

2. Criminal cases in which the appealed decision imposes the death penalty;

3. Cases raising novel questions of law;

4. Cases affecting ambassadors, other public ministers and consuls;

5. Cases involving decisions, resolutions or orders of the Civil Service Commission, Commission on Elections, and Commission on Audit;

6. Cases where the penalty to be imposed is the dismissal of a judge, officer or employee of the judiciary, disbarment of a lawyer, or either the suspension of any of them for a period of more than one (1) year or a fine exceeding P10,000 or both;

7. Cases where a doctrine or principle laid down by the court en banc or in division may be modified or reversed;

8. Cases assigned to a division which in the opinion of at least three (3) members thereof merit the attention of the court en banc and are acceptable to a majority of the actual membership of the court en banc; and

9, All other cases as the court en banc by a majority of its actual membership may deem of sufficient importance to merit its attention.”

With the foregoing guidelines, notably Item 8, the Banc has routinely taken over cases referred to it by at least three members of a Division.

’ The application of Circular 2-89, as amended, was put to a test in Firestone Ceramics v. Court of Appeals? and its companion, Republic v. Court of Appeals. These cases were consolidated, raffled to and decided by the Third Division? on September.2, 1999. In a ponencia written by Justice Minerva P. Gonzaga-Reyes, the Division unanimously denied the Petitions and held that the subject land belonged to private respondents.

Thereafter, both the government and the private petitioners filed separate (1) Motions for Reconsideration and (2) Motions to Refer the Cases to the Court en banc.

By a vote of 4-1, with Justice Fidel P. Purisima casting the lone negative vote, the Third Division rejected the Motion to Refer the Cases to the full Court, because the movants had utterly failed to adduce any legal reason for such referral. Subsequently, at the instance of Justice Purisima, the Court en banc, in a Resolution dated June 28, 2000, decided to yank the cases out of and against the will of the said Division, and “treated [them] as en banc cases.” The Banc cited paragraph 9 of Circular 2-89 and justified its extraordinary takeover in this wise:

1. “The cases at bar involve a vast tract of land with an area of about 99 hectares x x x which ha[s] been adjudicated to private individuals by a court alleged to be without jurisdiction.” The ponencia of Justice Reyes allegedly “does not indicate the classification of the land in question x x x.”

2. The “subject cases are of sufficient importance meriting the action and decision of the whole Court.”

Eight justices (CJ Davide Jr.; JJ Bellosillo, Kapunan, Mendoza, Purisima, Buena, Santiago and De Leon) voted for the Purisima proposal and seven (JJ Melo, Puno, Vitug, Panganiban, Quisumbing, Pardo and Reyes) voted against it. Justice Reyes, author of the assailed Third Division Decision, wrote the main Dissenting Opinion. She pointed out the following:

1. “The fact alone that the property involved covers an area of 99 hectares does not provide a cogent: reason to elevate the cases to the Court en banc. Nowhere in the extant guidelines for referral to the Court en banc is the value of the property subject of the case relevant to determine whether the Division should refer a matter to the Court en banc. Moreover, the validity of OCT No. 4216, which petitioner Republic raised as a principal issue in the instant Petition, had already been long settled by final judgments of this Court in three (3) cases.”

2. “The Motion for Reconsideration [of] the Decision unanimously adopted by the Third Division on September 2, 1999 is still pending. If there is any error to be rectified in the said Decision, the matter should be left to the sound judgment of the members of the Division which promulgated the Decision unless there is demonstrated incapacity or disqualification on the part of its members to render a fair and just resolution of the Motion for Reconsideration.”

- 3. The Court en banc is not an appellate court to which a judgment or a decision may be appealed. A decision of a Division is a decision of the Court itself. A Division is not inferior but coequal to the Banc.

4, The “Resolution dated November 18, 1993 is essentially an amendment to Sections 15 and 16, Rule 136 of the Rules of Court which deals with the form (‘unglazed paper,’ margins, number of copies, etc.) of unprinted and printed papers to be filed with this Court. This Resolution was clearly not intended to lay down new guidelines or rules for referral to the Court en banc of cases [previously] assigned to a Division. Thus, the principle that the Court en banc is not an appellate court to which decisions or resolutions of a Division may be appealed could not have been intended to be abrogated. Article VIII, Section 4 of the Constitution, earlier quoted, expressly provides that ‘when the required number (the concurrence of at least three members of the Division) is not obtained, the case shall be decided en banc.’ The obvious contemplation is that when the required vote of at least three members is obtained, the banc’s participation is not called for.”

5. “The Court en banc should be shielded from the importunings of litigants who perceive themselves aggrieved by a decision of a Division of the court and resort to the convenience of an appeal to the Court en banc on the plea that its case is ‘of sufficient importance to merit its attention.’”

Justice Reynato S. Puno and I rendered separate Dissents. In his one-page Opinion, J. Puno said that inasmuch as the Third Division “intends to hear the Motion for Reconsideration filed by the Republic,” then the “Court en banc should defer its actions over its action to assume jurisdiction over the case at bar.”

For my part, I argued: “[W]ith due respect, the majority has not given any cogent or compelling reason” for its unprecedented takeover. “Its Resolution, penned by Justice Purisima, simply pontificates that ‘these consolidated cases are of sufficient importance to merit the attention and disposition of the entire Court’ without stating why. x x x. If at all, the lame excuse given that the ‘subject Decision [promulgated by the Third Division] does not clearly indicate the classification of the land’ is merely an argument why the pending Motion for Reconsideration should be granted, not why the banc should take over this case.”

But what specially disturbed and saddened me was the preferential treatment the Court accorded the private petitioners here, as compared with that given to the farmers who were petitioners in Fortich v.

Corona.* Let me quote my anguish in what I believed was a “double standard” of justice tilted against the poor:

“In the celebrated Sumilao farmer’s case, a similar motion to refer to the full Court was turned down by the Second Division by a vote of 3-1. Arguing that the Division’s earlier vote of 2-2 on the Motion for Reconsideration was not decisive, Justice Jose A.R. Melo (who was then a member of the Second Division) subsequently asked the banc to take over the case. Justice Melo argued that under Article VIII, Section 4(3) of the Constitution, ‘[c]ases or matters heard by a division shall be decided or resolved with the concurrence of a majority of the members who actually took part in the deliberations on the issues in the case and voted thereon, and in no case without the concurrence of at least three of such members.’ Since the Motion for Reconsideration did not obtain the required three votes in the Division, he added that the banc should thus take over and resolve the impasse. In other words, Justice Melo presented a genuine ‘question of sufficient importance’ -- which the Second Division was not in a position to resolve -- to justify a takeover by the banc. Yet, the full Court turned down his proposal. Only Justice Vitug and I supported Justice Melo.

“T therefore cannot understand why the banc is now taking over this case against the wishes of the Third Division. It turned down

* GRNo. 131457, April 24, 1998, November 17, 1998 and August 19, 1999, the poor farmers’ plea and the Melo proposal. Why then should the banc grant the not-so-poor private petitioners’ prayer here? Why then should it approve the groundless Purisima proposal? At the very least, if it should take over this case, then it should likewise assume jurisdiction over the farmer’s suit. After all, the vote in the Motion for Reconsideration in that case was two in favor and two against, while in the present case, the Third Division has not even voted on the plea for reconsideration. In other words, there was sufficient reason for the banc to take over the Sumilao problem because of the 2-2 vote of the Division. Here, no cogent reason whatsoever -other than the motherhood peroration that the case was ‘of sufficient importance’ -- is given by the majority.”

I concluded my Dissent with a clarion call for equal justice for the “poor goose” and “the rich gander” in this language:

“Having taken over this case, the banc -- in the name of equal justice -- should also take over the Sumilao farmers’ Petition. But having rejected their case, then it should also turn down this one. Sauce for the poor goose should be the same sauce for the rich gander. That is simple, equal justice for all.”
