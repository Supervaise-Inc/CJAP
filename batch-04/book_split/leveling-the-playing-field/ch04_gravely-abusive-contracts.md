# Chapter 4: Gravely Abusive Contracts

On September 11, 2003, several business leaders from the United States comprising the US-ASEAN Business Council paid a courtesy call on the Supreme Court. Chief Justice Hilario G. Davide Jr. requested three members of the Court to receive them formally: I, as chairman of the Court’s Committee on Public Information; as well as two other members of the Committee, Justices Antonio T. Carpio and Adolfo S. Azcuna.

Explaining during an open forum that they were planning to invest in the country, the visiting Americans asked what advice we could give them to safeguard their investments.

I replied that, normally, justices do not render advisory opinions. Under the adversarial system of dispensing justice prevailing in the Philippines — and in many other countries including the United States — judges are passive participants in a controversy. They speak mainly through their decisions that are rendered only af-‘ter meticulously hearing the parties. They recite the precise facts in litigations and the specific law applied to them. Hence, these decisions must always be understood in the context of their peculiar facts.

## Contracts Tainted with Grave Abuse

I realize that the question of our American visitor, while couched in polite general terms, is reflective of a frequent criticism from some businessmen that the Supreme Court is interfering inordinately in economic matters, specifically in nullifying billion-peso contracts entered into by the government. For this reason, I was requested to talk on this subject’ by your president, Rene Y. Soriano; and the chairman of the Organizing Committee for the National Conference of Employers of the Employers Confederation of the Philippines (ECOP), Feliciano L. Torres.

Specifically, I have been asked to focus on a number of recent Decisions, in which the Supreme Court voided the contracts entered into by the following companies:

1. The Amari Coastal Bay and Development Corporation (AMARI), for the reclamation, development and purchase of a portion of Manila Bay”

2. The Philippine International Air Terminals Company, Inc. (PIATCO), for the construction and operation of Ninoy Aquino Airport Terminal III (NAIA Terminal III)’

3. The Mega Pacific eSolutions, Inc. (MEGA PACIFIC), for the supply of computer hardware and software for automating the counting and canvassing of votes for the 2004 elections’

4. The Western Mining Company, Philippines, Inc. (WMC), for financial and technical assistance in the development and utilization of several mining claims in Mindanao’

In this address, permit me to omit a discussion of the fourth item that involves the constitutionality of portions of the Mining Law of 1995, because this case is sull sub judice, a Motion for Reconsideration is still pending. Having filed the main Dissenting Opinion in this case, I should at this time refrain from commenting on the Decision of the majority of my colleagues until the Motion for Reconsideration will have finally been disposed of.

Let me then discuss the first three landmark Decisions popularly known as the AMARI, the PIATCO and the MEGA PACIFIC cases. These three litigations, which were brought directly before the Supreme Court, invoked its extraordinary of certiorari power to void the Contract that had allegedly been entered into by the government with grave abuse of discretion.

I should stress that the Constitution of the Philippines has given the judicial branch of government the duty — not just the power or authority — to strike down any act of any agency or instrumentality of government, including the Presidency and Congress, if such act is tainted with grave abuse. Once a government action — he it an order, a directive or a contract — is shown to have been abused gravely, the courts have no choice but (0 invalidate it as a matter of constitutional duty.

In its simplest terms, “there is grave abuse of discretion (1) when an act is done contrary to the Constitution, the law or jurisprudence; or (2) when it is executed whimsically, capriciously or arbitrarily out of mal-

After hearing the parties and after meticulous deliberation, the Supreme Court concluded that these three gargantuan Contracts had all been entered into with grave abuse of discretion by the government agencies and officials concerned and were thus void and deemed nonexistent in law.

Let us examine each of these Decisions and find out exactly how and where grave abuse of discretion lies.

## The AMARI Contract

In 1995, AMARI entered into a Joint Venture Agreement (JVA) with the Public Estates Authority (PEA) for the development and the sale of three reclaimed islands on Manila Bay. These were known as the “Freedom Islands,” which comprised an area of 157 hectares. Further, the JVA required AMARI to reclaim and purchase an additional 250 hectares of submerged lands surrounding these islands.

In 1998, Francisco Chavez — a former solicitor general — brought suit before the Supreme Court to nullify the JVA. The threshold issue of the Petition was “whether AMARI, a private corporation, can acquire and own reclaimed and submerged foreshore land in Manila Bay.”

In a unanimous 13-0 vote, the Court, speaking through Justice Antonio T. Carpio, answered with a resounding “No.” Reclaimed lands are “alienable lands of the public domain” which, under the Constitution, may be leased — but not sold — to private corporations. Subject to certain constitutional limitations especially as to area, reclaimed land may be purchased by Filipino individuals. Submerged areas, on the other hand, are inalienable natural resources and are outside the commerce of man. The government, through the Public Estates Authority, may reclaim these submerged areas, however. (Once reclaimed, the assets may be classified by the government as alienable or disposable agricultural lands of the public domain; thereafter, they may be leased to private corporations.

In a subsequent Resolution dated May 6, 2003, the Court clarified that “reclaimed alienable lands of the public domain if sold or transferred to a public or municipal corporation for a monetary consideration become patrimonial property x x x [which] may be sold x x x to private parties, whether Filipino citizens or qualified corporations.” This clarification was welcomed by the business community, because it quieted the anxieties of many companies that had bought reclaimed real estate from Pasay City, the local government that had territorial jurisdiction over the questioned Manila Bay reclamation project.”

In a later Resolution dated November 11, 2003,"° the Court — citing the investigation reports of two Senate committees — said that it could not legitimize the AMARI Contract, because it had “conveyed to a private entity 157.84 hectares of reclaimed public lands along Roxas Boulevard in Metro Manila at a negotiated price of P1,200 per square meter, x x x [considering that] the market price of land near that area at that time [was] P90,000 per square meter. The difference in price is a staggering P140.16 billion, equivalent to the budget of the entire judiciary for seventeen years and more than three times the Marcos Swiss deposits that this Court forfeited in favor of the government.” With this revelation, the Court wrote finis to the “grandmother of all scams.”

## The PIATCO Contract

Let me now turn to the second Contract. In 1997, after a public bidding conducted by the Manila International Airport Authority (MIAA), PIATCO was awarded a “Concession Agreement” (CA) for the construction and the operation of Ninoy Aquino International Airport Terminal III under the Build Operate and ‘Transfer (BOT) Law (RA No. 7718). Thereafter, the Concession Agreement was amended without any public bidding in 1998. Subsequently, three Supplements to the CA were signed in 1999, 2000 and 2001, also without any public bidding, Brought directly before the Supreme Court in “002 were suits challenging the validity of the original (A, the amended CA and the three Supplements.

In a Decision written’ by Justice Reynato S. Puno, the Court — by a vote of 10-3-1 — ruled that the government (specifically the Manila International Airport Authority or MIAA) gravely abused its discretion in entering into these PIATCO Contracts for the following reasons:

1. MIAA violated its own bidding rules when it awarded the original CA to an entity that had failed to inept the financial requirement of the rules.

2. The Amended Concession Agreement signed by MIAA and PIATCO substantially differed from the Agreement that the bidding rules said would be awarded to the winning bidder. In other words, the CA nullified the “fair competition essence” of public bidding by granting more benefits to the winner than that which had been offered for bidding. The benefits given to PIATCO “alter[ed] the basic parameters of the contract and constitut[ed] a denial to the other bidders of the opportunity to bid under the same terms.” The “executed contract” was “entirely different from the one that was bidded upon” and gave PIATCO “more favorable terms than what was available to other bidders at the time the contract was bidded out.”

3. The CA obligated the government to pay PIATCO's creditors “in the event that PIATCO defaults in its loan obligations,” in violation of the BOT Law proscribing “government guarantees in any form.”

4. The CA likewise obligated the government — when forced by national emergencies to take over Terminal III — to pay PIATCO, contravening Section 17 of Article XII of the Constitution.”

5. In granting PIATCO the “exclusive right to operate a commercial international passenger terminal within the Island of Luzon,” the CA transgressed the constitutional prohibition against monopolies, as such grant would have compelled the closure of NAIA Terminals I and II.

In its subsequent Resolution dated January 21, 2004, the Court stressed that a public bidding was to be conducted strictly in accordance with the “prescribed terms, conditions and parameters laid down by government.” Once an award is made, “all that is left to be done by the parties is to execute the necessary agreements and implement them.” No material changes to the parameters can thereafter be allowed. Otherwise, “public bidding becomes a mockery and the modified contract must be struck down.”

## The MEGA PACIFIC Contract

Let me now take up the third and final Contract that we agreed to discuss today. This Contract was awarded by the Commission on Elections (Comelec) to MEGA PACIFIC for the supply of automated counting machines (ACM) intended to be used during the 2004 election.

Voting 9-5," the Court — in a Decision I had the honor of writing — invalidated the Contract, because it had been awarded in violation of law and jurisprudence; in “reckless disregard" of Comelec’s bidding rules and procedure; and “in inexplicable haste, without adequately checking and observing mandatory financial, technical and legal requirements.” Let me explain these pronouncements further.

First, by a formal Resolution, Comelec awarded the Contract to “Mega Pacific Consortium,” an entity that had not participated in the bidding. Despite this grant, the poll body signed the actual automation agreement with “Mega Pacific Solutions, Inc.,” a company that had joined the bidding, but had not met the eligibility requirements.

Second, the automated counting machines (ACMs) proffered by MEGA PACIFIC flunked the technical tests conducted by the Department of Science and Technology (DOST). Specifically, the ACMs failed to meet the accuracy requirement of 99.9995 percent set up by the Comelec bidding rules. Acknowledging that this rating could have been too steep, the Court nonetheless observed that “the essence of public bidding is violated by the practice of requiring very high standards or unrealistic specifications that cannot be met, x x x only to water them down after the award is made. Such scheme, which discourages the entry of bona fide bidders, is in fact a sure indication of fraud in the bidding, designed to eliminate fair competition.”

Furthermore, the ACMs failed to meet another key requirement — “for the counting machines’ software program to be able to detect previously downloaded precinct results and to prevent these from being entered again into the counting machines.” Had the Supreme Court ignored this failure and validated the Contract, unscrupulous persons would be able to repeatedly download and feed into the computers the results favorable to a particular candidate. Thus, “election fraud could be perpetrated on a massive scale by means of just a few key strokes.”

Also, the DOST tests revealed that the machines were unable to print the audit trail without loss of data. “This particular deficiency is significant, not only to the bidding, but to the cause of free and credible elections. The purpose of requiring audit trails is to enable Comelec to trace and verify the identities of the ACM operators responsible for data entry and downloading, as well as the times when the various data were downloaded into the canvassing system, in order to forestall fraud and to identify the perpetrators.”

Third, aggravating these failures to comply with bid requirements was the inability of Comelec to explain these deficiencies, and its dismaying admission that “the software used by Comelec was merely a ‘demo’ version inasmuch as the final version that would actually be used in the elections was still being developed and had not been finalized.”

If that were so, why then were the ACMs brought in and paid for “when there is as yet no way of knowing if the final version of the software would be able to run them properly” and assure an orderly and accurate count? Even more clearly, “Comelec awarded the Contract without having seen, much less evaluated, the final product being purchased.”

After noting these violations by Comelec of its own bidding rules, the Court asked, “What then was the point in awarding the bid when the software that was the subject of the Contract was still to be created by the winner?”

The Court ruled that by awarding the bid and later on allowing the winner to alter substantially the latter's bid without any public bidding, Comelec desecrated the law on public bidding.

As an epilogue to this Decision, the Court noted that the MEGA PACIFIC case involved not merely the invalidation of a business contract, but “the ability and capacity of the Commission on Elections to perform properly, legally and prudently its legal mandate to implement the transition from manual to automated election”} and in the final analysis to conduct honest, orderly and credible elections. In short, it voided the MEGA PACIFIC Contract, not merely because it restrained free trade and “desecrated legal and jurisprudential norms” but, even more important, because of the “illegal and abusive acts of Comelec”. put in jeopardy the “pith and soul of democracy — credible, orderly and peaceful elections.”

## Conclusion

In conclusion, permit me to say that by invalidating these three mega Contracts, which had been entered into with grave abuse of discretion by the government entities concerned — PEA, MIAA and Comelec — the Supreme Court has firmly upheld the rule of law, which by itself is an essential ingredient of economic governance. Equally important to the business community, the Court has sent a clear and an unwavering signal that government contracts must be entered into fairly and evenhandedly. Far from being an unwanted interloper in economic matters, the Court in these three Decisions has steadfastly upheld one of the most revered axioms in business policy — the “leveling of the playing field.” Contrary to the claim of its critics, it has ruled in favor of stability, rationality and transparency in business. Some businessmen who entered into these void government contracts may have suffered some losses, but business in general has been promoted and advanced. ‘The rule of law, fairness and integrity have steadfastly been upheld.

To show the Court’s unswerving commitment to fair competition and a level playing field, let me quote in closing from the Mega Pacific Decision:

“x x x. In common parlance, public bidding aims to ‘level the playing field.’ That means that each bidder must bid under the same conditions; and be subject to the same guidelines, requirements and limitations, so that the best offer or lowest bid may be determined, a// other things being equal.

“Thus, it is contrary to the very concept of public bidding to permit a variance between the conditions under which bids are invited and those under which proposals are submitted and approved, or x x x the conditions under which a bid is won and those under which the awarded Contract will be complied with. The substantive amendment of the contract bidded out — after the bidding process has been concluded — is violative of the public policy on public biddings, as well as [of] the spirit and intent of RA 8436.”

I thank you.
