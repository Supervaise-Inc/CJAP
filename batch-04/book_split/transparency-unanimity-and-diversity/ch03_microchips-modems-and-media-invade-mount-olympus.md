# Chapter 3: Microchips, Modems and Media Invade Mount Olympus

Tonight, I would like to discuss the impact of cyber technology: first, on traditional legal doctrines; second, on judicial tools and facilities; and third, on the Supreme Court's relationship with its various publics, especially the media. Otherwise stated, my topic is how have microchips, modems and media affected life on Mount Olympus?

## The Impact of Cyber Technology

## The Three Waves of Civilizations

In his 1980 best seller entitled The Third Wave,’ Author Alvin Toffler divides known civilization into three main parts: “a First Wave agricultural phase, a Second Wave industrial phase and a Third Wave [information] phase x x x.” He explains that “the First Wave of change — the agricultural revolution — took thousands of years to play itself out. The Second Wave — the rise of the industrial civilization — took a mere three hundred years. Today, history is even more accelerative, and it is likely that the Third Wave will sweep across history and complete itself in a few decades. x x x. This new civilization x x x will topple bureaucracies, reduce the role of the nation-state, and give rise to semiautonomous economies in a post-imperialist world. It requires governments that are simpler, more effective, yet more democratic than any we know today. It is a civilization with its own distinctive world outlook, its own ways of dealing with time, space, logic and causality.”

Twenty years ago when I first read this book, I could not imagine how possession and distribution of information could become more valuable than the production and the sale of food and machines; how intangibles like electronic data and Internet access could command higher stock prices than gold, oil and real estate. But there is more than enough evidence that the personal computer, the Internet and the “dotcom” revolution are indeed radically transforming the ways of the world — from its mundane eating habits to electronic commerce to virtual entertainment to genome mapping and even to judicial doctrines and tools. Yes, the Web has convincingly proven that Internet “knowledge is power.” A few recent examples are in order.

## Biggest Merger, Largest Retailer and Most Powerful Economy

First. Just two months ago in January 2000, the biggest merger in corporate history took place when the world’s largest media company, Time Warner — which itself had been born of a prior marriage among Time Publishing, Warner Communications and Turner Broadcasting — was absorbed by America Online or AOL for $162 billion in AOL stock. The combined market value of the merged conglomerate is a staggering $350 billion dollars.* That is 22 times more than the budget of the Republic of the Philippines for Year 2000. Amazingly, AOL is barely a fifteen-year-old Internet company which, only three years ago, was mired in “an operational mess.”

Second. The new millennium will usher in the world’s biggest retail store. No, I am not talking about Walmart or Harrod’s or Mitsukoshi. I am referring to Amazon.com, the online shopping giant of e-commerce. It promises to be the earth’s biggest seller of everything from anywhere — from safety pins to airplanes, from jeepneys of Manila to diamonds of South Africa. Strangely enough, it will sell all these without owning or operating a single store or shopping center. And yet, it guarantees the cheapest price possible and on-time delivery of all purchases. The best evidence that this info-age phenomenon will happen is the proclamation of Jeff Bezos, Amazon.com's 35-year-old chief executive, as Time magazine’s 1999 “Person of the Year.”

Third. The United States continues to be the most powerful economy in the world, not because it produces the best automobiles or the tastiest hamburgers or the sweetest apples, but because it is the global center of information technology. And American Bill Gates is the richest man outside government, not because he has a monopoly of gambling casinos or petroleum products, but because he has been able to optimize the compression of knowledge into tiny microchips and its processing through computer software he simply calls “Windows.” Even Japanese behemoth Sony bowed to the Net when it bet its future, no longer on the audio sensation Walkman, but on the e-powered Playstation 2.

These massive transformational shifts have changed not only the economic face of the globe, but also everything else that matters — political systems, lifestyles, work habits, sciences, philosophies and, yes, even the normally reclusive and exclusive enclave of decision making.

## Impact on Judicial Doctrines

In my recent book, Leadership by Example, I briefly mentioned that in the new century, the Philippine judiciary “will be confronted with a new world order x x x. The cyber and space age — along with fiber optics, outer space exploration, genetic engineering, e-commerce, in-vitro fertilization, paperless communication and DNA advances — will definitely need new ways of coping.” The following are samples of the doctrinal challenges posed by the new information highway.

On e-commerce. Using his personal computer in Manila, Juan Biglangyaman orders from the Amazon.com central computer in Seattle a custom-made car to be manufactured in Germany and to be delivered to his girlfriend, Jane Bilmoko, who resides in Hongkong. May the bare computer order be considered a contract of sale? Will paperless electronic communication be admissible in evidence to prove the terms of the contract? In case the delivered left-hand-drive car is rejected by authorities in Hongkong, where only right-hand steering wheels are accepted, who should be held liable for the mistake? Where may one file a suit for the misdelivery of the car? Will a pleading transmitted via the Internet be legally acceptable?

On telemedicine. Juan Biglangyaman undergoes an executive checkup at the Makati Medical Center. Then he sends the laboratory, X-ray and CT scan results to an e-medical company, which transmits them through the Web to its worldwide network of experts, who then give him their so-called “second opinions.” Not fully satisfied, Juan consults, via video teleconferencing, one of the company’s medical specialists abroad. Questions: Is the e-medical company engaged in an illegal practice of medicine in the Philippines? In case Juan dies due to wrong diagnosis and prescription, is the e-medical company liable? How about its worldwide associates? Where and how should the suits be filed?

On surrogate parenthood. Who are deemed to be the legal parents of the child? What are the rights of the donor and the donee over that child? Add human cloning and we will be faced with even tougher questions like: May a person patent himself? May human tissue be regenerated for commercial purposes?

On e-economics. As liberalization, deregulation and globalization erase territorial frontiers, states and governments tend to surrender some of their authority and powers to the market and to the renewed energy of laissez faire. In such a situation, would the threats to civil liberties and human rights in fact shift from the government to the more bedeviling market forces that transcend geographical boundaries and political sovereignties? Several recent Supreme Court decisions tackled certain aspects of these new economic paradigms. Some of these were Tajsiada v. Angara,’ in which the Court upheld Philippine membership in the World Trade Organization; Tatad v. Secretary of Energy in which it nullified the first Oil Deregulation Law (RA 8180); and Garcia v. Corona,’ in Which it validated the second Oil Deregulation Law (RA 8479).

On e-media. The Supreme Court, in the very recent ABS-CBN v. Commission on Elections,” has declared that exit polls and the dissemination of their results to the public via the mass media are essential parts of the freedom of speech and expression. Therefore, they cannot be banned by the Comelec. This decision is precedent-setting, not only in the Philippines but elsewhere. I asked my legal staff to research, but they could not find a decision tackling an identical issue, written by any other credible Supreme Court in the world.”

In another media-fortifying Decision, Vasquez v. Court of Appeals,” the Court has ruled that truth is a complete defense in a libel case involving a public official. The avant garde Court explained that “even if the defamatory statement is false, no liability can attach if it relates to official conduct, unless the public official concerned proves that the statement was made with actual malice — that is, with knowledge that it was false or with reckless disregard of whether it was false or not x x x.” In other ~ ‘words, conviction in libel suits filed by public officials may be obtained only by proving that the defamatory words were (1) false and (2) made with the knowledge of their falsity or with a reckless disregard of whether or not they were false. This landmark Decision reversed existing jurisprudence requiring the accused to prove absence of malice in order to be entitled to an acquittal.

Because of our very limited time, I cannot exhaustively discuss the full impact of the e-age on our judicial doctrines. Let me just say that, for better or for worse, we in the judicial branch of government realize the inevitable onslaught of the cyber age and the need to adjust, and adjust speedily, to it.

## Impact on Judicial Tools

Let me now proceed to the second topic of tonight’s lecture — the Court’s response to cyber intrusion. A few years ago, the High Court formed the Committee on Computerization to oversee its transition to the Third Wave. This Committee, which was once chaired by then Associate Justice Davide, is now headed by Justice Jose A.R. Melo, with me as vice-chair, and Justices Consuelo Ynares-Santiago and Sabino R. De Leon Jr. as members. To back up the Committee, the Court also created the Management Information Systems Office or MISO headed by Atty. Ivan John Enrile Uy with a full-time staff of computer geeks, Let me now enumerate the e-related tools and facilities the judiciary has acquired or is in the process of acquiring.

The Case Administration System or CAS, which tracks for fast and easy access to all cases in the Supreme Court from the time they are filed until they are finally disposed of.

The Computer-Assisted Legal Research or CALR, an electronic compilation of all Decisions of the Supreme Court from 1901 to the present. This is an ongoing project in which the electronic compilation of Decisions promulgated from January 1996 to the recent past has already been done, with the rest to be finished in two years’ time. Once completely updated, this system will (lo away with bulky volumes like the SCRA and the l'hilippine Reports.

The computerization of various administrative processes like payrolls,' personnel record tracking, supplies and property accounting,' revenue monitoring,’ financial information and records management.!” For the curious, each of these programs is described in the footnotes to this lecture, and I shall not bore you with a verbal discussion.

The recent opening of the Supreme Court Internet homepage —www.supremecourt.gov.ph — through which the most recent Decisions and issuances are made accessible. In the near future, we hope to make this facility interactive to enable the public to “converse” with the Court.

The High Court's acquisition of 1,500 personal computers to be distributed to all regional trial courts and selected first-level courts nationwide. Through computerization, we hope to be able to administer lower courts more effectively and efficiently and thereby produce speedier and more judicious pronouncements.

The continuing legal education program of the Philippine Judicial Academy, including subjects like computer technology, hands-on computer training, and computer-aided legal research.

Apart from these ongoing Third Wave projects, the Court has also planned to adopt a number of technological innovations which should become realities during the next five years, well within Chief Justice Davide's term. Some of these are the following:

Voice or speech recognition whereby, in place of stenography, software and hardware will be used to recognize spoken words and convert them to printable or electronic format.

Electronic data interchange or paperless communication, whereby orders and writs as well as pleadings will be electronically exchanged among the courts, lawyers and parties. Of course, issues of security and authenticity will have to be addressed.

Electronic information kiosk, whereby case-calendaring will be made available via the Internet, with display terminals installed in public places like city halls, malls, and IBP houses.

Video teleconferencing, whereby detention prisoners, physically handicapped witnesses or victims of psychologically wounding crimes will be shielded from the trauma of courtroom appearances.

## Impact on the Court's Relationship with Its Publics

So much for cyber technology and its impact on judicial tools. I will now take up our third topic, which is really the consequence of the Court's relationship with its various publics, especially the media.

Let me start by recalling briefly that in the not too distant past, courts were sacrosanct. Supreme Court justices were treated like little deities on Mount Olympus, whose pronouncements and decisions were accepted with finality by both the litigants and the general public. Magistrates were thought to possess infinite wisdom and fairness, and thus it was unsportsmanlike for litigants to question their judgments. Indeed, no sane or credible journalist dared lampoon the Supreme Court. In turn magistrates lived in hermetic isolation, took a vow of silence in the face of current political, historical and social events, and clothed their internal processes with deliberate confidentiality or secretiveness.

However, the judicial enclave has been invaded by the information revolution, and the public has raised questions about the conduct of the erstwhile untouchable dwellers of Mount Olympus. Indeed microchips, modems and media cameras have been dangled as new, effective and efficient tools for processing truth.

Added to that, a new generation has arisen — young people schooled in the western ways of challenging the assumptions, the traditions and the customs of the old. These people, 30 years or younger who compose 70 percent of our population, have been raised in an atmosphere of independence and trained to criticize books and to challenge ideas. And the “people power” culture has encouraged them to question authoritarian or hierarchical impositions. After EDSA, democratic space and a libertarian spirit have pervaded the Filipino mentality and psyche.

Indeed, in a free society where transparency and accountability are the desirable standards of public office,!® and where the technologies of modern communications are readily available, some criticism of the judiciary is inevitable. I know that this public Vigilance is not confined to the Philippines. The same phenomenon affects almost all judicial jurisdictions in (he free world, particularly the US, Britain, Continental rope and Australia. As Justice Michael Kirby of the Australian High Court explained, “The media encourage conflict, dramatic visual images and demand instant solutions.”” Truly, the invasive power and influence of the media have penetrated the thick Walls of the judicial fortress.

## Four Options for Confronting Criticisms

To confront this reality, the Philippine judiciary has at least four options.

Una, sumuko agad. It can cave in and pander to what is popular and palatable. But that would be an abdication of the judicial function. A litigation is won, | because one side gets more cheers from the gallery, but because it is upheld by the law through a process of logic, through reasoned arguments based on legal principles and precedents. The duty of magistrates is to uphold the law regardless of the popular view. They must be independent not only of the parties to the case but also of everyone else including the President, Congress, the Church, the media and the mob.

Pangalawa, magmatigas at huwag pansinin. Magistrates can ignore the barrage and take refuge in Abraham Lincoln’s “if the end brings me out all right, what is said against me won’t amount to anything. If the end brings me out wrong, ten angels swearing | was right would make no difference.” Magistrates can silently suffer the indignities, even the slander, of the mob and wait for passions to die in the hope that the sorcery will go away. But this attitude is self-defeating because silence — worse, bullheadedness — is normally construed by the public to be an admission of guilt. Certainly, an unreasonable refusal to answer prima facie criticisms erodes public trust in the judicial institution.

Pangatlo, lumaban nang puspusan. Jurists can answer and explain all the slings, darts and arrows flung at them. However, there are limits to what they can do in this regard. They have no expertise in public debates. Because they spend their time and effort in hearing both sides of a conflict and in searching for principles and doctrines to resolve them, they do not have the aptitude or the appetite for media battles. Joining a public discussion requires a talent different from judicial reflection and decision making. Putting one’s tongue on the ramparts is as risky as putting one’s neck on the guillotine.

Pang-apat, magpatulong sa marunong. Courts may employ competent public affairs officers who can help journalists in understanding and accurately reporting their decisions and actions. The Supreme Court has opted for this fourth solution. It has recently created the Committee on Public Information, which I chair, and the Public Information Office or PIO headed by Atty. Ismael G. Khan Jr. I must hasten to add that the Committee on Public Information and the PIO will not “sanitize” news or “angle” stories. Neither will they “proselytize.” As I see it, our work is simply to tell the whole truth about the Court and its work.

## Transparency and Accountability: The Best Option

All told, I believe that the spirit of transparency and accountability, which has opened windows to the judicial fortress, is still the Court’s best response to the challenge of media criticism. The Chief Justice in his now familiar program, the “Davide Watch,” says it quite plainly: “[T]he Bench must be fully accountable to the people by remaining transparent, yet not betray those aspects of the judicial process which require utmost confidentiality.” I know that the Supreme Court is one of the most inscrutable institutions in this country. To be appreciated and defended, it needs, a priori, to be understood by our people.

The MOPC will be pleased to know that this policy of transparency and accountability is espoused not only by the Chief Justice and me. It is shared by many other justices and judges as well. In an unprecedented act in pursuit of this policy, the Court, in cooperation with the United Nations Development Program and the National Economic and Development Authority, embarked on a broad-based nationwide initiative to listen to suggestions of various sectors on how to improve the delivery of justice. During the last eighteen months, the justices have been patiently listening to the complaints and the recommendations of more than a thousand individuals from the basic sectors, nongovernmental organizations, government units, the academe, the media, religious groups, business and the various components of the judiciary. The results of this effort are summarized, complete with a timetable for implementation over a five-year period, in “Blueprint of Action for the Judiciary,” which was launched by the Court on February 4, 2000. An executive summary of the Blueprint was included in the press kits distributed earlier.

Likewise, on February 18, 2000, the High Court, together with the Asian Institute of Journalism and Communication, held a dialogue with selected leaders of the business community to discuss specific and concrete judicial reform projects.

Even more recently, exactly a week ago on March 3, 2000, the Court, this time assisted by the World Bank, conducted a whole day “Consultation-Workshop to Identify Judicial Reform Priorities.” Attended by various segments of the justice system, this Workshop focused on the issues of judicial integrity, independence and transparency. It prioritized various reforms, from the writing of more reader-friendly decisions to the continuing education — both intellectual and ethical — of judges and lawyers and the elimination of the dreaded three “ships” in public service: kinship, friendship and relationship.

I mention all these to demonstrate that the Court and its members have gone to great lengths to respond to the challenges of the new century. My friends, as you may realize by now, we are firm believers in transparency, accountability and integrity in the judiciary.

This was why | accepted the chairmanship of our Committee on Public Information, why I have shed my vow of silence and agreed to speak before you tonight as well as in a few other fora, and why I write one book a year to report on my work as a magistrate and to open a new window to the Court.

Verily, I believe that some of the heightened media focus on courts and their doings may be justified or even desirable. Incompetent, inefficient, inept, not to say corrupt, judges certainly deserve to be scrutinized and exposed, so long as the objective is truth, not vengeance or cheap entertainment at the prodding of defeated litigants in a terminated judicial combat. I also welcome fair comment on judicial decisions, provided that the decisions themselves are first objectively presented. I will not, however, countenance baseless attacks on the person, the honor, the dignity, or the alleged ulterior motives of jurists.

## A Plea for Discreet Restraint

Having said that, I would nonetheless plead for discreet restraint in your criticisms. As I said earlier, it is my hope that the media will understand that, Generally, judges cannot answer back. At least, they cannot do so effectively. Because they are inexperienced and their access to media is inadequate, their attempts to respond may result, ironically enough, in compounding their problems and demeaning their offices. The judiciary and its members should not be likened to politicians who cater to controversy and love to see themselves in the limelight for whatever reason, whether for right or for wrong, provided their names are spelled correctly. When subjected to unwarranted, reckless and wanton attacks, the judicial institution may be permanently damaged and judicial integrity hopelessly undermined.

True, the judiciary must stand, and stand steadfast, on its duty to decide cases according to law and reason. True also, cases are won or lost not because of popular palatability or compromise or friendship, not to mention pecuniary consideration, but because of reasoned arguments flowing from legal principles and precedents.

This is not to say that the Court is averse to public opinion. In fact, I dare say that it is effective only as long as it retains the faith and trust of the public. Without the support of the populace, it becomes useless, as it cannot enforce by itself its own pronouncements. It does not command the army or the police or, for that matter, the “raging mob”; neither does it have the money to grease political patronage.

But there is a difference between the shifting winds of public emotion and long-term public trust in the institution. While critics and pressure groups may rant from time to time against some of its decisions or opinions, the Court must enjoy enough residual respect and esteem, with the constant hope that one day it will prove to be right after all. And even if its level-headed critics may disagree, they should respect it enough to concede its good faith and likewise the wisdom, the probity and the diligence with which its members have done their work. They must realize that in the very nature of our system of government, the Court's function is not to mirror popular ideas, but in fact to teach and open vistas to enlightened opinion. Like journalists and broadcasters, judges are effective only as long as they retain long-term public acceptance.

All things said, it should be quite obvious to you now that the media and the judiciary are natural partners, because they are bound together by the same reasons for being — the search for truth, the protection of the people’s rights, and the defense of the basic norms of society. So, too, they are both plagued by the same difficulty: they do not have direct access to government resources or to the police and the military to enforce their opinions and judgments. And in fulfilling their duties, both the judiciary and the media appeal only to the persuasive power of the word, reason, logic and the core values of organized community.

## Epilogue

Mount Olympus has been invaded by microchips, modems and media, and the lives of the gods have irreversibly been altered. Jurists who are used to the conservative, patrician, even authoritarian, ways of the past realize the need for adjustment to the “megashifts” demanded by the information age. In turn,  I ask you, the purveyors of truth and molders of Opinions, to understand the generational gap between the graying men and women of the judiciary and the Wired generation of 30 years and below which constitutes 70 percent of our population.

Indeed, I close this address with a plea to you, our natural partners, to help us engender an informed appreciation of judges and the rule of law, not by means of public relations hype or blind and uncritical faith, but by a thoughtful evaluation of the role of the judiciary as the last bulwark of democracy and as the final recourse of the damned. In fine, I pray that you help us uphold the integrity, the independence and the dignity of the judiciary and its members.

I thank you.
