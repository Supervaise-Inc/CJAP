# Chapter 11: The NLRC Cases: Finding the Right Balance Between Labor and Management

Prior to St. Martin Funeral Home v. NLRC,’ the Supreme Court directly entertained petitions for certiorari challenging decisions of the National Labor Relations Commissions (NLRC). Finding the right balance between labor and management in these reviews can sometimes become really contentious. The Constitution, however, guarantees full protection to labor. And in case of doubt, the law favors labor?

In any event, I would like to briefly discuss some of my labor decisions which were unanimously supported by the other members of the Court during my first three years in office (October 10, 1995 to October 10, 1998).

A. San Pedro Hospital v. Secretary of Labor ruled that the labor secretary may order parties to a labor dispute to enter into anew Collective Bargaining Agreement and grant back wages even without ruling on the legality of the strike.

## Facts

While negotiating for the renewal of their Collective Bargaining Agreement (CBA), the union proposed a P60 salary increase with a union security clause, while the hospital countered with a P2 increase, claiming it was incurring losses, and expressly opposed the inclusion of such clause. After conciliation failed to resolve their deadlock, the union struck. It picketed the hospital premises until the operations came to a halt, forcing doctors and patients to leave the site. The labor secretary assumed jurisdiction over the labor dispute and issued a return-to-work Order. Although the union members were willing to return to work, the hospital refused to accept them. Accordingly, it notified the Department of Labor and Employment of its temporary suspension of operations for six months. Labor Secretary Ruben Torres (a) ruled that the suspension of operations was intended to defeat the right of the workers to self-organization, (b) granted them back wages from the time they were refused admittance, and (3) ordered the hospital to enter into a new CBA with them and to include therein a union security clause.

## Issues

1. Was the temporary suspension of hospital operation valid?

2. May the secretary of labor order the parties to enter into a new CBA and grant back wages without ruling on the legality of the strike?

## Ruling

1. No, the suspension was not valid. A temporary suspension of operations is recognized as a valid exercise of management prerogative, provided it is not carried out in order to circumvent the provisions of the Labor Code or to defeat the rights of the employees. The burden of proving that such suspension is bona fide falls upon the employer. A precarious financial condition must be established by evidence. The hospital’s unexplained and continued failure to submit its financial statements raised grave doubts about its claimed financial crisis and the real purpose of its suspension of operations. It was not enough merely to raise such claim or to discuss it only in passing. It was essential to prove it.

2. Yes, the labor secretary may order the parties to enter into a new CBA and may grant back wages without ruling on the legality of the strike. As provided in the Labor Code and its implementing rules, an employer-employee relationship shall be deemed suspended in case of a suspension of operation, but with the implicit assumption that once operations are resumed, the relationship shall be revived and restored. The secretary validly ordered the parties to enter into a new CBA, assuming that operations would resume after six months. Since the hospital had refused to bargain without valid cause, he properly granted the wage increase and imposed the union security clause in the exercise of his powers under Article 263(i) of the Code.

The secretary of labor acted within his jurisdiction when he granted back wages based on the illegality of the temporary suspension, not on the illegality of the strike. Under Article 263(g) of the Code, he was authorized to do so, in order to penalize an erring employer who refused to accept returning employees.

B. North Davao Mining v. APT ruled that companies which closed their operations because of business losses — in this case amounting to over P20 billion — were not required to pay separation benefits, not even in the case of the petitioner which had a “longstanding policy of giving separation pay benefits equivalent to 30-days’ pay.” Debunking respondents’ reliance on Businessday Information Systems and Services (BISSI) v. NLRC,* the Court ruled in this wise:

“In resolving the present case, it bears keeping in mind at the outset that the factual circumstances of BISSI are quite different from [those of] the current case. The Court noted that BISSI continued to suffer losses even after the retrenchment of the first batch of employees; clearly, business did not improve despite such drastic measures. That notwithstanding, when BISSI finally shut down, it could well afford to (and actually did) pay off its remaining employees with MORE separation benefits as compared with those earlier laid off; obviously, then, there was 10 reason for BISSI to skimp on separation pay for the first batch of discharged employees. That it was able to pay one-month separation benefit for employees at the time of closure of its business meant that it must have also been in a position to pay the same amount to those who were separated prior to closure. That it did not do so was a wrongful exercise of management prerogatives. That is why the Court correctly faulted it with ‘impermissible discrimination.’ Clearly, it exercised its management prerogatives contrary to ‘[g]eneral principles of fair play and justice.’

“In the instant case however, the company’s practice of giving one month’s pay for every year of service could no longer be continued precisely because the company could not afford it anymore. It was forced to close down on account of accumulated losses of over P20 billion. This could not be said of BISSI. In the case of North Davao, it gave 30-days’ separation pay to its employees when it was still a going concern even if it was already losing heavily. As a going concern, its cash flow could still have sustained the payment of such separation benefits. But when a business completely ceases operations, i.e.. upon its death as a going business concern, its vital lifeblood — its cashflow — literally dries up. Therefore, the fact that less separation benefits were granted when the company finally met its business death cannot be characterized as discrimination. Such action was dictated not by a discriminatory management option but by its complete inability to continue its business life due to accumulated losses. Indeed, one cannot squeeze blood out of a dry stone. Nor water out of parched land.”

Below is a more detailed summation of the case.

## Facts

Petitioner North Davao Mining Corporation (North Davao) was incorporated in 1974 as a 100 percent privately owned company. Later, the Philippine National Bank (PNB) became part owner thereof as a result of the conversion into equity of a portion of loans obtained by the former from the latter. On June 30, 1986, PNB transferred all its loans to, and equity in, North Davao in favor of the national government which, by virtue of Proclamation No. 50 dated December 8, 1986, later turned them over to Petitioner Asset Privatization Trust (APT). As of December 31, 1990 the national government held 81.8 percent of the common stock and 100 percent of the preferred stock of said company.

On May 31, 1992, Petitioner North Davao completely ceased operations due to serious business reverses. As of December 31, 1991, or five months prior to its closure, its total liabilities had exceeded its assets by P20.392 billion. When it ceased operations, its remaining employees were separated and given the equivalent of 12.5 days’ pay for every year of service, computed on their basic monthly pay, in addition to the commutation to cash of their unused vacation and sick leaves. However, during the life of the petitioner corporation, from the beginning of its operations in 1981 until its closure in 1992, it had been giving separation pay equivalent to thirty days’ pay for every year of service.

## Issue

When forced by huge business losses to close its business, is a company legally required to pay separation benefits to its employees at the time of its closure, in an amount equivalent to the separation pay paid to those who were separated when the company was still a going concern?

## Ruling

No, the company is not required to pay such separation benefits. Article 283 of the Labor Code governs the grant of separation benefits “in case of closures or cessation of operation” of business establishments “NOT due to serious business losses or financial reverses.” Where, however, the closure is due to proven business losses — as in the instant case, in which the aggregate losses amounted to over P20 billion — the Labor Code does not, for obvious reasons, impose any obligation upon the employer to pay separation benefits.

The company’s practice of giving one month’s pay for every year of service could no longer be continued, precisely because it could no longer afford to do so.

C. Philippines Today v. NLRC held that, by way of exception to the general rule, a memorandum written by an employee apprising his employer of his frustrations in his job and his differences with his immediate boss, taken together with confirmatory circumstances, constituted voluntary resignation. Here is a synopsis of the case.

## Facts

Felix R. Alegre Jr. was assistant to Maximo V. Soliven, publisher of Philippine Star, a daily newspaper owned by petitioner, when he wrote a Memorandum addressed to Betty Go-Belmonte (now deceased), the chairman and CEO of the Star Group of Publications, with copies sent to all other officers and members of the board of directors. In that Memorandum, he apprised his employer of his frustrations in his job and his differences with the publisher, who was his immediate superior. Petitioner considered his Memorandum as a letter of resignation, to which he objected, saying he had never meant to resign. Thus, he filed a case for illegal dismissal.

## Issue

Did the respondent's Memorandum constitute voluntary resignation?

## Ruling

Yes, taken with confirmatory circumstances, it did. Considering the peculiar circumstances attendant to its writing — together with the antecedent, contemporaneous and subsequent actions of private respondent — it juridically constituted a letter of resignation. His offensive and disrespectful choice of words and way of expression belied his allegation that it was simply an “opportunity to open the eyes of Belmonte to the work environment” at the Star. Besides, he directly addressed it, not to his immediate superior, but to the chairman and CEO, with copies sent to all other officers and members of the board. Furthermore, he was a highly confidential employee who could hold his job for only as long as he enjoyed the trust and confidence of his employer. His use of abrasive language in his Memorandum, as well as the manner in which he disseminated it, rendered him unworthy of such trust and confidence.

More significantly, some peculiar actions confirming his intention to terminate his employment with the Star were noted by the Court. First, he claimed that his leave of absence was due to medical reasons, for which he was supposed to seek relief abroad. However, the Court found nothing in the records to prove such claim. Second, even before he knew of the acceptance of his resignation, he had cleared his desk of his personal belongings, thus baring his intent to leave his job. Third, he did not return to work after his authorized leave of absence had expired. Finally and most tellingly, prior to receiving Mrs. Belmonte’s letter accepting his resignation, he had worked as chief of staff of the office of then Senator Sotero H. Laurel, with a much higher compensation. His assumption of another full-time job was clearly inconsistent with any desire to remain in employment with petitioner.

I am reproducing the Epilogue of the Decision to stress the rationale for this exception to the general rule on voluntary resignation. Indeed, “each case has its own flesh and blood and cannot be decided simply on the basis of isolated clinical classroom principles.”

“Both the Constitution and the Labor Code mandate a bias in favor of labor. Hence, this Court, as a matter of judicial policy, leans backwards to protect labor and the working class against the machinations and incursions of their more financially entrenched employers. In the present case, however, it is obvious to us that private respondent’s memorandum could not have been intended merely to persuade management to improve the work environment at the Philippine Star. Rather, it was evidently a recitation of the facts and reasons why respondent Alegre could no longer continue working under what he believed were unbearable conditions in the work place. The offensive language used by a well-educated man endowed with unusual writing skill could not have been intended merely for the “suggestion box.” That it was addressed and given to persons _ of uncommon perception themselves takes the letter out of ordinary employer-employee communications. It is true that there was no direct mention of the word ‘resignation.’ However, the incendiary words employed denote a clear intent to end the writer’s association of trust and confidence with his superiors and employer. This intent becomes even more manifest when viewed in light of attendant acts of Alegre, particularly his prolonged leave of absence, his clearing of his own desk of personal belongings, his failure to report back to work after the expiration of his approved leave, his verbal expression of his intent to resign, and most notably, his assumption of a higher paying job in a political office which was incompatible with his work at the Star.

“In deciding cases, this Court does not matter-of-factly apply and interpret laws ina vacuum. General principles do not decide specific cases. Rather, laws are interpreted always in the context of the peculiar factual situation of each case. Each case has its own flesh and blood and cannot be decided simply on the basis of isolated clinical classroom principles. The circumstances of time, place, event, person, and particularly attendant circumstances and actions before, during and after the operative fact should all be taken in their totality so that justice can be rationally and fairly dispensed.”

D. Naguiat v. NLRC ruled that taxi drivers, upon closure of business by a non-losing employer, are entitled to regular separation pay benefits. It further held that in the payment of this obligation, the owners who had actively managed the business were jointly and severally liable.

## Facts

Petitioner Clark Field Taxi, Inc. (CFTI) used to operate taxi services within Clark Air Base. Because of the phase-out of the US military bases, it ceased Operations and terminated the services of its taxicab drivers. As severance pay, the drivers were each given P500 for every year of service. But herein individual respondents demanded separation pay equivalent to their average monthly earnings per year of service.

## Issues

1. Were private respondents entitled to regular separation pay as provided under the Labor Code?

2. Were officers/owners of the corporation solidarily liable with the company?

## Ruling

1. Yes, private respondents were entitled to regular separation pay, because the closure of petitioners’ taxi business within Clark was due to the phase-out of U.S. military installations there, not to any great financial loss. In fact, the business was earning profitably at the time of its closure. Under Article 283 of the Labor Code, “x x x in cases of closures or cessation of operations of establishment or undertaking not due to serious business losses or financial reverses, the separation pay shall be equivalent to one (1) month pay or at least one-half (1/ 2) month pay for every year of service, whichever is higher. A fraction of at least six (6) months shall be considered one (1) whole year.”

Based on the factual submissions of the parties, private respondents were regular employees of CFII who received wages on a boundary or commission basis. In their amended Complaint, respondents detailed their work schedule and financial arrangement with CFTI. It could be inferred therefrom that their monthly take-home pay amounted to not less than $240, an inference which petitioners did not refute with contrary evidence. Thus, they were estopped from questioning the monthly earnings of respondents,

2. Only the owner who was actively engaged in the management of the business was solidarity liable with the company. Petitioner Naguiat was admittedly the president of CFTI which he actively managed. He fell within the meaning of employer as contemplated in Art. 212(c)? of the Labor Code — one who could be held jointly and severally liable for the corporation’s obligations to its dismissed employees.

Moreover, petitioners also conceded that CFTI was a close family corporation. Section 100, paragraph 5 (under Title XII on Close Corporations) of the Corporation Code, states:

“(5) To the extent that the stockholders are actively engage(d) in the management or operation of the business and affairs of a close corporation, the stockholders shall be held to strict fiduciary duties to each other and among themselves. Said stockholders shall be personally liable for corporate torts unless the corporation has obtained reasonably adequate liability insurance.”

Essentially, tort is the violation of a right given or the omission of a duty imposed by law. It is a breach of a legal duty. Article 283 of the Labor Code mandates the employer to grant separation pay to employees in case of closure or cessation of operations of the establishment or undertaking not due to serious business losses or financial reverses, which was the condition obtaining at bar. CFTI failed to comply with this law-imposed duty or obligation. Consequently, its stockholder who was actively engaged in its management and operation was held personally liable.

E. Caurdanetaan Piece Workers Union v. Laguesma reiterated the “four fold” test to determine the existence of employer-employee relations; namely, (1) the power to hire, (2) the payment of wages, (3) the power to dismiss and, most important, (4) the power to control. Applying this test, the Court ruled that the members of petitioner-union were regular employees. In its words:

“It is undeniable that petitioner’s members worked as cargadores for private respondents. They loaded, unloaded and piled sacks of palay from the warehouses to the cargo trucks and from the cargo trucks to the buyers. This work is directly related, necessary and vital to the operations of Corfarm. Moreover, Corfarm did not even allege, much less prove, that petitioner’s members have ‘substantial capital or investment in the form of tools, equipment, machineries, [and] work premises, among others.’ Furthermore, said respondent did not contradict petitioner’s allegation that it paid wages directly to these workers without the intervention of any third-party independent contractor. It also wielded the power of dismissal over petitioners; in fact, its exercise of this power was the progenitor of the Second Case. Clearly, the workers are not independent contractors.

“Applying Article 280" of the Labor Code, we hold that the CPWU members were regular employees of private respondents. Their tasks were essential in the usual business of private respondent.

“As we have ruled in an earlier case, the question of whether an employer-employee relationship exists in a certain situation has bedevilled the courts. Businessmen, with the aid of lawyers, have tried to avoid or sidestep such relationships, because that juridical vinculum engenders obligations connected with workmen’s compensation, social security, medicare, minimum wage, termination pay and unionism." All too familiarly, Respondent Corfarm sought refuge from these obligations. However, the records of this case clearly support the existence of the juridical vinculum.”

In the interest of speedy justice, the Decision I penned refused to go along with the NLRC’s ruling to remand the case to the labor arbiter. Hence, on the issue of whether petitioners had illegally been dismissed, it ruled in this wise: “Why Respondent NLRC refused to rule directly on the appeal escapes us. The remand of a case or an issue to the labor arbiter for further proceedings is unnecessary, considering that the NLRC was in a position to resolve the dispute based on the records before it and particularly where the ends of justice would be served thereby." Remanding the case would needlessly delay the resolution of the case which has been pending since 1992. As already observed, the evidence on record clearly supports the findings of the labor arbiter.

“Pursuant to the doctrine that this Court has a duty to settle, whenever possible, the entire controversy in a single proceeding, leaving no root or branch to bear the seeds of future litigation, we now resolve all issues.”

“It is axiomatic that in illegal dismissal cases, the employer always has the burden of proof,’* and his failure to discharge this duty results in a finding that the dismissal was unjustified.” Having defaulted from filing its position paper, Respondent Corfarm is deemed to have waived its right to present evidence and counter the allegations of petitioner’s members.”

F. Somerville Stainless Steel v. NLRC discussed, among others, the sufficiency of proof of losses to justify retrenchment of workers. My ponencia held as follows:

“To prove its loss, petitioner presented only its financial statements for the fiscal year that ended on December 31, 1992.'9 These financial statements show that the petitioner’s gross income was P7,451,981.35 and — deducting therefrom the cost of goods sold, which was P4,843,787.87, and the operating expenses of P2,714,835.15 — the net loss was P106,641.67. This net loss plus the deficit as of January 1, 1992, which was P286,354.71, resulted in the total loss of P392,996.38 as of the end of 1992.

“These, however, fall far short of the stringent requirement of the law that the employer prove sufficiently and convincingly its allegation of substantial losses. The failure of petitioner to show its income or loss for the immediately preceding years or to prove that it expected no abatement of such losses in the coming years bespeaks the weakness of its cause. The financial statement for 1992, by itself, does not sufficiently prove petitioner’s allegation that it ‘already suffered actual serious losses,’”2° because it does not show whether its losses increased or decreased. Although the petitioner posted a loss for 1992, it is also possible that such loss was considerably less than those previously incurred, thereby indicating the company’s improving condition.”

“Hence, in AG&P,” the employer sufficiently proved its case when it presented proof that its income continuously decreased from P205 million in 1984, P175 million in 1985, to P101 million in 1986 and, eventually, to a loss of P34 million in 1987 prior to the retrenchment it effected in 1988. Afterwards, it submitted to the NLRC further evidence showing that it incurred a loss of P176 million in 1990. All these proofs showed that its losses were substantial and urgent enough to justify retrenchment. In North Davao Mining Corporation? the employer presented proof that it suffered net losses averaging three billion pesos (P3,000,000,000) a year for five years prior to its closure. In the instant case, petitioner insists that its total deficit or accumulated losses as of December 31, 1992 were exactly 98.25% of the stockholder’s equity for the same year. This percentage by itself does not conclusively prove that the petitioner could have avoided substantial losses only through retrenchment.

G. In Pascua v. NLRC, I reiterated the general rule that resignation must be made voluntarily and accompanied by an intention of relinquishing the office and by an act of relinquishment. To allow an employer to dismiss an employee based on mere allegations and generalizations would place the latter at the mercy of the former, and the right to security of tenure would be unduly emasculated.

In addition to reinstatement with full back wages and other benefits, the petitioners in this case were awarded moral damages and attorney’s fees, because their dismissal “was attended by bad faith or constituted an act oppressive to labor or was done in a manner contrary to morals, good customs or public policy.” Ruled the Court:

“In cases involving breaches of contracts, moral damages may also be awarded where

the defendant acted fraudulently or in bad faith.” Good faith refers to a state of mind, which is manifested by the acts of the individual concerned. It consists of an intention to abstain from taking an unconscionable and unscrupulous advantage of another.® Henry Lao’s dismissals of petitioners, except Santos who resigned, was marked by precipitate dispatch and utter disregard of due process. Clearly, Lao abused his position as an employer.

“It is well-settled that, for dismissals to be valid, not only must employers show sufficient ground therefor, but must also prove that they observed procedural due process by giving their employees two notices — first, of the intention to dismiss, indicating therein their acts or omissions complained against; and, second, of the decision to dismiss — and, in between such notices, an opportunity for them to answer and rebut the charges against them.” In the present case, not only was the private respondent's dismissal of petitioners without just cause; it was also without procedural due process.

“Attorney’s fees may likewise be awarded, for concerned petitioners were dismissed in bad faith.° Petitioner Pascua was summarily dismissed. Petitioner De Castro was also dismissed without due process after she demanded to be paid her correct wages as mandated by labor laws. Petitioner Soriano, in spite of her explanation in answer to the capricious charges raised by Henry Lao, was still summarily dismissed. Attorney’s fees may be awarded when petitioners, illegally dismissed in bad faith, are compelled to litigate or incur expenses to protect their rights by reason of the acts and the omissions of their employers.”
