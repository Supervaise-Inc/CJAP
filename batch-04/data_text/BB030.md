I thank my compañero, your president, former Comelec Commissioner Chino Marquinez, for inviting me to keynote your award ceremonies tonight. Except for a few occasions when the topic is directly relevant to Supreme Court projects, I normally turn down speaking invitations. However, Chino was insistent. He claimed that the Court was active lately in explaining its recent Decisions touching on the economy. He explained that there was a need to discuss not only how Supreme Court Decisions affected businessmen, but also how they protected consumers. Indeed, he had a point. And so I agreed to deliver this address tonight.

## Three Great Phenomena of the Twentieth Century

Ladies and gentlemen, three great phenomena of the 20'" century have changed the social behavior of everyone on this planet. The first was the invention of the automobile, which stimulated people to move about in unprecedented numbers. With its coming, a virtual social revolution took place. The car enabled people to move about frey and independently, to a degree never before imagined.

But a few decades after the automobile had sent people upon the highroad, television appeared and has done the opposite. It has tended to keep most of us at home by bringing history, movies, sporting events and cultural affairs from everywhere in the world into the privacy of our living rooms. Every type of news events — coronations, inauguration, ball games and, yes, even wars and moon landings — can now be witnessed right inside our home.’

The third great phenomenon is the personal computer. Combined with the Internet and the cell phone, the computer has enabled people to conduct their business at home and has thus made them even less dependent on the automobile. In fact, in many places in the world, one can shop without having to go to a grocery or store. Theoretical, we can order almost anything through e-commerce websites. Moreover, with the personal computer, we can communicate — or even confer -- with office mates as if they were physically present in the office.

In spite of these great scientific milestones, the consumer has remained at the center. In the words of the late US President Lyndon B. Johnson, “the consumer is moving forward; we cannot test content until he is in the front row, not displacing the interest of the producer, yet giving equal rank in representation with that interest.”

As I speak before you today, the world is irreversibly being propelled towards trade liberalization, deregulation, globalization and privatization. Indeed, these have become the third-millennium buzz words.’ In our day and age, we know that the advances in trade, banking transactions, tee- — communications, transportation and migration of people have begun to blur national legal boundaries. But in the midst of all these crucial changes in the world, the concern for the total interest of the consumer has become even more pronounced. The right to safety, the right to be informed, the right to choose and the right to be heard have become the priority concerns of nations and governments.

In a speech before the 1962 US Congress, President John F. Kennedy identified who the consumers were and rationalized the need to safeguard their rights in this wise:

“Consumers by definition include us all. They are the largest economic group in the economy, affecting and affected by almost every public and private economic decision. ‘Two-thirds of all spending in the economy is by consumers. They are the only important group in the economy who are not effectively organized, whose views are often not heard

“If consumers are offered inferior products, if prices are exorbitant, if drugs are unsafe or worthless, if the consumer is unable to choose on an informed basis, then his dollar is wasted, his health and safety may be threatened, and the national interest suffers.”

As Kennedy pointed out, whatever financial loss or injury consumers suffer as a result of substandard or hazardous products indirectly affects the economy. Hence, the necessity of state action in consumer protection.

## The Need for Consumer Protection

The need for consumer protection cannot be overemphasized. In the Philippines, most consumers share certain general characteristics that make them especially vulnerable to abuses by merchants, sellers, and manufacturers:

First, most Filipino consumers still view caveat emptor as the controlling rule in consumer transactions. The prevailing notion is that consumers must sufficiently ex- — amine goods before buying them. Hence, they must bear the loss resulting from any failure to identify flaws or defects.

Second, some shoppers view consumer protection as a choice between two substantially similar products: a more expensive product equipped with a guarantee versus a cheaper item sold “as is.” While the consumer can easily calculate the difference in price, it is not easy to discern how much the warranty is worth. Many Filipino buyers opt to pay less. Having chosen to buy the cheaper product, they are less likely to return to the seller to demand the shoddy or inferior. Having “taken the risk,” they feel obliged to absorb the loss.

Third, Filipinos value pakikisama’ or “the ability” get along.” For a person to be considered as market makisama or “one who knows how to get along,” one must be able to articulate demands and resolve conflicts without being pushy; and without resorting to anger, threats or confrontation.

Filipinos also value pakikipagkapwa, which incorporates the prescribed rules of reciprocal privileges and obligations of members of a given social group. When they fail to act according to the behaviors and values expected by the group, they are labeled as walang kapwa-tao. When merchants, whether sellers or manufacturers, exhibit manifest bad faith — whether or not through unfair, deceptive or fraudulent behavior — they may be regarded as walang kapwa-tao and outside the protection of pakikisama. It is only when merchants are characterized as walang kapwa-tao and the relationship with them is not worth saving that Filipino consumers will resort to legal remedies to enforce their rights. Otherwise, the latter will usually just shrug and bear any inconvenience caused by the merchants’ products or trade practices.

Fourth, most consumers lack awareness of their legal rights and the remedies available to them. ‘They are the discouraged from initiating legal action, because it usually requires them to hire a lawyer, and so the costs old be high. Most would be willing to “let go [of] a raw deal [rather] than engage in expensive court battles against a powerful adversary — the manufacturer.”

Moreover, the very structure of customer transactions works against consumers. They are typically in a weak bargaining position because of the disparity in knowledge and resources between the parties. Unlike ordinary contracts, consumer contracts are standardized, with the terms dictated by the stronger party. Today, businesses have grown larger; and the bulk of everyday transactions involve powerful chains, franchises, multi-nationals or conglomerates. Often, consumers deal directly only with the other party’s agents, who do not have the authority or the discretion to adjust the contract to the former’s needs. The notion of “shopping around” for a better bargain is hardly true today, when manufacturers and their competitors may often have a monopoly of the product and offer essentially the same terms.

## The Role of the Supreme Court

The Constitution has vested the Supreme Court with wide powers to protect the rights of consumers. It has the prerogative to pass upon lower-court judgments that have been appealed to it. Moreover, under Section 1 of Article VIII, it may review and set aside any act of any agency of government — including the Presidency and Congress — on the ground of grave abuse of discretion, which may consist of the following:

1. Violation of the Constitution, the law or judicial doctrine

2: Whimsical or arbitrary action arising from manifest bias or personal animosity, of such gravity as to have deprived the losing party of due process

With this mandate, the Supreme Court has had the occasion to rule on the rights of consumers in cases that are, ultimately, really armed at protecting them. Let us examine some of these cases.

## Consumers Favored by the Constitution

In Tañada v. Angara, the Supreme Court ruled that the Constitution favored consumers, not industries or enterprises. Debunking the argument that the Philippine adherence to the World Trade Organization (WTO/GATT/GATT) had violated the constitutional policy favoring a “self-reliant and independent national economy,” the Court upheld the WTO/GATT/GATT concept aimed at making available the best goods and services obtainable anywhere in the world at the most reasonable prices. Affirming the importance of the promotion of general welfare, the Court upheld prosperity, employment, purchasing power, and quality products at the most reasonable rates.

## Oil Deregulation

One of the most important consumer issues faced by the Court was the constitutionality of Republic Act No. 8180, which deregulated the oil industry. Said the Court:

“The issues involved in the deregulation of the downstream oil industry are of paramount significance. The ramifications, international and local in scope, are complex. ‘The impact on the nation’s economy is pervasive and far-reaching. The amounts involved in the oil business are immense. Fluctuations in the supply and price of oil products have a dramatic effect on economic development and public welfare.”

I should stress that the Court — in Tatad v. Secretary of the Department of Energy — nullified RA No. 8180, not because the SC necessarily agreed with deregulation as an economic policy, but because “as cobbled by Congress in its present form, the law violates the Constitution. The right call therefore should be for Congress to write a new oil deregulation law that conforms to the Constitution and not for this Court to shirk its duty of striking down a law that offends the Constitution. Striking down RA No. 8180 may cost losses in quantifiable terms to the oil oligopolists. But the loss in tolerating the tampering of our Constitution is not quantifiable in pesos and centavos. More worthy of protection than the supra-normal profits of private corporations is the sanctity of the fundamental principles of the Constitution.”

To protect consumers, the Supreme Court has the duty to strike down any law that violates the fundamental law of the land. The Constitution mandates the Court to be the guardian not only of the people’s political but also of their economic rights. ‘True, in the President and (Congress — not in the Court — is vested the power and the prerogative to determine whether to adopt the policy of oil deregulation and, if so, under what conditions and circumstances. However, all such policies and their ramifications must conform to the Constitution. (Otherwise, the Court has the duty to strike them down, not because they are unwise or inconvenient, but because they are constitutionally impermissible.

In Tatad v. Secretary of Energy, the Court found that oil deregulation had, in essence, shifted the burden of price control from the government to the “market forces,” in order (1) to eliminate government intervention that might “do more harm than good”; and (2) to achieve a truly competitive market of fair prices. Aimed at removing government abuse and corruption in price-setting, deregulation was at bottom supposed to provide the best goods and services at the cheapest prices.

## Public Utility Regulation

In Third World countries like the Philippines, equal justice will not have an authentic ring unless the economic rights of the people, especially the poor, are protected with the same resoluteness as their right to liberty. The case Republic v. Meralco is of utmost significance because it upheld the right of our people to avail themselves of electricity and to be reasonably charged for it.

At the outset, it should be stressed that the regulation of rates to be charged by public utilities is founded upon the police powers of the State. Statutes prescribing rules for the control and regulation of public utilities are a valid exercise of such power. Explained the Court:

‘When private property is used for a public purpose and is affected with public interest, it ceases to be juris private only and becomes subject to regulation. The regulation is to promote the common good. Submission to regulation may be withdrawn by the owner by discontinuing use; but as long as use of the property is continued, the same is subject to public regulation.

x x x

“In regulating rates charged by public utilities, the State protects the public against arbitrary and excessive rates while maintaining the efficiency and quality of services rendered. However, the power to regulate rates does not give the State the right to prescribe rates which are so low as to deprive the public utility of a reasonable return on investment. Thus, the rates prescribed by the State must be one that yields a fair return on the public utility upon the value of the property performing the service and one that is reasonable to the public for the services rendered. The fixing of just and reasonable rates involves a balancing of the investor and the consumer interests.”

In this case, the Court held that the Energy Regulatory Board (ERB) had correctly ruled that income tax should not be included in the computation of the operating expenses of a public utility. The Court said:

“Income tax paid by a public utility is inconsistent with the nature of operating expenses. In general, operating expenses are l those which are reasonably incurred in connection with business operations to yield revenue or income. They are items of expenses which contribute or are attributable to the production of income or revenue. As correctly put by the ERB, operating expenses should be a requisite of or necessary in the operation of a utility, recurring, and that it redounds to the service of benefit of customers.”

The customers of a public utility derive no benefit from the taxes it pays, and its payment of income tax contributes nothing directly to its operation for pur-— poses of generating revenue or profit. Accordingly, the burden of paying income tax should be Meralco’s alone; it should not be shifted to the consumers by including that tax in the computation of the company’s operating expenses. ‘To charge them for its expenses that are not related to the service or benefit they derive from it is unjustified and inequitable. Thus, ERB’s Order directing Meralco to refund its customers for amounts it had charged them to cover its income tax liabilities was upheld by the Court.

On the other hand, in protecting consumers from the provisional rare increases granted by the Energy Regulatory Commission (ERC), the Court ruled in a later case, Freedom from Debt Coalition v. ERC,“ that the challenged provisional rate increase had transgressed the EPIRA law and the implementing rules and regulations (IRR) thereof.

In this case, the Court stopped the collection of higher electricity rates granted by ERC and stressed the importance of two basic rules: first, the need to publish the application for rare increase in a newspaper of general circulation in the locality where the applicant operate; and second, the need for ERC, in its action on the application or motion for provisional rare adjustments, (to consider the comments or pleadings of the customers and the local government unit (LGU) concerned.

Obviously, the new IRR requirements are aimed at protecting the consumers and at diminishing the disparity or imbalance between them and the public utility. [The publication requirement consciously enhances their opportunity to weigh the application for the rare increase, in terms of its basis and the additional financial burden that such increase would entail. With the publication of the application itself, the consumers would be equipped - right from the start — with the needed information to determine for themselves whether to contest the application or not; and if they decide to do so, to take further steps needed to repulse it.

On the other hand, the imposition on the ERC to consider the comments of the customers and the LGUs concerned extends the comforting assurance that their interest will be taken into account in any rare increase proposal. Indeed, the requirements address the right of the consuming public to due process and at the same advance the cause of people empowerment.

The system of interim rates cannot be dispensed with, since it helps ensure the financial viability of a public utility. The system, however, may be abused to the detriment of the consumers, if safeguards are not put in place. It has happened many times that after the provisional rare increase had been granted, no action on the main Petition was taken; or if one was taken, it was made only after the lapse of a long period of time. The ultimate effect of the inaction or delay was to make the provisional rare virtually permanent. ‘Thus, the consumers were made to pay what effectively evolved to be the permanent rate, without the benefit of a hearing. In the meantime, the collections on the provisional rate were spent by the utility.

Clearly, therefore, although these new requirements are procedural in character, they represent significant reforms in public utility regulation, as they engender substantial benefits to the consumers. It is in this light that the new requirements should be appreciated and their observance enforced.

## Public Utility Consumers

In Spouses Quisumbing v. Meralco, the Court clarified that a public utility — in this case, Meralco — had no legal right to disconnect the consumer’s electrical supply immediately, without observing the requisites of law. Before being held liable for their allegedly illegal consumption of electricity, consumers should be given the opportunity to controvert any initial finding thereon.

“One cannot deny the vital role which a public utility such as MERALCO, having a monopoly of the supply of electrical power in Metro Manila and some nearby municipalities, plays in the life of people living in such areas. Electricity has become a necessity to most people in these areas, justifying the exercise by the State of its regulatory power over the business of supplying electrical service to the public, in which petitioner MERALCO is engaged. ‘Thus, the state may regulate, as it has done through Section 97 of the Revised Order No. 1 of the Public Service Commission, the conditions under which and the manner by which a public utility such as MERALCO may effect a disconnection of service to a delinquent customer. Among others, a prior written notice to the customer is required before disconnection of the service. Failure to give such prior notice amounts to a tort.”

In balancing the interests of businesses and consumers, the Court succinctly said that “there is a right way to do the right thing at the right time for the right reason.”

In Meralco v. Macrotextile Mills, the defects in an electric meter were allowed to continue indefinitely, until the public utility suddenly demanded payment for the unrecorded electric consumption. Considering that Meralco could have remedied the situation immediately, the Supreme Court ruled that the company should bear the loss. ‘The Court reasoned that ignoring Meralco’s failure to act promptly might encourage neglect by public utilities, to the detriment of the consuming public.

## Credit Card Transactions

Since their introduction into the United States 1 the 1950s, transactions involving credit cards have been a fast-growing category of credit. In the Philippines, they have become a convenient staple tool for consumers. A plethora of cases has consequently arisen from this mode of payment.

In BPI Express Card v. Olalia, a credit card company was collecting unpaid charges made on an extension card. However, the consumer denied ever having applied for, much less received, such card. The Court found that, indeed, he had never applied for an extension card in the name of his wife. Furthermore, it also found that the credit card company had also failed to show any receipt for any fee given in payment of and for the purpose of securing an extension card. ‘Thus, the Court ruled that the consumer should not be held liable for the purchases made on the irregularly issued extension card used by an unauthorized party.

Contracts of this nature are contracts of adhesion, so-called because their terms are prepared by only one party, while the mere affixation of the signature of the other signifies adhesion thereto. As such, their terms are construed strictly against the party who drafted it. In this case, it was the credit card company that required the procedures for the application for an extension card; thus, it had the task of showing vigilance over compliance therewith.

In Ermitaño v. C.A, the card holder complied with the requirement to immediately notify the credit card company of the loss of the card. According to the Credit (Card Contract, for the card holder to be absolved from liability for unauthorized purchases made on a card holder's lost or stolen card, two steps must be followed: (1) the card holder must give written notice to the company; and (2) the company must notify its member-establishments of such loss or theft — a step that it can take only upon receipt of a notice from the card holder.

Having thus performed her part in the notification procedure, the consumer had reason to expect that the credit card company would perform its part — immediate notification of its member-establishments. Clearly, the company failed to promptly notify the establishment in which the unauthorized purchases had been made with the use of the lost card. Hence, it must bear the consequences of the loss. Said the Court:

“Prompt notice by the cardholder to the credit card company of the loss or theft of his card should be enough to relieve the former of any liability occasioned by the unauthorized use of his lost of stolen card. The questioned stipulation in this case, which still requires the cardholder to wait until the credit card company has notified all its mem- 3 ber-establishments, puts the cardholder at the mercy of the credit card company which may delay indefinitely the notification of its 4 members to minimize if not to eliminate the possibility of incurring any loss from unauthorized purchases. Or, as in this case, the credit card company may for some reason fail to promptly notify its members through absolutely no fault of the cardholder.

To require the cardholder to still pay for unauthorized purchases after he has given prompt notice of the loss or theft of his card to the credit card company would simply be unfair and unjust. The Court cannot give its assent to such a stipulation which could clearly run against public policy.”

Moreover, in Mandarin Villa v. CA, the Court ruled that negligence could also be committed by the establishment in which the credit card had been used or, in this case, declined. The Point of Sales Guidelines in the case required the establishment to check the expiry date embossed on the card itself; if the card was unexcited, the store had to honor it. In this case, the consumer's credit card had not yet expired when it was wrongfully dishonored by the establishment/defendant. Hence, the latter did not use the reasonable care and caution that a prudent person would have used in the same situation.

Interestingly, the establishment in this case even argued that the proximate cause of the alleged damage to the consumer was his own negligence in not bringing sufficient cash. Of course this argument was such a lame excuse and so patently absurd that I need not even bother to answer it.

## Contracts and Interests

In contracts of adhesion, as already stated, one of the contracting parties imposes a ready-made contract that the other party may accept or reject, but cannot modify. One party prepares the contract in its entirety, while the other party merely signs or “adheres” to it, giving no room for negotiation and depriving the latter of the opportunity to bargain on equal footing,”

The binding effect of any agreement between parties to such a contract is premised on two settled principles: (1) that any obligation arising from the contract has the force of law between the parties; and (2) that there must be mutuality based on the parties’ essential equality. One major consumer issue in this kind of contract is the interest rate imposed on the borrower.

Although neither the law nor the courts will extricate a party from an unwise or undesirable contract he or she has entered into with all the required formalities and with full awareness: of its consequences, courts are still given the discretion to reduce excessive or unconscionable interest rates. Article 1229 of the Civil Code is clear that “even if there has been no performance, the penalty may also be reduced by the courts if it is iniquitous or [imposes] unconscionable interest rates.”

It is important to stress that the Court is not pre-clouded from ruling out blind adherence to the terms of an adhesion contract, if the attendant facts and circumstances show that such a contract should be ignored for being obviously too one-sided.

In Development Bank of the Philippines v. CA, the (Court noted that the interests paid by private respondents were more than the principal obligation that they owed. Moreover, the additional interest alone amounted to almost half of what had already been paid by them. Thus, the Court equitably reduced the said interest payment.

In Segovia Development Corp. v. Dumatol Realty, the contracts showed that the three percent (3%) penalty interest on unpaid monthly installments would translate t0 a yearly penalty interest of thirty-six percent (36%).

However, instead of eliminating the interest payment, the Court said that a reduction was more consistent with fairness and equity. It took into consideration the fact that the defendant had remained an unpaid seller; and that it had suffered, one way or another, from the nonperformance by the consumer of the latter’s contractual obligations.

While the Usury Law ceiling on interest rates was lifted by Central Bank Circular No. 905, nothing in this Circular granted lenders carte blanche authority to raise interest rates to levels that would either enslave their borrower or lead to a hemorrhaging of the latter’s assets.

## Truth in Lending Act

In the very recent case New Sampaguita Builders Construction v. Philippine National Bank, which I had the honor of writing about, the Court asserted its authority to strike down provisions in promissory notes that would grant leaders unrestrained power to increase interest rates, penalties and other charges at their sole discretion and without giving prior notice to and securing the consent of borrow.

That case was of utmost importance because the Court declared therein, in unequivocal terms and probably for the first time, that “the time is now ripe to give teeth to the often ignored forty-one-year old “Truth in Lending Act’ and thus transform it from a snivelling paper tiger to a growling financial watchdog of hapless borrower.”

The Court made it clear that when the borrower was not clearly informed of the disclosure statements prior to the consummation of the availment or drawdown, the effect was that the lender would have no right to collect upon penalty charges or increases thereof, even if! stipulated in the promissory notes. This is the essence of the Truth in Lending Act. Excessive interests, penalties and other charges not revealed in disclosure statements issued by banks — even if stipulated in the promissory notes — cannot be given effect.

In the same case, the Court also voided a uniform clause that permitted the lender to increase the interest rate “within the limits allowed by law at any time depending on whatever policy it may adopt in the future,” without even giving prior notice to the borrowers. In the words of the Court, “ilt]t would be the zenith of farcicality to specify and agree upon rates that could be subsequently upgraded at whim by only one party to the agreement.”

## Sales Transactions

Although it is not the function of the Supreme (Court to impose its own wisdom on sellers and buyers, the law gives it the authority to protect consumers by ensuring that what the buyer purchases is indeed what the seller has warranted it to be. In fact, the Civil Code of the Philippines has devoted an entire section on sales warranted — whether express or implied.

In Harrison Motors Corp. v. Nazario, the seller sold certain automobiles, the taxes and customs duties on which had not yet been paid. The Bureau of Internal Revenue (BIR) went after the buyer. According to the Court, although it was true that the ownership of the automobiles shifted to the buyer after the sale, it was equally true that prior to its consummation, the seller expressly intimated to the buyer that it had already paid the taxes and customs duties. Such representation was considered as the seller’s express warranty under Article 1546 of the Civil Code.

In Del Rosario v CA, the seller was engaged in the business of selling roofing materials. Through its brochures and extensive advertisements in media, it made representations with respect to the durability of its tiles and the sturdiness of roofing installed in accordance with its specified method. However, barely two months after completion of the installation of a roof by its workers, portions thereof were blown away by a typhoon. The Court ruled that the seller had acted in bad faith and/or/or with gross negligence for failing to deliver the necessary accessories for the’ proper installation of the structure at the buyer’s residence; and for instead installing inferior roofing materials in violation of the proper installation procedure expressly specified in the seller's brochures and advertisements.

## Airline Passengers

Equally important during these times is the business of common carriers, especially airline companies. The (Court has repeatedly declared that such business is im-bred with public interest and duty.

In Savellano v. Northwest Airlines, we outlined an airline company’s responsibility towards its passengers. The Court ruled that when — as a result of engine malfunction — a commercial airline is unable to ferry its passenger on the original contracted route — it nonetheless had the duty of fulfilling its responsibility of caring, them to their contracted destination on the most convenient route possible. Failing in this effort, it cannot just unilaterally shuttle them — without their consent — to other routes or stopping places outside of the contracted sectors.

However, the Court did not award moral damage, as there was no proof of the carrier’s bad faith, ill will, malice or wanton conduct. Neither did it award actual damages, because convincing and timely proof of loss was absent. But it did award nominal damages to the passengers for the inconvenience they had suffered, and to vindicate and recognize their right to be notified and consulted before a change in their contracted stopping place.

The Court also recognizes the responsibility of a ticket-issuing airline, even if the latter has endorsed the passenger to another carrier. In China Airlines v. Chiok, which I also had the honor of writing, the Court has ruled that a ticket-issuing airline is the principal in a contract of carriage, while the endorsee-airline is the agent. Thus, any negligence on the part of the endorse-airline is still the responsibility of the ticket-issuing airline. A common carrier has a peculiar relationship with its passengers and an exacting responsibility to them. For reasons of public interest and policy, the ticket issuing-airline acts as the principal in a contract of carriage and is thus liable for the acts as well as the omissions of any errant carrier to which it may have endorsed any sector of the en continuous trip.

In this case, although the failure of the endorsee airline to transport the passenger was due to a typhoon, such failure did not terminate the carrier's responsibility to its passengers. The passenger in this case was harangued and prevented from boarding the original and the replacement flights. The airline company thus breached its duty to transport him.

Also in this case, the Court reminded airline companies that because of the nature of their business, they must not merely give cursory instructions to their personnel — but require them — to be more accommodating towards their customers and the public.

## Conclusion

In the last 30 minutes, I have endeavored to show that under the authority granted to it by the Constitution and the laws, the Supreme Court has played its part in protecting consumers. The old adage caveat emptor no longer strictly applies to consumer transactions. On the contrary, the seller must equally beware. Indignation over fraudulent, unfair and iniquitous consumer practices will no longer be tolerated; the Supreme Court will be ever ready to stand in the middle to protect the interests of consumers.

I thank you.
