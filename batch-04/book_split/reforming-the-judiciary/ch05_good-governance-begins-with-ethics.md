# Chapter 5: Good Governance Begins with Ethics

Relevant to our conference theme today is the “YoungBlood” column, which appeared in the May 16, 2002 issue of the Philippine Daily Inquirer. Entitled “I want to be a lawyer,” it was written by Joan A. de Venecia, a 21-year-old freshman at the UP College of Law. I quote her as follows:

“Admittedly, lawyering and lawyers in particular, have had bad press. From being the butt of Internet jokes, to the informal survey of the typical man on the street, lawyers have been depicted as ‘guns for hire’ and greedy bastards with no scruples and a lot less actual skill. To the question, ‘What do you call 10,000 lawyers drowned in the sea?’ the famous reply is ‘a good start.’”

May I also relate to you a little anecdote about a lawyer who was asked by a judge, "Is there any truth to the accusation that you kicked your wife?" The attorney vehemently protested, "That is absolutely false, Your Honor."

"I'll ask you once more, this time with a reminder that you are testifying under oath. Are you categorically saying that you did not kick your wife?" The lawyer then answered, "Well, under oath, 1 am categorically saying that that is absolutely false. She is not my wife!"

Ladies and gentlemen, let me now be serious. In a report entitled "Combating Corruption in the Philippines," the World Bank noted a Social Weather Stations survey release in February 2000, saying that "62 percent of respondents believed that there were significant levels of corruption within the judiciary; 65 percent x x x believed that 'many' or 'most' lawyers could be bribed, while 57 percent thought the same about judges."

Apart from perceived corruption, there are other accusations that have been hurled at judges and lawyers, deliberate delay in court proceedings, incompetence, arrogance, neglect of duty, conduct unbecoming a member of the bench or the legal profession, partiality and immorality.

Truly, there are many complaints against the legal and the judicial professions. But before we go any further, let me quickly assure you of two things.

First, while there are some undesirable judges and lawyers, the vast majority are honest, competent, hardworking and God-fearing.

Second, the Supreme Court has taken notice of the problems I mentioned earlier and has firmly instituted a wide-ranging reform program to address them.

Upon taking over as head of the judiciary on November 30, 1998, Chief Justice Hilario G. Davide Jr. announced his reform program entitled "Davide Watch." It envisions a "judiciary that is independent, effective and efficient and worthy of public trust and confidence; and a legal profession that provides quality, ethical, accessible and cost-effective legal services to our people x x x."

The Davide Program for the judiciary and the legal profession is not meant to be merely a broad statement of intent and principles. It is implemented day after day as an ongoing Action Program for Judicial Reform (APJR). Notably, the program is actively supported not only by the entire Philippine judiciary, but by the national government and local donor groups as well. It is assisted also by international development bodies like the United Nations Development Fund (UNDP), the World Bank, the Asian Development Bank (ADB), UNICEF, the Asia Foundation; and government-to-government assistance from several countries including Japan, Canada, Australia, the United States and the Netherlands.

The judicial reforms I speak of are pervasive, students and law schools and involve such topics as how to improve the curriculum, upgrade the bar examinations, grant financial assistance to bright but poor law students, and provide professorial chairs and endowments to law schools. The reforms extend to the rigid screening and appointment to the judiciary of only the best and the brightest, the strict enforcement of professional and judicial ethics, the provision of continuing legal education for both judges and lawyers, and the rigorous monitoring of the performance of all judicial personnel.

All told, the Supreme Court is trying its best to change mind sets, modernize procedures, and speed up the delivery of quality justice by a corps of competent, dedicated, honest and courageous judges. These reform policies are aimed at creating stability in the rule of law and predictability in the rendition of decisions - conditions that are indispensable to economic development and, ultimately, to good governance. To be sure, I have discussed in some detail the progress of the foregoing pursuits in three books I have written over the last three years. Understandably, I cannot exhaustively expound on all of them in the twelve minutes given me for this address.

Let me just emphasize that the central theme of this reform program is a clarion call to ethics. The Supreme Court has become increasingly strict in enforcing the Code of Judicial Conduct for judges and the Code of Professional Responsibility for lawyers. While all public officials are duty-bound to observe the Code of Conduct and Ethical Standards for public officials enunciated in RA 6713, the Supreme Court has opted to enforce its own codes of ethics even more strictly as they apply to judges and lawyers. Indeed, it has taken unprecedented steps to discipline members of the judiciary who fall short of the standards demanded by ethics.

Thus, for the first time in its 100-year history, the Supreme Court has censured one of its own members, dismissed from service a Court of Appeals justice, and suspended the presiding magistrate of t h e Sandiganbayan from his administrative functions. Since Chief Justice Davide took office on November 30, 1998, the High Tribunal has disrobed more than 20 lower court judges; fined more than a hundred; and suspended, admonished or otherwise disciplined more than two hundred others.

But weeding out, suspending or fining the undesirables in the judiciary is not enough. What is even more important is to attract and recruit the best and the brightest lawyers to join it. Only when we have competent, hard-working and ethical lawyers in the judiciary can we expect speedy quality justice.

At present, however, more than one third of all trial courts are vacant. This is because of low compensation for judicial posts in general and security concerns in certain areas of the country. Regional trial court judges, who form the backbone of the judiciary, are currently paid P25,333 basic monthly salaries; Court of Appeals and Sandiganbayan magistrates, ₽27,500; and Supreme Court justices, P38,500. Some secretaries, clerks or assistants in the big and progressive companies in Makati get higher salaries. Indeed, judicial compensation must be rationalized. For this to happen, the judiciary needs the passage of two bills in the legislature: one, for real fiscal independence; and two, for exemption from the Salary Standardization Law.

Although the Constitution accords it fiscal independence, the judiciary in actual practice still depends on the political branches of government for its budget. Worse, the total judicial budget has shrunk from about two percent of the national budget in the early 90s to less than one percent in the current year. Ideally, Congress should just appropriate a lump sum - say, two percent of the national budget —and then let the High Tribunal allocate the money to various judicial services. In this manner, real fiscal independence would be assured. And upon exemption from the Salary Standardization Law, judicial officials can be paid properly without any need for additional appropriation.

As it is now, judges are given so-called “allowances” or “expense reimbursements” by the local government units — cities, provinces and towns - where they are located. But I believe even these are not appropriate, because LGUs and their officials - the mayors and the governors — have occasional court cases. Thus, suspicions of bias or unethical conduct persist. Besides, these additional compensations are granted unevenly. They depend to a large measure on the LGU's financial capacity as well as on the generosity of local officials.

Two years ago, after I spoke of the financial problems of the judiciary during a meeting of the Management Association of the Philippines (MAP), a sympathetic business leader approached me. He offered to raise funds for the judiciary by asking the top 1,000 companies in the Philippines to donate one percent of their net profits similar to what they were doing for the Philippine Business for Social Progress. While I appreciated the good intention behind the proposal, I replied that such an arrangement — while it could solve our financial problem - might compromise judicial independence, especially in labor, corporate or tax cases brought to us.

A similar caution was aired by a journalist during an open forum at the Manila Overseas Press Club, after I had delivered a speech saying that the World Bank, the ADB and other international agencies were offering assistance to enable the Supreme Court to implement its reform program. The journalist asked: "Will the Supreme Court's independence and integrity not be compromised if it accepts assistance from these agencies, considering that they espouse certain policies like free trade and globalization?" I replied that we were aware of these constraints, but that the assistance was really going to be given to the national government, which would turn it over to the judiciary without any conditions or "strings attached."

In sum, may I say that the Supreme Court has been quite forthright and steadfast in enforcing ethical standards to discipline judges and attorneys. Moreover in its reform program, it has stressed strict adherence to ethics and faithful devotion to excellence. All the essential traits expected of judges and lawyers — integrity, independence, intelligence and industry, which I call the four "ins" of a good judge — are encapsulated in ethical conduct. Indeed, quality justice and good governance always begin with ethics.

I thank you.
