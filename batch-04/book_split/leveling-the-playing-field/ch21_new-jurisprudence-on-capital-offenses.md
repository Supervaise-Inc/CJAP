# Chapter 21: New Jurisprudence on Capital Offenses

As in my previous books, I will now take up the latest jurisprudence on death penalty cases decided by the Court from October 10, 2003, to October 10, 2004, the period marking my ninth year in office.

2. Total number of cases in which the death penalty was affirmed	32

The aforementioned figures more or less reflect the overall picture from the time the Supreme Court te-viewed capital cases from December 31, 1993 — when the Death Penalty Law took effect — to the present. In short, less than one third of such cases have been affirmed, a statistic that reflects the special assiduousness of the Court in passing upon these cases. Verily, it left no stone unturned in ensuring that the rights of the — accused were well protected before and during the time of their arrest, up to their trial, until judgment was rendered.

## Death Penalty Cases to Be Reviewed by the Court of Appeals

The most significant death penalty Decision handed down by the Court during my ninth year in office was People v. Mateo,’ which held it “wise and compelling” to have the Court of Appeals (CA) review all judgments on heinous crimes before they were elevated to the Supreme Court. Mateo was Justice Jose C. Vitug's valedictory ponencia, which was promulgated one week before his retirement. Wrote the esteemed magistrate:

“While the Fundamental Law requires a mandatory review by the Supreme Court of cases where the penalty imposed is reedysion perpetua, life imprisonment, or death, nowhere, however, has it proscribed an intermediate review. If only to ensure utmost circumspection before the penalty of death, reclusion perpetua or life imprisonment is imposed, the Court now deems it wise and compelling to provide in these cases a review by the Court of Appeals before the case is elevated to the Supreme Court. Where life and liberty are at stake, all possible avenues to determine his guilt or innocence must be accorded to an accused, and no care in the evaluation of the facts can ever be overdone. A prior determination by the Court of Appeals on, particularly, the factual issues, would minimize the possibility of an error of judgment. If the Court of Appeals should affirm the penalty of death, reclusion perpetua, or life imprisonment, it could then render judgment imposing the corresponding penalty as the circumstances warrant, refrain from entering judgment and elevate the entire records of the case to the Supreme Court for its final disposition.”

Consistent with Mateo, the Court promulgated AM No. 00-5-03-SC on September 28, 2004. This en banc Resolution amended Sections 3 and 10 of Rule 122, as well as 12 and 13 of Rule 124, of the Revised Rules of Criminal Procedure as follows:

“Rule 122

“Sec. 3. How appeal taken — (a) The appeal to the Regional Trial Court, or to the Court of Appeals in cases decided by the Regional Trial Court in the exercise of its original jurisdiction, shall be by notice of appeal filed with the court which rendered the judgment or final order appealed from and by serving a copy thereof upon the adverse party.

“(b) The appeal to the Court of Appeals in cases decided by the Regional Trial Court in the exercise of its appellate jurisdiction shall be by petition for review under Rule 42.

“(c) The appeal in cases where the penalty imposed by the Regional Trial Court is reclusion perpetua, life imprisonment or where a lesser penalty is imposed for offenses committed on the same occasion or which arose out of the same occurrence that gave rise to the more serious offense for which the penalty of death, reclusion perpetua, or life imprisonment is imposed, shall be by notice of appeal to the Court of Appeals in accordance with paragraph (a) of this Rule.

“(d) No notice of appeal is necessary in cases where the Regional Trial Court imposed the death penalty. The Court of Appeals shall automatically review the judgment as provided in Section 10 of this Rule. (3a)

x x x

“Sec. 10. Transmission of records in case of death penalty. — In all cases where the death penalty is imposed by the trial court, the records shall be forwarded to the Court of Appeals for automatic review and judgment within twenty days but not earlier than fifteen days from the promulgation of the judgment or notice of denial of a motion for new trial or reconsideration. The transcript shall also be forwarded within ten days after the filing thereof by the stenographic reporter. (10a)

x x x

“Rule 124

“Sec. 12. Power to receive evidence. — The Court of Appeals shall have the power to try cases and conduct hearings, receive evidence and perform all acts necessary to resolve factual issues raised in cases falling within its original and appellate jurisdiction, including the power to grant and conduct new trials or further proceedings. Trials of hearings in the Court of Appeals must be continuous and must be completed within three months, unless extended by the Chief Justice. 12(a)

“Sec. 13. Certification or appeal of case to the Supreme Court. — (a) Whenever the Court of Appeals finds that the penalty of death should be imposed, the court shall render judgment but refrain from making an entry of judgment and forthwith certify the case and elevate its entire record to the Supreme Court for review.

“(b) Where the judgment also imposes a lesser penalty for offenses committed on the same occasion or which arose out of the same occurrence that gave rise to the more severe offense for which the penalty of death is imposed, and the accused appeals, the appeal shall be included in the case certified for review to the Supreme Court.

“(c) In cases where the Court of Appeals imposes reclusion perpetua, life imprisonment or a lesser penalty, it shall render and enter judgment imposing such penalty. The judgment may be appealed to the Supreme Court by notice of appeal filed with the Court of Appeals.”

I voted in favor of Mateo (the vote was in fact unanimous) for many reasons that I did not write in a Separate Opinion anymore. However, let me now express them in this book in nonlegal, easy-to-understand English.

First. For all those who oppose the death penalty, Mateo is welcome news, because it gives death convicts an intermediate layer of review to assure an error-free judgment. If those convicted of lesser — as distinguished from “heinous” — crimes are given the opportunity for multiple review in the entire four-level judicial hierarchy, why should death convicts be denied the same privilege? If those charged with simple theft can have access to municipal courts, with rights of appeal to regional trial courts and the Court of Appeals, why should the same protection be denied to those on whom the capital penalty has been imposed?

Second. In these capital cases, the main issue is almost always factual in nature — did the appellants commit the crime attributed to them? As every law student knows, the Supreme Court is not a trier of facts. As a rule, it does not review the factual findings of the lower courts. Upon the other hand, the Court of Appeals is specifically mandated to pass upon factual conclusions of trial courts. Hence, it has developed expertise in this regard. For the sake of truth and fairness then, a factual review by the CA should assure a more prudent dispensation of justice.

Third. Since the Death Penalty Law (Republic Act No. 7659) became effective on December 31, 1993, the Supreme Court has laid down sufficient jurisprudence to guide the Court of Appeals in reviewing such cases. In other words, during the last 10 years, the High Court has already covered almost every conceivable factual situa~ tion and has formulated doctrines needed to rule on them. Most of these doctrines have been discussed in many of my past books.

Fourth. In any event, by constitutional fiat, the Supreme Court will still automatically review all instances in which the Court of Appeals believes that the capital penalties imposed by the trial courts should be affirmed in full. In the words of Mateo, the CA “could render judgment imposing the corresponding penalty as the circumstances warrant, refrain from entering judgment and elevate the entire records of the case to the Supreme Court for its final determination.” Of course, acquittals are unappealable.

Fifth. Recently, a new law (Republic Act No. 8246) has increased the membership of the Court of Appeals from 51 to 69. The CA now has 23 Divisions composed of three justices each. Thus, it can readily tackle its new responsibility. True, Mateo may crowd the already heavy dockets of our brethren in the CA, but I trust that they would agree that this new Vitug masterpiece grants equal access to justice to those convicted of capital offenses, as discussed earlier.

Sixth. Finally, the Supreme Court will be given more time to attend to equally compelling cases involving the political, the social and the economic life of the country. The Court will be able to deliberate more deeply on the many novel disputes spawned by new economic paradigms, new Information Age problems, as well as new biotechnological and bioscientific developments. It will also be accorded enough opportunity to fast-track its Judicial Reform Program, especially the full computerization of the judiciary to ensure the speedy and transparent delivery of justice for all.

## Admissibility of DNA Evidence

As I reported in Reforming the Judiciary (2002 ed.), the Court in People v. Vallejo has already upheld the reliability of deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) testing in the Philippines. In that rape case, after the DNA analysis had shown that the DNA profile found in the vaginal smears of the victim was identical to the gene type of the accused, the identity of the latter as the culprit was established.

This year, in People v. Yatar — a case of rape with homicide — DNA evidence was used to establish that — the appellant was indeed guilty of the crime. The post mortem report indicated the presence of semen in the vaginal canal of the victim. Upon further analysis, it was discovered that the DNA of the sperm specimen matched the gene type of the appellant.

However, in an attempt to exclude the DNA evidence, he contended that the blood sample taken from him, as well as the DNA tests, was conducted in violation of his right to remain silent and of his right against self-incrimination. The Court rejected this argument and held thus:

“This connection is untenable. The kernel of the right is not against all compulsion, but against testimonial compulsion. The right against self-incrimination is simply against the legal process of extracting from the lips of the accused an admission of guilt. It does not apply where the evidence sought to be excluded is not an incrimination but a part of object evidence.

“We ruled in People v. Rondero that although accused-appellant insisted that hair samples were forcibly taken from him and submitted to the National Bureau of Investigation for forensic examination, the hair samples may be admitted in evidence against him, for what is proscribed is the use of testimonial compulsion or any evidence communicative in nature acquired from the accused under duress..

“Hence, a person may be compelled to submit to fingerprinting, photographing, paraffin, blood and DNA, as there is no testimonial compulsion involved. Under People v. Gallarde, where immediately after the incident, the police authorities took pictures of the accused without the presence of counsel, we ruled that there was no violation of the right against self-incrimination. The accused may be compelled to submit to a physical examination to determine his involvement in an offense of which he is accused.

## Search and Seizure at Airports Valid

The right of a person against unreasonable search and seizure is inviolable. Any evidence obtained in violation of this right will be inadmissible for any purpose in any proceeding.⁸ But a reasonable search is not forbidden. In People v. Suzuki,⁹ the Court validated the search and seizure conducted in accordance with routine airport security procedures. It emphasized that searches of such nature were reasonable, considering the increased security concerns at airports. It elucidated as follows:

“Persons may lose the protection of the search and seizure clause by exposure of their persons or property to the public in a manner reflecting a lack of subjective expectation of privacy, which expectation society is prepared to recognize as reasonable. Such recognition is implicit in airport security procedures. x x x. There is little question that such searches are reasonable, given their minimal intrusiveness, the gravity of the safety interests involved, and the reduced privacy expectations associated with airline travel. Indeed, travelers are often notified through airport public address systems, signs and notices in their airline tickets that they are subject to search and, if any prohibited materials or substances are found, such would be subject to seizure. These announcements place passenger on notice that ordinary constitutional protections against warrantless searches and seizures do not apply to routine airport procedure.”'”

Moreover, the Court stressed that whenever the right against unreasonable search and seizure is challenged, people may choose between invoking the constitutional protection and waiving their right by giving consent to the search or seizure. In Suzuki, the Court affirmed the findings of the trial court that the appellant had consented to the search. Consequently, when the search of his belongings revealed marijuana fruit tops, he was considered to have been caught in flagrante delicto. Thus, even without a warrant, his arrest was justified.

## No Complex Crime of “Robbery With Homicide and Assault Upon Authority”

In ordinary language, a special complex crime consists of more than one crime; but in the eyes of the law, it is only a single indivisible offense. While the Revised Penal Code specifically penalizes the special complex crime of “robbery with homicide,” the Court in People v. Otayde reversed the trial court’s finding that the appellants, in stealing from and killing a barangay captain, committed — the crime of “robbery with homicide and assault upon a person.” The Court held thus:

“There is no complex crime of ‘robbery with homicide and assault upon a person in authority.” The law is clear that, if the victim is killed on the occasion or by reason of a robbery, the offense becomes a special complex crime of robbery with homicide defined and penalized under Article 294 (1) of the Revised Penal Code. Even if the victim, as in this case, was a barangay captain and therefore a person in authority under Article 152 of the Revised Penal Code, the crime committed by appellants would still be robbery with homicide. As long as robbery is the main purpose and objective of the criminals and the killing merely results by reason or on the occasion of the robbery, the indictable offense is robbery with homicide.”

Furthermore, in determining the degree of participation of the accused, whenever homicide has been committed as a consequence of or on the occasion of robbery, all those who have taken part as principals in the robbery will also be held guilty as principals in the special complex crime of robbery with homicide, although they have not actually taken part in the homicide, unless they have clearly endeavored to prevent the killing. This rule, cited in Otayde, was reiterated in People v. Abes.

## The Preamble of the Information as Essential as Its Accusatory Part

The information should be so worded as to accord the accused their fundamental right to be informed of the nature and the cause of the accusation against them. In this regard, the Supreme Court carefully considers the allegations made in the information and ensures that all the vital information that would aid the accused in intelligently preparing for their defense are contained therein. For example, the Court adheres strictly to the rule mandating that qualifying and aggravating circumstances must be specifically alleged; otherwise such circumstances cannot be appreciated against the accused.

However, People v. Villanueva emphasized that the information must be read, not in sections or parts, but as one whole document. As such, the allegations contained in its introduction or preamble should be read together with the allegations in its accusatory part.

In Villanueva, the appellant argued that he could not be held guilty of qualified rape, since the qualifying circumstance of relationship had not been alleged in the accusatory part of the information. In rejecting this defense, the Court deemed it proper to “revisit and re examine” the rulings in People v. Bali-balita and People v. Rodriguez. It explained:

“There is no law or rule prescribing a specific location in the Information where the qualifying circumstances must “exclusively” be alleged before they could be appreciated against the accused.

x x x x

“Nothing in Secs. 6 and 8 of Rule 110 mandates that material allegations should be stated in the body and not in the preamble or caption of the Information. Instead, both sections state that as long as the pertinent and significant allegations are enumerated in the Information it would be deemed sufficient in form and substance. We hold that it is irrelevant and immaterial whether the qualifying circumstance of relationship is mentioned in the opening paragraph of the Information or in the second paragraph which alleges the acts constituting the crime charged since either paragraph is an integral part of the Information.

“The preamble or opening paragraph should not be treated as a mere aggroupment of descriptive words and phrases. It is as much an essential part of the Information as the accusatory paragraph itself. The preamble in fact complements the accusatory paragraph which draws its strength from the preamble. It lays down the predicate for the charge in general terms; while the accusatory portion only provides the necessary details. The preamble and the accusatory paragraph, together, form a complete whole that gives sense and meaning to the indictment. Thus, any circumstance stated in the preamble (i.e., minority, relationship) should also be considered as an allegation of such fact.

“Moreover, the opening paragraph bears the operative word ‘accuses,’ which sets in motion the constitutional process of notification, and formally makes the person being charged with the commission of the offense an accused. Verily, without the opening paragraph, the accusatory portion would be nothing but a useless and miserably incomplete narration of facts, and the entire Information would be a functionally sterile charge sheet; thus, making it impossible for the state to prove its case.

“The information sheet must be considered, not by sections or parts, but as one whole document serving one purpose, i.e., to inform the accused why the full panoply of state authority is being marshaled against him. Our task is not to determine whether allegations in an indictment could have been more artfully and exactly written, but solely to ensure that the constitutional requirement of notice has been fulfilled. Accordingly, the sufficiency of the allegations of qualifying circumstances therein must be judged objectively, and measured by practical considerations. Allegations of qualifying circumstances should not be declared insufficient merely by virtue of a perceived formal defect in their locations, which do not otherwise prejudice the substantial right of the accused. As long as they are adequately pleaded within the four corners of the charge sheet, as in the instant case, they could not be invalidated by the fact that they are found only in the introductory paragraph.

x x x

“Finally, were we to persist in the mistaken belief on the necessity of stating the qualifying circumstances strictly and exclusively in the accusatory paragraph of an Information, we would be placing premium on a highly technical and artificial rule of form, and completely sacrificing the substance, purpose and reason for the indictment. We believe that this requirement is without any corresponding benefit to the interest of justice. On the contrary, it is only bound to unduly burden our prosecutorial agencies and, worse, provide criminals with a convenient avenue to elude the punishment they truly deserve.”

## Caution Necessary When the Right to Present Evidence Is Waived by the Accused

Due process in criminal cases includes the right of -the accused to present evidence to support their innocence and the right to cross-examine the witnesses presented against them. The courts cannot simply overlook these rights, especially when the penalty imposable is the deprivation of life. The Supreme Court emphasized this point in People v. Macarang'’ and People v. Beriber,” when it remanded these cases to the trial courts for fur~ ther proceedings.

Annoyed by the numerous postponements of the case, the trial judge in Macarang issued a warning against the defense counsel that failure to present evidence in the next hearing would be considered a waiver of the latter's right to do so. When the appellant “was still not ready” to present evidence by then, the judge deemed the case submitted for decision on the basis only of the prosecution evidence. Thereafter, the appellant was held guilty of the crime charged, for which the penalty of death was imposed.

Even if the objective of the judge was to instill discipline in the litigants and their counsel so that undue delays would be prevented, the Court nonetheless held that trial courts should be more circumspect when the accused waived their right to present evidence. It gave judges the following reminder:

“x x x [I]n criminal cases where the imposable penalty may be death, as in the present cases, the presiding judge is called upon to see to it that the accused is made aware of the consequences of not heeding the warning given by the trial court. It must be noted that the waiver of the right to present defense evidence in the present cases was not even voluntary nor upon the instance of the appellant but imposed by the trial court, apparently to penalize appellant, after he and his counsel repeatedly moved for the postponements of the scheduled hearings.

Moreover, the Court was not satisfied that in the same Order declaring that the appellant had waived his right to present evidence, the trial judge granted the motion of his counsel to withdraw also his appearance. It held that the trial court was not justified in rendering judgment against the accused and in sentencing him to suffer the penalty of death without first ensuring that he was aware of the consequences of the waiver of his right to present evidence; and without exerting any effort to ask him if he would like to be represented by another lawyer chosen by him or through the assistance of the Public Attorney’s Office (PAO), or through a counsel de officio appointed by the court.

The Supreme Court gave a similar exhortation in Beriber. In that case, the defense counsel failed to cross-examine five of the six prosecution witnesses and voluntarily waived the right of the accused to present dence. ‘The Court warned:

“A waiver by the accused of his right to present evidence should never be taken lightly and should always be subjected to careful scrutiny by the court. To be upheld as valid, it must be established that the waiver is made voluntarily, knowingly, intelligently and with sufficient awareness of the relevant circumstances and possible consequences.

x x x

“Although there is no specific provision in the law requiring the trial court to conduct an inquiry into the voluntariness of an accused’s waiver of the right to present evidence, the circumstances of the present case, the gravity of the imposable penalty and the plea of ‘not guilty’ entered by the accused should have prompted the trial court to conduct a thorough inquiry into the reasons behind such waiver, the voluntariness thereof, and the sufficiency of appellant’s knowledge and understanding of the effects of his waiver.”

## A Searching Inquiry Upon a Plea of Guilty

The Revised Rules of Criminal Procedure provides that when a plea of guilty is made to a capital charge, the court shall conduct a searching inquiry into its voluntariness and full comprehension by the accused of the consequences of their plea, and shall require the prosecution to prove their guilt and the precise degree of their culpability.

This rule was overlooked by the trial court in People v. Besonia. Holding that the three-page, double-spaced Decision “leaves much to be desired,” the Supreme Court explained:

“It must be stressed that a plea of guilty is only supporting evidence or secondary basis for a finding of culpability, the main proof being the evidence presented by the prosecution to prove the accused’s guilt beyond reasonable doubt. Once an accused charged with a capital offense enters a plea of guilty, a regular trial shall be conducted just the same as if no such plea was entered. The court cannot, and should not, relieve the prosecution of its duty to prove the guilt of the accused and the precise degree of his culpability by the requisite quantum of evidence. The reason for such rule is to preclude any room for reasonable doubt in the mind of the trial court, or the Supreme Court on review, as to the possibility that the accused might have misunderstood the nature of the charge to which he pleaded guilty, and to ascertain the circumstances attendant to the commission of the crime which may justify or require either a greater or lesser degree of severity in the imposition of the prescribed penalties.”

It observed in Besonia that the trial court and the prosecution had unduly relied on the plea of guilty made by the accused and on his admissions during the searching inquiry. It found that the prosecution “was lacking in assiduity that is necessarily expected in a prosecution for a capital offense”; thus, the case was remanded to the trial court for further proceedings.

## Effect of a Second Custodial Investigation on the Infirmities of the First One

In People v. Mojello, the Court was not unanimous in deciding whether a second custodial investigation had cured the violations committed during the initial investigation of the appellant. It must be noted that the investigation was conducted on December 17, 1996; and the second, six days later on December 23, 1996.

A majority of the justices held that in determining whether both investigations had been conducted in accordance with the stringent standards of the law on custodial investigation, the two should be treated separately. The Court found that while the first investigation had clearly violated the right of the appellant to counsel, the second one was not tainted with the same infirmity.

Dissenting from the majority, Justice Leonardo A. Quisumbing explained thus:

“The majority opinion concedes that the first custodial interrogation was constitutionally infirm because of the absence of counsel. But the ponencia considers the succeeding interrogation lawful, without any infirmity, despite appellant’s claim that he was boxed and mauled by the police. The ponencia holds that the police complied with the Miranda requirements, given the fact that the presence of a counsel was finally secured, and that the content of the extrajudicial confession which appellant signed was translated into Visayan dialect, which he understood, before he affixed his signature thereon. On this point, we cannot agree. To our mind, facts on record do not show compliance with the Miranda mandates aforecited. On the contrary, the facts unmistakably show a violation by the police of Section 17 of the Bill of Rights x x x ‘no person shall be compelled to be a witness against himself’

“As the record shows, at the very start of the custodial investigation, appellant was not assisted by counsel. Nor was he informed of his right to counsel from the time the police started to extract information from him regarding the crime that he was charged with. Much less did he waive his right to counsel in writing. What happened was contrary to the prevailing doctrine that the accused under custodial interrogation must continuously have a counsel assisting him from the very start thereof.

“In this case, appellant Mojello’s first signed statement on December 17, 1996, taken illegally and involuntarily, without the presence of counsel, could not be admitted in evidence. Neither should his flawed second statement taken without assistance of his chosen counsel on December 23, 1996, be used against him. For the alleged confession of appellant is the undeniably unlawful consequence of the first instance of custodial interrogation without counsel, a classic case of a fruit of the poisonous tree.”

## Finger Insertion into the Vagina Considered a Sexual Assault

Construing paragraph 2 of Republic Act No. 8353 (“The Anti-Rape Law of 1997”), the Court in People v. Palma’ held that the insertion of appellant’s middle finger into the vagina of the victim constituted consummated rape through sexual assault.

## Appreciating Relationship as an Alternative Circumstance

On the question of whether the alternative circumstance of relationship should be appreciated, especially in death penalty cases, the Supreme Court spa in People v. Orilla as follows:

“The Revised Penal Code is silent as to when a relationship is mitigating and when it is aggravating. Jurisprudence considers relationship as an aggravating circumstance in crime against chastity. However, rape is no longer a crime against chastity for it is now classified as a crime against persons. The determination of whether an alternative circumstance is aggravating or not to warrant the death penalty cannot be left on a case-by-case basis. ‘The law must declare unequivocally an attendant circumstance as qualifying to warrant the imposition of the death penalty. The Constitution expressly provides that the death penalty may only be imposed for crimes defined as heinous by Congress. Any attendant circumstance that qualifies a crime as heinous must be expressly so prescribed by Congress.

x x x

“In People v. Catubig, we held that the alternative circumstance of relationship serves as basis for an award of exemplary damages because the term ‘aggravating circumstances’ must be understood in its broad or generic sense. However, this interpretation is only applicable to the civil aspect, not the criminal aspect of rape, which involves the imposition of the proper penalty. When the penalty to be imposed on the accused is teetering between reclusion perpetua and death, the term ‘aggravating circumstance’ in Article 63 of the Revised Penal Code must be understood in the strictness sense. The ‘aggravating circumstance’ that would spell the difference between life and death for the accused must be that specifically listed in Article 14 of the Revised Penal Code.

x x x

“However, we resort to the strict interpretation of the term ‘aggravating circumstance’ only for the purpose of imposing the death penalty. When the penalty to be imposed is a range of penalties where the maximum penalty is death and the appreciation of an aggravating circumstance would call for the imposition of the maximum penalty, which is death, the term ‘aggravating circumstance’ must be strictly construed. The aggravating circumstance sufficient to justify the imposition of the death penalty must not only be duly alleged and proven it must be one of those enumerated in Article 14 of the Revised Penal Code or that specified by law. In all other cases where the maximum penalty is not death, the term ‘aggravating circumstance’ must be interpreted in its broad or generic sense so as to include the alternative circumstances under Article 15 of the Revised Penal Code.”

## Pretrial Stipulations

In People v. Pepito, the Court stressed that admin-— sions made during pretrial are binding on the parties and must be given weight. Thus, it appreciated the circus. stances of minority and relationship based on the admissions that the victim and the accused had made during pretrial, even absent any presentation of a birth certificate or any other official document to support the allegations.

But in People v. Balbarona, the Court apparently took the opposite direction in resolving the same issue. Although it found the appellant guilty of the crime of rape, it did not impose the penalty of death, because the qualifying circumstances of minority and relationship were wanting. The Court, speaking through Justice Conchita Carpio Morales, held that “the circumstances that qualify a crime and increase its penalty to death cannot be the subject of stipulation.”

## Robbery with Rape Not Aggravated by the “Use of a Deadly Weapon”

The penalty of death is imposed when the crime of robbery with rape is attended by an aggravating circumstance. However, the Court clarified in People v. Carpio” that the “use of a deadly weapon” can be appreciated only in the crime of rape’ — not in the complex crime of robbery with rape, which is not among those provided in Article 14 (“Aggravating Circumstances”) or Article 15 (“Alternative Circumstances”) of the Revised Penal Code.

## Appreciating Age as an Element of Rape

In appreciating age as an element or a qualifying circumstance of rape, the Court held in People v. Macabata that in the absence of a birth certificate, the testimonies of the victim and her mother, as well as the admission of the appellant in his direct testimony, were sufficient proofs to establish the age of the victim.

The Court applied the same ruling to People v. Antivola. It declined to impose the penalty of death, because the appellant had not expressly admitted the testimony of the victim that she was less than seven years — old when he raped her.

On the other hand, the Court clarified in People v. Estrada that even if the counsel of the appellant had admitted the date of birth of the victim, “subject to the presentation of the certified copy of her birth certificate from the National Census,” the admission could not be appreciated if the prosecution failed to present the actual birth certificate as evidence.

## Baptismal Certificate as Proof of Filiation in Rape

The Court noted in People v. Cea that a baptisma certificate could not by itself be considered as proof of filiation, because it proved only the administration of the act of baptism on the date specified, not the veracity of the statements made therein regarding the relative or parents of the person baptized. However, when a baptismal certificate is coupled with the admission of the accused that he is the father of the victim, such document can be considered as sufficient proof of filiation.

## “Use of Explosives” as a Qualifying Circumstance

When a killing is attended by treachery and committed by “means of explosives,” the Court ruled in People v. Comadres® that the use of explosives shall be considered as the qualifying circumstance. It explained that “since the use of explosives is the principal mode of attack, reason dictates that this attendant circumstance should qualify the offense instead of treachery which will then be relegated merely as a generic aggravating circumstance.”

Moreover, the legal implications of Republic Act (RA) No. 8294** — when the crime is qualified as one committed “by means of explosion” under Article 248 of the Revised Penal Code — has been clarified by the Court thus:

“RA No. 8294 was a reaction to the onerous and anachronistic penalties imposed under the old illegal possession of firearms law, PD 1866, which prevailed during the tumultuous years of the Marcos dictatorship. The amendatory law was enacted, not to decriminalize illegal possession of firearms and explosives, but to lower their penalties in order to rationalize them into more acceptable and realistic levels.

“This legislative intent is conspicuously reflected in the reduction of the corresponding penalties for illegal possession of firearms, Of ammunitions and other related crime under the amendatory law. Under Section 2 of the said law, the penalties for unlawful possession of explosives are also lowered. Specifically, when the illegally possess explosives are used to commit any of the crimes under the Revised Penal Code, which result in the death of a person, the penalty is no longer death, unlike in P.D.D. No. 1866, but it shall be considered only as an aggravating circumstance.

“Clearly, Congress intended RA No. 8294 to reduce the penalty for illegal possession of firearms and explosives. Also, Congress clearly intended RA No. 8294 to consider as aggravating circumstance, instead of a separate offense, illegal possession of firearms and explosives when such possession is used to commit other crimes under the Revised Penal Code.

“It must be made clear, however, that RA No. 8294 did not amend the definition of murder under Article 248, but merely made the use of explosives an aggravating circumstance when resorted to in committing any of the crimes defined in the Revised Penal Code. The legislative purpose is to do away with the use of explosives as a separate crime and to make such use merely an aggravating circumstance in the commission of any crime already defined in the Revised Penal Code. Thus, RA No. 8294 merely added the use of unlicensed explosives as one of the aggravating circumstances specified in Article 14 of the Revised Penal Code. Like the aggravating circumstance of ‘explosion’ in paragraph 12, ‘evident premeditation’ in paragraph 13, or ‘treachery’ in paragraph 16 of Article 14, the new aggravating circumstance added by RA No. 8294 does not change the definition of murder in Article 248.”

Dissenting from the majority opinion, Justice Romeo J. Callejo Sr. opined that the killing should be qualified by the presence of treachery, not by the use of explosives. He explained:

“Under paragraph 3, Article 248 of the Revised Penal Code, as amended by Republic Act No. 7659, the use of explosive in killing a person is a circumstance which qualified the killing to murder, the imposable penalty for which is reclusion perpetua to death. When the crimes were committed by the appellants on August 6, 1995, Rep. Act No. 7659 was already in effect. But while the case was pending, Rep. Act No. 8294 was approved on June 6, 1997. Section 2 of the latter law provides that when a person commit any of the crimes defined in the Revised Penal Code with the use of explosives, detonation agents or incendiary devices which results in the death of any person or persons, the use of such explosives, etc. shall be considered as an aggravating circumstance.

“Paragraph 3 of Article 248 of the Revised Penal Code, as amended by Rep. Act No. 7659, was thus amended by Section 2 of Rep. Act No. 8294. Under the latter law, the use of a hand grenade in killing the victim was downgraded from being a qualifying circumstance to a mere generic aggravating circumstance. Considering that Section 2 of Rep. Act No. 8294 is favorable to the appellant, the same should be applied retroactively. Considering the factual milieu in this case, the generic aggravating circumstance of the use of explosives is absorbed by the qualifying circumstance of treachery.”

## Required Number of Votes to Render a Decision

Can the Court validly impose the death penalty if only seven justices of the Court, sitting en banc, deliberate on the case? This was the issue raised by the appellant in his Motion for Reconsideration of the Court's Decision in People v. Ebio. The assailed Decision convicted him of qualified rape and sentenced him to the death penalty.

According to the appellant’s Motion for Reconsideration, the Court lacked a quorum when it deliberated on the case, because only seven justices were present; only seven signed the Decision. The other seven were on official leave at the time.

Finding merit in his argument, the Court in a Resolution dated September 7, 2004, recalled its earlier Decision and resubmitted the case to the banc for re-deliberation. The Court held as follows:

“There is no question that the Court’s Decision in this case was concurred in by the majority of the members of the Court who actually took part in the deliberations. It was in fact unanimously signed by the seven Justices who were present during the deliberations. The issue now is whether the seven constitute a quorum of the 14-member Court.

“The term ‘quorum’ has been defined as ‘that number of members of the body which, when legally assembled in their proper places, will enable the body to transact its proper business, or, in other words, that number that makes a lawful body and gives it power to pass a law or ordinance or do any other valid corporate act. The question of the number of judges necessary to authorize the transaction of business by a court is as a general rule to be determined from the Constitution or statutory provisions creating and regulating the courts, and as a general rule a majority of the members of a court is a ‘quorum’ for the transaction of business and the decision of cases.”

“The Constitution is clear on the quorum when the Court meets by Division. There should be at least three members present for the Division to conduct its business. This may be deduced from paragraph 3 Section 4 Article VIII. There is no similar pronouncement, however, when the Court meets en banc. The second paragraph of Article VII Section 4 of the 1987 Constitution does not expressly state the number of Justices required to be present to constitute a quorum when the Court is composed of only fourteen members. In case of doubt in a criminal case, especially where the death penalty is imposed, the doubt should be resolved in favor of the accused.

“Thus, in this case, considering that the life of the accused is at stake, we deem it wise to resubmit the case to the Court en banc for re-deliberation.”

After a careful re-deliberation, with twelve justices present” and two on leave,” the Court unanimously voted to affirm the RTC’s finding of guilt and sentenced the appellant to the death penalty just the same.”
