# Transparency, Unanimity & Diversity -- Ch. 4: Paradigms Change But Values Endure

I thank Dr. Josefina R. Cortes, UE president and chief academic officer, as well as Law Dean Carlos M. Ortega for inviting me to be your commencement speaker. Graduation ceremonies bring back memories of my own student days some forty years ago.

As you may know, I finished my law degree from neighboring Far Eastern University (FEU). I have, however, fond memories of UE, because your school was the site of the 1960 bar examinations in which I was fortunate to be one of the topnotchers. You may want to know that I nearly did not take the said examinations because a few days before the first test was given, I became sick and had to be confined at the FEU Hospital, which was then located at N. Reyes Street just a block from here. I remember, on that first day, I had to walk over flooded R. Papa Street from the FEU Hospital to the UE gate in Lepanto Street, now known as Sergio Loyola Street. A typhoon hit the city and the street was knee-deep in water. I am grateful to my law dean at the time, Dr. Jovito R. Salonga who was later elected senate president of the Republic, for encouraging me to go on with the examinations in spite of illness and misfortune.

Let me congratulate all of you, dear graduates, on your victory at this turning point in your lives. Let me also felicitate your parents who, like my own father and mother, persevered and sacrificed hard-earned money to be able to provide their children with quality education, a reassuring first step in facing the real world out there.

## The Megashift to the Information Age

Dear graduates, you are indeed very special because you are finishing your schooling at the turn of the new century and the new millennium. In this sense, you should really be called millennial graduates, an honor that comes only once every one thousand years. As you leave the portals of your beloved UE, you will be stepping into a new era, a new civilization referred to as the information age or the e-age. According to John Naisbitt in his book Megatrends,' the world is now rapidly transforming — he calls it a “megashift” — from an industrial community to an information society. He explains that prosperity and economic well-being will depend no longer on machines and traditional industries but on the processing, the possession and the distribution of information. Significantly, Peter Drucker? adds that “the central workforce in the knowledge society will consist of highly specialized people.”

In the past, we read of success stories of great visionaries and entrepreneurs like John Rockefeller who became an icon because of petroleum, like Cornelius Vanderbilt because of the railroad, and like Henry Ford because of the automobile. But now we have new e-age pioneers who made their mega fortunes by taking advantage of the new opportunities offered by computers, the Internet and the dotcom revolution. William H. Gates, by founding and nurturing the computer software giant Microsoft, became the richest man in the world at age 45. His net worth is pegged at about $85 billion. His yearly earnings alone, estimated at $20 billion, are enough to completely fund the budget of the Philippines for the year 2000. Rivaling him are John Morgridge and John Chambers of Cisco Systems, Inc. which, some people claim, has now replaced Microsoft as the most valuable company in the world.

Alongside them are Gordon Moore and Bob Noyce, the men behind Intel, the world’s largest producer of microchips — the “brains” of the information age. Coming up very fast is 35-year-old Jeff Bezos, the chief executive of Amazon.com which promises to be the biggest retailer in the world without owning or Operating a single store or shopping center, but which will sell and deliver anything from anywhere via their Internet website. In the same e-age billionaires’ league are Stephen Case of America Online, Michael Dell of Dell Computer Corporation, and Charles Schwab of Charles Schwab Corporation.’

## Effect of the Information Age on Lives and Careers

In his book, The Road Ahead; Bill Gates explains that in the near future, miniature but extra-powerful computers as small as wallets will become available and will replace the many things people carry in their bags or use at home and in their offices. It will serve the function of money, credit card, checkbook, traveler’s check, address book, note pad, reading material, camera, TV, video recorder, tape recorder, cellular phone, pager, concert ticket, map, compass, calculator, thermometer, heart rate sensor, barometer, scanner and even a loud whistle to summon help.

Yes, cutting-edge technological innovations will have tremendous impact not only on your leisure but also on your careers. As young lawyers, dentists, thinkers, managers, scientists and other professionals, you will have to cope with fast-changing times.

## Law, Dentistry and Education

For lawyers, laws and court processes will radically change. To meet the challenges of paperless communications and scientific advances in ascertaining facts, court procedures and the rules of evidence will be amended radically. Electronic commerce and new economic paradigms like deregulation, privatization and liberalization will need new doctrines in commercial, political and international law. Recent discoveries in DNA, surrogate parenthood and human cloning will alter our traditional concepts of civil and criminal law.

In dentistry, new scientific and informational discoveries will render obsolete some of the dental equipment we know today. When I was a teenager some 50 years ago, I dreaded going to the clinic of the then young dentist Rufino Achacoso, because his pedalpowered dental drills frayed my nerves. These days I still see the graying Dr. Achacoso, but this time I am no longer nervous because he can bore holes in molars and imbed new teeth into jawbones without inflicting pain. I am told that one day soon, the dental surgeon we know today may become obsolete, because tooth decay and gum disease may be totally prevented.

The same is true with other professions. Science and information technology will accelerate knowledge, and you will need to keep up all the time. According to our Ambassador to the US, Ernesto M. Maceda,‘ this year alone 1.6 million new “infotech” jobs will be created by American companies. More than one half of them will be available to non-Americans. Essentially, the job categories are database and software developers, programmers, technical writers, digital media specialists and systems integrators. If you have not heard of these jobs, it is time to know them and in fact to specialize in them. Indeed, as the information revolution matures, new opportunities and higher standards in traditional ones will have to be met. In the face of these onslaughts on your careers, what advice can I offer you on your graduation day?

You may find this strange. But to meet the challenges of new paradigms, I advise you to retain, practise and internalize old values. This is because paradigms change but values endure. These values can be summarized by three Es: EXCELLENCE, ETHICS and ETERNITY. To help you remember I will coin an acronym, ExEE (pronounced “Excy”). Let me explain each one of these Es.

## Excellence and Self-fulfillment

Excellence is a value that demands the best performance from all of us all the time. True, not everyone has been endowed with the same amount of gray matter or artistic talent or motor skill or athletic prowess, but it is everyone’s responsibility to develop to the maximum whatever natural gift one has been given by the Lord. By becoming experts in our chosen lines or careers, we become self-confident and self-assured. Excellence in everything we say or do becomes our source of self-dignity, self-worth and self-fulfillment.

Excellence need not always be achieved with Latin honors during graduation. It can be attained even by the most ordinary graduate. It can be the product of enterprise arising from mundane activities, such as finding a need and filling it, or it can be the result of sheer passion. Let me give you an example.

In 3M, the giant US adhesive company, there was a very ordinary employee named Art Fry. During his off hours, he sang in a choir in his local church. He discovered to his dismay that the bits of paper used to mark the church hymns kept falling out of the books. He yearned for an adhesive-backed paper that would stick as long as necessary, but would leave no trace when removed. With this little background, he suggested to his employer the creation of this simple yellow paper device which is now called “Post It” note pad.’ Thus began a new $300 million business for 3M, and for Art Fry an instant place in the company’s honor roll.

## A Passion for Excellence

The foregoing story shows that excellence, innovation and creativity need not be sourced only from formal schooling. Verily, after these graduation ceremonies, most of you will no longer re-enroll to obtain another degree. Instead, you will face the real world. To advance in the information society, however, you will need to constantly study and specialize. And you will soon realize that learning is not something you do only in classrooms or only under the tutelage of professors. True, the e-age demands constant updating. But the computer and the Internet will open new vistas and quicker ways to educate yourselves.

For example, if you are curious why the cost of gasoline seems to be always increasing, you can get answers from CD-ROMs available today, or directly from the Internet. They will provide not only valuable text materials, but also accompanying interactive photos, charts, diagrams, videos and animations explaining how oil is drilled, transported, refined, segregated, priced and delivered. You can learn much faster from these new media than if you merely read passive books or even attended occasional lectures.

The point is: in the new millennium you should not stop learning after you step out of college. Whether you opt to pursue further graduate studies or learn on your own, you must develop a passion for excellence, a habit of moving on, of always being ahead in learning, training and experience.

## Ethics, Source of True Success

Let me now discuss the second E which stands for ethics. Every profession has a code of ethics. All these codes, or canons as we prefer to call them in the legal profession, are in turn based on character ethic,’ on “old-fashioned” principles like integrity, humility, fidelity, courage, prudence and hard work. These are basic norms that must be internalized as the primary sources of true success and enduring happiness. All great persons, heroes and saints have integrated these principles deep within themselves and have depended on them in times of stress and crisis.

## A Change in Men, Not a Change of Men

Indeed, to change society, to change our government, what we need is not just a change of men, but a change in men. A change in men is possible only if people accept and practise these ethical values, if they begin the change from inside their hearts, if they change their old habits and embrace ethical principles and values.

Some people are intrigued when they see others become rich, famous or talked about as role models; or when they simply have happy homes, happy marriages and successful children. So they ask, “How did you do it? Give me some quick advice so I can solve my own predicaments and succeed like you. Is there a motivating seminar or yoga class you can recommend?”

Motivational speeches and quick-fix solutions, like Band Aid and aspirin, have their limited uses to ease the pain or symptoms, but the real solution lies deep inside each one of us — patiently cultivating and actually living the basic ethical values I mentioned earlier.

In the final analysis, what we are communicates far more eloquently than anything we say or do.” In the words of Emerson, “What you are shouts so loudly in my ears I cannot hear what you say.”

Indeed, a swindler cannot talk about integrity, a chatterbox cannot teach about contemplative life, and an atheist cannot preach about God.

There are people we trust because we know their character; because we know them to be honest, fair and gentle. They may not be eloquent, they may not know public relations techniques, they may not know how to polish our egos, but we trust them because they have integrity and fidelity.

## Eternity Is Our Final Destiny

Let me now discuss the third and final E, which stands for eternity. Never for a moment believe that our life on earth is the only life we have been gifted with. Rather, believe that there is life after death, that there is eternity where our final destiny lies. Graduates and friends, let me assure you: God watches over all of us and awaits us in eternity. We may not see or hear Him, but He is present here today. He is amongst us, above us to inspire us, before us to lead us, beside us to guide us, behind us to protect us, and within us — in our hearts and our spirits — to possess us and bring us to His everlasting Kingdom.

Have faith in God. Many times we pray and ask God for what we want — for a good career, for a passing grade, for a reciprocated love, for a healing of some illness, for a car and even for a house. Sometimes when we do not get what we want, we feel embittered and doubt God’s power and providence. But our God is an infinitely intelligent and powerful God. He knows our needs better than we do. He will always provide all things good for us — in His own time and in His own way. As God has Himself said in the Bible, in the book of Isaiah:

My thoughts are not your thoughts,

And my ways are not your ways;

As high as the heavens are above the earth,

So high are my ways above your ways,

And my thoughts above your thoughts

## Epilogue

Dear graduates, let me summarize my message today. As you leave the portals of your beloved UE, you will be facing a rapidly changing world, a world which will demand a new mind set and require a lot of hard work. If you are able to meet its challenges, the world will accord you fortune and fame. It is a world that will offer you an opportunity to live not only for the here and now, but also for the hereafter and forever. But it may also bring difficulties, headaches and predicaments. In your frustration, pain and angst, you will meet counselors, motivators and pseudo experts who will advise you on how to confront these new worldly paradigms, standards and theories of working, living and leisuring. In the end, however, what will truly serve you, save you and enable you to succeed and to live a life worth living, hot only in this life but also in the next, are these three £8 — excellence, ethics and eternity. In short, ExEE.

Someday, twenty or thirty years from now, while you bask at the summit of your own respective careers, it is my hope that in sharing the secret of your success with your children, you will tell them something like this: “I succeeded in my life and career because during My graduation day on April 25, 2000, I heard, believed and then practised the three Es or ExEE message from Mir commencement speaker. I believed him when he said that paradigms change but values endure. Since then, my life changed from within and success came Naturally,”

I thank you.

## Summary

Chapter 4 of 'Transparency, Unanimity & Diversity' reproduced CJP's commencement address at the University of the East, in which he told the graduates that the world they were entering had already undergone what John Naisbitt called a 'megashift' from an industrial community to an information society, where prosperity depends on the processing, possession and distribution of information and where, as Drucker wrote, the central workforce would consist of highly specialized people. He opened by thanking UE president Josefina R. Cortes and Law Dean Carlos M. Ortega, recalling that although he studied law at FEU, UE was the site of the 1960 bar examinations in which he placed among the topnotchers, and that he had nearly missed those examinations after falling ill, walking through knee-deep floodwater from the FEU Hospital at the urging of his dean, Jovito R. Salonga. He then catalogued the new e-age fortunes -- Gates, Morgridge and Chambers, Moore and Noyce, Bezos, Case, Dell and Schwab -- and, drawing on Gates's 'The Road Ahead,' described a wallet-sized computer that would replace money, camera, phone and map. He warned that the shift would reshape the graduates' careers: rules of evidence and court procedure would be radically amended, electronic commerce and deregulation would require new doctrines, DNA and cloning would alter civil and criminal law, dentistry would be transformed, and 1.6 million new infotech jobs were opening. His advice, he conceded, would sound strange: to meet new paradigms the graduates should retain, practise and internalize old values, because paradigms change but values endure. He summarized these as three Es under the acronym ExEE -- excellence, illustrated by the 3M employee Art Fry whose Post It note grew from a choir problem; ethics, rooted in character ethic and requiring a change in men rather than a change of men; and eternity, the life after death where our final destiny lies -- closing with Isaiah and an imagined graduate crediting his success decades later to that day's message.
