# Love God, Serve Man -- Ch. 4: On Improving the Administration of Justice
Date:       1992-10-17
Publisher:  Philippine Daily Inquirer (ed. Isagani Yambot)
Source:     Love God, Serve Man, Philippine Daily Inquirer (ed. Isagani Yambot) — ch. 4 (batch-04 split)
By: Artemio V. Panganiban

(Editor's note: This text is transcribed from an extemporaneous report rendered to President Fidel V. Ramos by Atty. Artemio V. Panganiban, chairman of the Workshop on Administration of Justice of the Multi-Sectoral Conference convened by the Philippine Chamber of Commerce and Industry to propose a program of government for the next 1,000 days. This speech was delivered on October 17, 1992, at the De La Salle University, Greenhills, Mandaluyong. It was reprinted in full in the Philippine Star [October 22, 1992), Lawyers [November 30, 1992], Business Lawyer [December 15, 1992] and in abstract in the Philippine Daily Inquirer [October 27, 1992]. Along with the recommendations of the nine other workshops - on Energy, Banking, Exports, etc. - these proposals were accepted by President Ramos and became the backbone of his program of government.)

Some 2,000 years ago, on the resurrection of our Lord Jesus Christ, our Lord's disciples were gathered in that famous upper room - the Cenacle. The disciples were demoralized; only the previous Friday, Peter denied our Lord three times and the rest of the apostles, disappointed at the sight of a suffering Messiah, abandoned Him to die on the cross. This cowardly lot was gathered in fear and confusion. And although the doors and windows of that upper room were locked, our Lord suddenly appeared and the first thing He said was, "Peace be with you!" But He was not satisfied with just one greeting. After showing them His pierced hand and slashed side, He repeated His greeting: "Peace be with you!"

Since that time and for almost 2,000 years, mankind has aspired to possess and to enjoy that peace which our Lord bequeathed. And in our country, the search for that elusive peace has occupied a major part of the first 100 days of President Ramos. In fact, this effort was highlighted this morning during the opening ceremonies when the De la Salle choir tearfully sang "My Peace I Give Unto You" as the invocation and theme of this Multi-Sectoral Conference.

For our Workshop on Administration of Justice, the government's single-minded pursuit of peace was selected as the major achievement during the first 100 days (June 30 - October 8, 1992) of President Ramos. Indeed, our workshop is now seeing the bright dawn of PAX RAMOS in our troubled land of the morning.

Our workshop realizes that there cannot be any peace - genuine peace that is not merely a cessation of hostilities but one that solves the root causes of violence - unless there is first justice. Justice that is speedy, efficient and objective. Justice that is based on solidarity with the oppressed. Justice that will mean development for the poor. Justice that will renew our country and transform our people.

Our workshop is also aware that the administration of justice is a responsibility not just of the President and Congress, but also of the Supreme Court, which has been vested by our Constitution with "administrative supervision over all courts" as well as with the power "to determine whether or not there has been a grave abuse of discretion amounting to lack or excess of jurisdiction on the part of any branch or instrumentality of the government."

It would be relevant to point out that our workshop noted with appreciation the many steps which our Supreme Court has taken and continues to take to hasten adjudication of disputes, particularly through (a) conducting periodic on-the-job training of judges; (b) directing continuous hearings to resolve cases within 90 days from start of trial; and (c) issuing desk books to judges to guide them in their work. Unfortunately, in spite of these, there are still complaints about serious delays in the delivery of justice and against the very quality of decisions.

While the President appoints justices and judges from a list of nominees submitted by the Judicial and Bar Council, he is hardly involved in the resolution of legal disputes, except:

1. In the supervision of prosecutors, through the Department of Justice and of police agencies through the Department of Interior and Local Government, and

2. In reviewing decisions of some administrative agencies exercising quasi-judicial functions, where, very recently, the President ordered decisions in these agencies to be issued within 30 days from submission.

Because of the above, our workshop believes there cannot possibly be any real and meaningful judicial reforms unless the President, Congress and most importantly, the Supreme Court, come together in what we term as a National Covenant of Justice and Peace. Being aware, however, that our Constitution prohibits justices and judges from performing non-judicial activities and thus, from openly participating in such national covenant, our workshop decided to write an appeal for judicial reforms addressed not only to the President and to Congress but also to the Supreme Court, as follows:

"The participants in the workshop on the Administration of Justice address the President, the Chief Justice, the Senate President, the Speaker of the House, the Secretary of Justice and all those concerned to introduce bold and positive reforms to hasten the prosecution of offenses and the resolution of civil, administrative and electoral disputes; to ensure the integrity of judges and hearing officers; to improve the quality of decisions; and to expedite the enforcement of judgments in order to increase the confidence of the people in the administration of justice by courts and by quasi-judicial agencies."

And now, Mr. President, let me report to you in summarized form the various recommendations of our workshop which are designed to upgrade the administration of justice during the next 1,000 days of your presidency. These proposals will need action not only by you, but we repeat, also by Congress and by the Supreme Court. They were taken from the suggestions of our workshop members, especially from our resource speakers; namely, former Supreme Court Justice Irene R. Cortes and Dean Eduardo de los Angeles, president of the Philippine Bar Association, as well as from our resource persons, Justice Secretary Franklin M. Drilon and Presidential Legal Counsel Antonio T. Carpio. They are as follows:

1. Support the on-going peace process, particularly the broadbased public consultations, and urge all affected sectors to participate actively in the attainment of peace that is based on justice for all, particularly the poor and the oppressed. We know that the pursuit of peace will mean the leveling of political, social and economic imbalances, which in turn will require sacrifices from all sectors, particularly the affluent and the powerful.

2. Monitor periodically the performance and behavior of judges, prosecutors and personnel and the strict observance of the Code of Judicial Conduct and Code of Professional Responsibility. In this connection, the 188 NGOs represented in this Multi-Sectoral Conference shall form a JUDGEWATCH to assist in the implementation of this proposal.

3. Raise the level of citizen awareness and participation in the speedy and efficient administration of justice through media, education and NGO participation.

4. Upgrade the quality of legal education of law students and continuing legal education for judges and lawyers. Among other things, this will mean a redirection of emphasis from litigation to peaceful resolution of disputes.

5. Improve police visibility through the assignment of uniformed policemen in each of the barangays all over the Philippines. A corollary Citizens-Police Monitoring Group in each barangay, to be spearheaded by the Parish Pastoral Council for Responsible Voting, shall be created to monitor the presence and activities of the policemen in their respective areas. The idea is to revive the "foot patrol" of the police.

6. Institutionalize gun control and enforce stricter prosecution of violators. Unclog court dockets by:

a. Establishing small claims courts and adopting summary procedure for small claims (P50,000 or less).

b. Initiating legislation to require mandatory arbitration of commercial disputes and encourage (for other cases) the use of mediation, arbitration and other alternative methods of settling disputes.

c. Lessening the load of the Supreme Court by relieving it of jurisdiction over appealed cases from the Civil Service Commission and the National Labor Relations Commission.

d. Adhering strictly to the constitutional and legal mandates on time limits for the resolution of cases. While our laws require judges and prosecutors to decide cases within specific time limits, these restrictions have not been strictly followed.

e. Encouraging judges to expedite trials through the use of depositions, discovery procedures and continuous hearings.

f. Training a group of judges to mediate and convince litigants to settle/compromise during the pre-trial stage in litigations. Or at least to limit issues and thus, minimize trial time. This unclogging is very important because statistics during the past three years have shown that more cases are filed in our courts than are resolved or decided. Hence, unless these steps are taken, justice will be delayed even more.

8. Coordinate, through the enactment of appropriate legislation, the law functions of the executive departments (i.e., DOJ, PCGG and OSG) through the secretary of justice.

9. Urge bar associations to adopt programs for free legal assistance for indigents.

10. Adopt a program of continuing review and assessment of legal rules and procedures to assure production of quality decisions, free from the love, justice and peace of money, politics and patronage.

11. Review the process of adjudicating cases involving overseas contract workers.

Mr. President, the foregoing is only a summary of our recommendations which, if implemented, will result in a speedy and effective delivery of justice in our country. As I close this presentation, I go back full circle to that event 2,000 years ago at the Cenacle. I know that today many of us, like the disciples of our Lord, are growing weary, sometimes even cynical, of the prospects of ever truly improving our justice delivery system. But to paraphrase the late President John F. Kennedy of the United States, let us not fear to negotiate for peace and justice. Instead, let us negotiate to remove fear and to enable peace and justice to reign at last in our land. As our Lord bequeathed His legacy, so I say: "May the love, justice and peace of Jesus be with all of us always!" I thank you.
