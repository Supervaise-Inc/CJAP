# Leveling the Playing Field -- Ch. 16: Information Technology Foundation v. Comelec: Flawed Contract for Election Automation
Date:       2004-12-01
Publisher:  Not stated in the file (Google Books lists the publisher as 'Artemio V. Panganiban, 2004')
Source:     Leveling the Playing Field, Not stated in the file (Google Books lists the publisher as 'Artemio V. Panganiban, 2004') — ch. 16 (batch-04 split)
By: Artemio V. Panganiban

For more than a decade, there have been attempts to automate elections in the Philippines. Our system of counting and canvassing votes has not materially changed in the last 100 years — it is still super-slow and fraud-prone.

The nation expected the presidential elections on May 10, 2004 to be fully automated. The Commission on Elections (Comelec) had divided the automation project into three phases: first, the cleanup of the list of voters; second, the automation of the counting and canvassing of votes; and third, the electronic transmission of the precinct election results to Comelec.

Unfortunately, the contracts entered into by the Commission to implement these three stages were so riddled with constitutional and legal flaws that they had to be voided by the Supreme Court. The first of these contracts was invalidated in Comelec v. Quijano-Padilla and Photokina, and the third in Brilliantes v. Comelec.

But perhaps, the most critical of the three stages was the second, which concerned the automation of the counting and canvassing of the ballots. The Comelecapproved Contract for the supply of the electronic machines and software for this purpose was voided by the Court in Information Technology Foundation v. Comelec. This case had initially been raffled to Justice Dante O. Tinga, but during the deliberations thereon starting in December 2003, the majority of the members of the Court voted to uphold my erstwhile Dissenting Opinion, which eventually became the new ponencia.

But that is racing ahead too soon. For a clearer understanding, let me rewind the clock and start with the facts.

## The Facts

On December 22, 1997, Congress enacted Republic Act 8436 authorizing Comelec to use an automated election system (AES). The legislature also mandated the poll body to acquire automated counting machines (ACMs), computer equipment, devices and materials; and to adopt new electoral forms and printing materials.

Pursuant to the AES, Comelec adopted Resolution 02-0170 which embodied a modernization program for the 2004 elections, indeed consisting of three phases; namely, Phase I - Voter Registration and Validation System, Phase II - Automated Counting and Canvassing System, and Phase III - Electronic Transmission.

On January 28, 2003, the Commission issued an “Invitation to Apply for Eligibility and to Bid.” On February 11, 2003, it issued Resolution 5929 clarifying certain eligibility criteria for bidders and providing a schedule of activities for the project bidding, as follows:

“1.) Open to Filipino and foreign corporations duly registered and licensed to do business and x x x actually doing business in the Philippines, subject to Sec. 43 of RA 9184 (An Act [P]roviding [for] the Modernization Standardization and Regulation of the Procurement Activities of the Government and for other purposes etc.)

2.) ‘Track Record:

a) For counting machines -should have been used in at least one (1) political exercise with no less than twenty million voters;

b) For verification of voters - the reference site of an existing data base installation using Automated Fingerprint Identification System (AFIS) with at least twenty million.

3.) Ten percent (10%) equity requirement shall be based on the total project cost; and

4.) Performance bond shall be twenty percent (20%) of the bid offer.”

On February 17, 2003, the poll body released the Request for Proposal to procure the election automation machines. The Bids and Awards Committee (BAC) of Comelec gave prospective bidders until March 10, 2003 to submit their respective bids. ‘The public bidding was to be conducted under a two-envelope/two stage system. The first envelope, the “Eligibility Envelope,” should establish the eligibility of the bidder to bid and its qualifications to perform the acts if its bid is accepted. The second envelope would be the “Bid Envelope” itself.

Out of 57 bidders, the BAC found the Mega Pacific Consortium (MPC) and the Total Information Management Corporation (TIMC) eligible. For technical evaluation, their proposals were referred to the BAC’s Technical Working Group (TWG) and the Department of Science and Technology (DOST).

In its Report on the Evaluation of the Technical Proposals on Phase II, DOST pointed out that both MPC and ‘TIMC had obtained a number of failed mark Nevertheless, on April 15, 2003, Comelec en banc pre mulgated Resolution 6074 awarding the project to MPC The Commission publish this Resolution on May 16, 2003.

Assailed in this case was Comelec’s award to the Mega Pacific Consortium/Mega Pacific eSolutions, Inc. of the Contract for the automation of the counting canvassing of the ballots in the 2004 elections.

## The Issues

In the main, the substantive issue was whether Comelec, which was vested with the exclusive constitutional mandate to oversee elections, had gravely abused its discretion when — in the exercise of its administrative functions — it awarded to MPC the contract for the second phase of the comprehensive Automated Election System. The procedural issues concerned the locus standi of petitioners and the alleged prematurity of the Petition. The Decision discussed the procedural ahead of the substantive issues.

## Locus Standi

Our nation’s political and economic future would virtually hang on the outcome of the 2004 elections. Hence, there could be no serious doubt that the subject matter of this case was “a matter of public concern and imbued with public interest”; or of “paramount public interest” and “transcendental importance.” This fact alone justified relaxing the rule on legal standing, following the liberal policy of this Court whenever a case involved “an issue of overarching significance to our society.” The petitioners’ legal standing was therefore recognized and upheld.

## Prematurity/Non-Exhaustion of Administrative Remedies

The respondents claimed that the petitioners had acted prematurely, since they had not first utilized the protest mechanism available to them under RA 9184 — the Government Procurement Reform Act for the settlement of disputes pertaining to procurement contracts.

Section 55 of this law states that protests against (decisions of the Bidding and Awards Committee at all states of procurement may be lodged in the head of the procuring entity by filing a verified position paper and paying a protest fee. Section 57 of the same law provides that any such protest shall not stay or delay the bidding process, but must first be resolved before any award is made. Section 58 adds that court action may be resorted to only after the protest has been resolved Cases filed in violation of this process are to be dismissed for lack of jurisdiction. Regional trial courts have jurisdiction, pursuant to Rule 65 of the Rules of Court, over final decisions of the head of the procuring entity.

Respondent Comelec came out with its en banc Resolution 6074 dated April 15, 2003, awarding the project to Respondent MPC even before the BAC issued its writ ten report and recommendation on April 21, 2003. How then could the petitioners have appealed the BAC’s recommendation or reported to the head of the procuring entity (the Comelec chairman) when — even before the learned of the BAC recommendation — the Comelec en banc had already approved the award of the Contract to MPC?

To put it bluntly, Comelec en banc itself made it legally impossible for petitioners to avail themselves of the administrative remedy that it had so impiously harped upon. Nemo tenetur ad impossibilia, to borrow the private respondents’ favorite Latin excuse.

In any case, the letter addressed to Comelec Chairman Benjamin Abalos Sr. dated May 29, 2003, served to eliminate the premature issue, as it was an actual written protest against the Comelec Decision to award the Contract to MPC. The letter was signed by/for, inter alia, two of herein petitioners: the Information Technology Foundation of the Philippines, represented by its president, Alfredo M. Torres; and Ma. Corazon Akol. The letter-protest was a sufficient compliance with the requirement to exhaust administrative remedies, because it hewed closely to the procedure outlined in Section 55 of RA 9184.

## Main Substantive Issue: Grave Abuse of Discretion

## 1. Identity, Existence and Eligibility of MPC as a Bidder

The respondents insisted that the bidder could not have been Mega Pacific eSolutions, Inc. (MPEI), which had been incorporated only on February 27, 2003, or 11 days prior to the bidding itself. Rather, the bidder was Mega Pacific Consortium (MPC), of which MPEI was but a part.

In the case of a consortium or joint venture desirous of participating in the bidding, the Eligibility Envelope would necessarily have included a copy of the joint venture agreement, the consortium agreement, the memorandum of agreement, or some other similar instrument establishing the due existence as well as composition and scope of the aggroupment. No such instrument was submitted to Comelec by MPC during the bidding process. What the latter submitted were the respective incorporation papers and financial statements of the members of the supposed consortium; and certain certificates, licenses and permits issued to them.

Prior to the award of the Contract, there was no documentary or other basis for Comelec to conclude that a consortium had actually been formed amongst MPEI, SK C&C and WeSolv, along with Election.com.com and ePLDT. Neither was there anything to indicate the exact relationships between and among these firms; their diverse roles, undertakings, investments and prestations — if any — relative to the project; or the precise nature and extent of their respective liabilities with respect to the Contract.

In brief, despite the absence of competent pre as to the existence and the eligibility of the alleged consortium (MPC), its capacity to deliver on the Court and the members’ joint and several liability therefor, Comelec nevertheless assumed that such consortium existed and was eligible. In gross violation of its own) ding rules and procedures contained in its Request Proposal, the Commission then considered the bid of MPC, to which the Contract was eventually awarded. Therein lay grave abuse of discretion by Comelec, in violation of its own bidding rules as well as of the Government Procurement Act.

Instead of one multilateral agreement executed by, and effective and binding on, all the five “consortium members” — as claimed by Commissioner Florentino Tuason Jr. in open court — it turned out that there were four separate and distinct bilateral Agreements. Obviously, Comelec furnished copies thereof only after the bidding process had been terminated, as these were not included in the Eligibility Documents. The Agreements were as follows:

1. Memorandum of Agreement (MOA) between MPEI and SK C&C

2. Memorandum of Agreement between MPEI and WeSolv

3. “Teaming Agreement” between MPEI and Election.com Ltd.

4. “Teaming Agreement” between MPEI and ePLDT

The MOAs of MPEI with WeSolv and SK C&C state that their liabilities, while joint and several with MPEI, were limited only to the particular areas of work wherein their services were engaged or their products utilized. As for Election.com and ePLDT, their separate “Teaming Agreements” with MPEI specifically ascribe to them the role of subcontractor vis-a-vis MPEI as com tractor; and based on the terms of their particular agreements, neither Election.com not ePLDT was — with MPEI — jointly and severally liable to Comelec. Thus, there was no justification for anyone, much less the Com mission, to resort to the rules on partnership and partners’ liabilities.

It must be stressed most vigorously that the sub mission of these four bilateral Agreements to Com clk after the bidding process did nothing to eliminate grave abuse of discretion it had already committed April 15, 2003, when it chose MPC as the winning bidder.

Furthermore, “The Automated Counting and Canvassing Project Contract” was executed between Comelec and MPEI — not the alleged consortium, MPC. Nowhere in that Contract was there any mention of consortium of joint venture, or of members thereof, mw less of joint and several liability. Supposedly executed sometime in May 2003, the Contract bore the notarization date June 30, 2003; and it contained the signature of Willy U. Yu signed as president of MPEI (not on behalf of MPC), along with that of the Comelec chair. The Contract provided in Section 3.2 that MPEI (not MPC) was to supply the equipment and perform the services under the Contract, in accordance with the appendices thereof; nothing whatsoever was said about any consortium, joint venture or partnership.

In Kilosbayan v. Guingona, the Court defined joint venture as “an association of persons or companies jointly undertaking some commercial enterprise; generally, all contribute assets and share risks. It requires a community of interest in the performance of the subject matter, a right to direct and govern the policy in connection therewith, and [a] duty, which may be altered by agreement to share both in profit and losses.”

In the instant case, the automation Contract with (Comelec was not executed by the “consortium” MPC ~ ot by MPEI for and on behalf of MPC — but by MPEI, period. The Contract did not mention any consortium or members thereof. This fact alone contradated all the suppositions about a joint undertaking that would normally apply to a consortium: that it was a commercial enterprise involving a community of interest; and a sparing of risks, profits and losses, and so on.

Neither of the two MPEI Agreements with WeSolv and SK C&C contained any specifics or details as to the exact nature and scope of the parties’ respective undertakings, performances and deliverables under the Agreement relative to the automation project. Likewise, the two Agreements were bereft of pesos-and-centavos data as to the amount of investment each party contributed its respective share in the revenues and/or profit, and so forth — all of which were normal for agreements of this nature. Yet, according to public and private respondents, the participation of MPEI, WeSolv and SK C&C comprised 90 percent of the election automation project which was worth about P1.3 billion.

The separate “Teaming Agreements” entered into by Election.com and ePLDT with MPEI for the remaining 10 percent of the project were ironically much longer and more detailed than the MOAs discussed earlier. Although specifically ascribing to them the role of subcontractor vis-a-vis MPEI as contractor, these agreements were likewise completely devoid of any pricing data (payment terms. Again, based on the terms of their particular Agreements, neither Election.com nor ePLDT was —— with MPEI — jointly and severally liable to Comelec.

Absent any clear-cut statement as to the ex# nature and scope of the parties’ respective undertakings commitments, deliverables and covenants, one part another could easily dodge its obligation and deny contest its liability under the Agreement, or claim that it was the other party that should have delivered but failed to.

## 2. Failure of the Automated Counting Machines to Pass Technical Tests

The BAC Report said: “Based on the 14 April 2003 report (Table 6) of the DOST, it appears that both MegaPacific and TIM (Total Information Management Corporation) failed to meet some of the requirements. Below is a comparative presentation of the requirements wherein Mega-Pacific or TIM or both of them failed: x x x.” What followed was a list of “key requirements,” referring to technical requirements, and an indication of which of the two bidders had failed to meet them.

The first of the key requirements was that the counting machines were to have an accuracy rating of at least 99.9995 percent. The BAC Report indicated that both Mega Pacific and TIMC had failed to meet this standard.

The Court stressed that the essence of public bid-(ding was violated by the practice of requiring very high standards or unrealistic specifications that could not be met — like the 99.9995 percent accuracy rating in this case — only to water them down after the bid had been awarded. Such scheme, which discouraged the entry of prospective bona fide bidders, was in fact a sure indication of fraud in the bidding and designed to eliminate fair competition. If no bidder met the mandatory requirements, standards or specifications, then a failed biddy should have been declared and no award made.

The BAC Report further indicated that MPC and TIMC had both failed to meet another key requirement — for the counting machine’s software program to be able to detect previously downloaded precinct results and to prevent these from being entered again into the counting machine. The same was true with respect to the ability to detect previously downloaded city/municipal as well as provincial/district results and prevent them from being reentered into the system.

Again, Comelec chose to ignore this crucial deficiency, which should have been a cause for the grave concern. During the elections, unscrupulous persons could take advantage of and exploit such deficiency by repeatedly downloading and feeding into the computers the results favorable to a particular candidate or candidates. The country would have thus been confronted with the grim prospect of election fraud on a massive scale by means of just a few key strokes.

According to the BAC Report, the ACMs of both bidders were unable to print out the audit trail without any loss of data. In the case of MPC, the audit trail system was “not yet incorporated” into its ACMs. This particular deficiency was significant to the cause of free and credible elections. The purpose of requiring aul trails was to enable Comelec to trace and verify the idea identities of the ACM operators responsible for data er and downloading, as well as the times when the various data were downloaded into the canvassing system, in order to forestall fraud and to identify the perpetrators. In fact, it was a mandatory feature required under Section 7 of RA 8436.

What really added to the Court’s dismay was the admission made by Commissioner Resurreccion Borra during the Oral Argument that the software then being used by Comelec was merely the “demo” version, inasmuch as the final one that would actually be used in the election was still being developed. It was not clear when the final version of the software would be ready for testing and deployment. It seemed to the Court that Comelec was just keeping its fingers crossed and hoping that the final product would work.

## 3. Effect of Remedial Measures and Retestings Undertaken after the Award

One hundred percent testing of all machines (testing of every single unit) would not serve to eradicate the grave abuse of discretion already committed by Comelec when it awarded the Contract on April 15, 2003, despite the obvious and admitted flaws in the bidding process, the failure of the “winning bidder” to qualify, and the inability of the ACMs and the intended software to meet the bid requirements and rules.

## 4. Further Flaws

In its December 29, 2003 submission to the Court in response to the latter’s December 9, 2003 Resolution, the Comelec disclosed for the very first time that Phase II of the Automated Counting and Canvassing Project also involved the development of three types of software for (1) the evaluation of technical bids, (2) the testing and acceptance of procedures, and (3) election day use. The Commission explained: “In addition to the presentation of the ACM hardware, the bidders were required to develop a ‘base’ software program that will enable the ACM to function properly. Since the software program utilized during the evaluation of bids is not the actual software program to be employed on election day, there being two (2) other types of software programs that will still have to be developed and thoroughly tested prior to actual election day use, defects in the ‘base’ software that can be readily corrected by reprogramming are considered minor in nature, and may therefore be waived.”

In short, Comelec evaluated the bids and decided to award the Contract to the “winning” bidder, partly on the basis of the operation of the ACMs running a “base” software, which was nothing but a sample or demo version, not the actual one that would be used on election day. Keeping in mind that the Contract also involved the acquisition of ACMs or hardware, it was then even clearer that the Contract had been awarded without Comelec having seen — much less evaluated — the final product that would be utilized come election day.

Phase II of the Automated Counting and Canvassing Project also involved the development of three types of software for (1) the evaluation of technical bids, (2) the testing and acceptance of procedures, and (3) election day use. The Commission explained: “In addition to the presentation of the ACM hardware, the bidders were required to develop a ‘base’ software program that will enable the ACM to function properly. Since the software program utilized during the evaluation of bids is not the actual software program to be employed on election day, there being two (2) other types of software programs that will still have to be developed and thoroughly tested prior to actual election day use, defects in the ‘base’ software that can be readily corrected by reprogramming are considered minor in nature, and may therefore be waived.”

In response to the concerns expressed by this Court, Comelec professed: “Since the software program is an inherent element in the automated counting system, the certification issued by the MIRDC-DOST that one thousand nine hundred seventy-three (1,973) units passed the acceptance test procedures is an official recognition by the MIRDC-DOST that the software component of the automated election system, which has been reprogrammed to comply with the provisions of Republic Act No. 8436 as prescribed in the Ad Hoc Technical Evaluation Committee’s ACM Testing and Acceptance Manual, has passed the MIRDC-DOST tests.”

A scrutiny of the December 15, 2003 MIRDC-DOST letter¹⁵ that Comelec had relied upon did not justify its grand conclusion. The letter reads:

“Dear Commissioner Borra:

“We are pleased to submit 11 DOST Test Certifications representing 11 lots and covering 158 units of automated counting machines (ACMs) that we have tested from 02-12 December 2003.

“To date, we have tested all the 1,991 units of ACMs, broken down as follows:

1st batch - 30 units	4th batch - 438 units

2nd batch - 288 units 	5th batch - 438 units

3rd batch - 414 units 	6th batch - 383 units

“It should be noted that a total of 18 units have failed the test. Out of these 18 units, only one (1) unit has failed the retest.

“Thank you and we hope you will find everything in order.”

Even a cursory glance at the foregoing letter would show that it completely lacked any support of Comel contention that the “software component of the automated election system x x x has been reprogrammed to comply with” RA 8436 and “has passed the MIRDC-DOST tests.” In fact, the letter apparently referred to the testing of the ACMs or the hardware, not the software program. The fact that a total of 1,973 machines had ultimately passed the MIRDC-DOST tests did by itself serve as an endorsement of the soundness of the software program, much less as proof that it had been reprogrammed.

The Commission further said that before the software could be utilized on election day, it would require “customization” through the addition of data — like the list of candidates, project of precincts, and so on. And inasmuch as those data would become available only in January 2004 anyway, there was therefore no perceived need on Comelec’s part to rush the supplier into producing the final (or near-final) version of the software before then; and the software needed for the electoral exercise could have been continuously developed, tested, adjusted and perfected, practically all the way up to election day.

Clearly, such jumbled ratiocinations completely negate the rationale underlying the bidding process mandated by law. It was clear that the Commission had further desecrated the law on public bidding by permitting the winning bidder to change and alter the subject of the Contract (the software), in effect allowing a substantive amendment without public bidding.

Such stance was contrary to settled jurisprudence requiring the strict application of rules, regulations and guidelines for public bidding so as to place each bidder — actual or potential — on the same footing. The essence of public bidding is, after all, the opportunity for fair competition and a fair basis for the precise comparison of bids. In common parlance, public bidding aims (to “level the playing field.” That means each bidder must bid under the same conditions and be subject to the same guidelines, requirements and limitations, so that best offer or lowest bid may be determined — all other things being equal.

Thus, it was contrary to the very concept of public bidding to permit a variance between the condition under which bids were invited and those under which proposals were submitted and approved; or, as in th: case, the conditions under which the bid was won and those under which the awarded Contract was to be com: plied with. The substantive amendment of the Contract “bidded” out — without any public bidding or after the bidding process had been concluded — was violative 0 the public policy on public biddings, as well as the spirit and intent of RA 8436. The very rationale of public bidding was totally subverted by the Commission.

May I quote the Epilogue I wrote in closing the Decision:

“Epilogue

“Once again, the Court finds itself at the crossroads of our nation’s history. At stake in this controversy is not just the business of a computer supplier, or a questionable proclamation by Comelec of one or more public officials. Neither is it about whether this country should switch from the manual to the automated system of counting and canvassing votes. At its core is the ability and capacity of the Commission on Elections to perform properly, legally and prudently its legal mandate to implement the transition from manual to automated elections.

“Unfortunately, the Comelec has failed to measure up to this historic task. As stated at the start of this Decision, Comelec has not merely gravely abused its discretion in awarding the Contract for the automation of the counting and canvassing of the ballots. It has also put at grave risk the holding of credible and peaceful elections by shoddily accepting electronic hardware and software that admittedly failed to pass legally mandated technical requirements. Inadequate as they are, the remedies it proffers post facto do not cure the grave abuse of discretion it already committed (1) on April 15, 2003, when it illegally made the award; and (2) “sometime” in May 2003 when it executed the Contract for the purchase of defective machines and nonexistent software from a non-eligible bidder.

“For these reasons, the Court finds it totally unacceptable and unconscionable to place its imprimatur on this void and illegal transaction that seriously endangers the breakdown of our electoral system. For this Court to cop out and to close its eyes to these illegal transactions, while convenient, would be to abandon its constitutional duty of safeguarding public interest.

“As a necessary consequence of such nullity and illegality, the purchase of the machines and all appurtenances thereto including the still-to-be-produced (or in Comelec’s words, to be “reprogrammed”) software, as well as all the payments made therefor, have no basis whatsoever in law. The public funds expended pursuant to the void Resolution and Contract must therefore be recovered from the payees and/or from the persons who made possible the illegal disbursements, without prejudice to possible criminal prosecution against them.

“Furthermore, Comelec and its officials concerned must bear full responsibility for the failed bidding and award, and be held accountable for the electoral mess wrought by their grave abuse of discretion in the performance of their functions. ‘The State, of course, is not bound by the mistakes and illegalities. of its agents and servants.

“True, our country needs to transcend our slow, manual and archaic electoral process. But before it can do so, it must first have a diligent and competent electoral agency that can properly and prudently implement a well-conceived automated election system.

“At bottom, before the country can hope to have a speedy and fraud-free automated election, it must first be able to procure the proper computerized hardware and software legally, based on a transparent and valid system of public bidding. As in any democratic system, the ultimate goal of automating elections must be achieved by a legal, valid and above-board process of acquiring the necessary tools and skills therefor. Though the Philippines needs an automated electoral process, it cannot accept just any system shoved into its bosom through improper and illegal methods. As the saying goes, the end never justifies the means. Penumbral contracting will not produce enlightened results.”

Several members of the Court wrote Separate Opinions, which I will now summarize.

## The Santiago Concurrence

According to Justice Consuelo Ynares-Santiago, there were three main grounds for granting the Petition. First, the special circumstances availing in the case warranted the relaxation of the rule on exhaustion of administrative remedies. Second, the Comelec gravely abused its discretion when it clearly contracted with a non-eligible entity; therefore, the contract for the automated counting system was null and void. Finally, even assuming the eligibility of the other contracting party, the Comelec gravely abused its discretion when it changed the technical requirements for accuracy of the automated machines and software, an offense that warranted the nullification of the Contract.

The principle of exhaustion of administrative remedies, said Justice Santiago, was not an ironclad rule. That doctrine was relative, and its flexibility was called for by the peculiarity or uniqueness of the factual and circumstantial settings of a case.

Furthermore, there was no plainer example of a case in which the issues were of transcendental importance. The preservation of an honest, upright system of electing our nation’s public officers bore urgent public interest considerations. Moreover, the administrative action involved here was patently illegal, amounting to lack or excess of jurisdiction.

Moreover, the fact that WeSolve, SK C&C Election.com and ePLDT had bound themselves to MPEI: did not mean that a joint venture was thereby executed. These individual entities were merely the sub-contracts of MPEI, and their liability for any breach was only to MPEI.

Conspicuously absent from the records of this case were documents that would demonstrate that the individual members of the so-called “Consortium” ha actually formed or constituted themselves into a joint venture. Unable to conclude that the “joint venture had any legal existence, Justice Santiago thus found impossible to evaluate whether the third criterion — the nationality requirement for an eligible joint venture had been met by the “Consottium.” There was also marked absence of the individual members’ intent to be jointly and severally liable for the Contract.

In sum, there was a conspicuous dearth of evidence to demonstrate that there was indeed a consortium; if there was one, the specific composition thereof and the individual members’ liability to Comelec for breach of contract.

Furthermore, it was plain that Comelec entered into the contract, not with this “Consortium,” but rather with MPEI — admittedly, an entity that would per se ineligible to bid.

Finally, even if we were to concede that the Comelec had contracted with an eligible entity, it appeared that the counting machines and the ballot-counting software submitted by the “Consortium” simply failed to meet the accuracy rating of 99.9995 or better, as required by the Request for Proposal. After the bids had been submitted, the accuracy criteria were suddenly changed to 99.995 percent. Changing an eligibility requirement midstream, after bids had already been submitted, completely subverted the integrity of the bidding process and warranted the nullification of the award of the Contract ~— whether the other contracting party was MPEI of the “Consortium.”

## The Gutierrez Concurrence

Justice Angelina Sandoval-Gutierrez explained that elections were indeed the bedrock of every democratic institution. Thus, in automating the election system, the standards must be as high as the stakes.

She argued that petitioners’ recourse — prohibition and mandamus — was improper. She pointed out that it was a well-established rule, particularly in public biddings, that courts could not compel an agency to do or not to do a particular act within its prerogative or discretion. Not being ironclad, the rule admits of one noted exception — when, in the exercise of its authority, an agency gravely abuses or exceeds its jurisdiction. Judicial review may be justified on the grounds of grave abuse of discretion, arbitrary rejection of bids, and lack of freedom of competition among bidders.

The substantive issues may be reduced into queries: Did Comelec abuse its discretion when it allowed MPEI to participate actively in the bidding despite its failure to meet the mandatory eligibility requirement? Did Comelec abuse its discretion when it awarded the Contract to MPEI?

It bears stressing that MPEI was incorporated only on February 27, 2003, as evidenced by its Certificate of Incorporation. This Certificate showed that from the time Comelec issued its Invitation to Bid (January 28 2003) and its Request for Proposal (February 17, 2003 up to the time it convened the Pre-Bid Conference (February 18, 2003), MPEI was a nonexistent entity. It came into being only eleven days prior to the submission of i bid — on March 10, 2003. This fact posed a legal obstacle to its eligibility as a bidder. The Request for Proposal required the bidder to submit financial document that would establish, to the BAC’s satisfaction, its financial capability which included the following:

“(1) [A]udited financial statements of the Bidder’s firm for the last three (3) calendar years, stamped ‘RECEIVED’ by the appropriate government agency, to show its capacity to finance the manufacture and supply of [goods called for and a statement or record of volumes of sales;

(2) Balance Sheet;

(3) Income Statement; and

(4) Statement of Cash Flow.”

Surely, it was grave abuse of discretion on the part of Comelec to award a billion-peso Contract to an entity whose existence and eligibility was highly questionable. It raised the accomplishment of such a great undertaking as the automation of our country’s election system.

The requirements that MPEI failed to meet could not be considered insubstantial. They had a bearing on the required features of the automated election system under Section 7 of RA No. 8436, such as (1) the use of appropriate ballots; (2) a stand-alone machine that could count votes and an automated system that could consolidate the results immediately; (3) provisions for audit trails (4) minimum human intervention; and (5) adequate safeguards or security measures.

Misplaced was Comelec’s stance that it could waive certain requirements, which concerned the integrity and the accuracy of the ACM and affected the substance and the validity of the bids. Mandatory statutory or regulatory requirements with respect to the bidding of public contracts could not be waived.

## Dissent of Chief Justice Davide

Chief Justice Hilario G. Davide Jr. opined that non-issuance by the Court of a Temporary Restraining Order showed an initial or prima facie finding that the grant of the Petition was not sufficiently justified or warranted by the allegations therein. In the meantime, the parties were not barred from performing their respective obligations under the Contract. Comelec had already paid a large portion of its contracted obligation, and the private respondent had delivered the contracted equipment for automation.

It was to be reasonably presumed that during the same period, Comelec had focused its attention, time and resources toward the full and successful implementation of the comprehensive Automated Election System for the May 2004 elections. Setting aside the contract in question at this late hour might have an unsettling, disturbing and even destabilizing effect.

Comelec would be left with insufficient time prepare for the manual electoral process, which would necessarily include the acquisition of security paper, purchase of a “dandy roll” to watermark the ballot pa per, the printing of other election forms, as well as the bidding for the ballot boxes and the acquisition thereof. Also, as there was absolutely no more time to conduct re-bidding, such a move would put to naught the law on the Automated Election System (RA 8436), as well as the Executive Orders issued for its implementation in the May 2004 elections: Executive Order No. 172 (24 January 2003), which allocated the sum of P2.5 billion; and Executive Order No. 175 (10 February 2003), the additional sum of P500 million.

Finally, there was no suggestion that graft and corruption attended the bidding process, or that the contract price was excessive or unreasonable. All that the petitioners claimed was that “the bidding and the award process was fatally flawed. The public respondent acted without or in excess of its jurisdiction or with grave abuse of discretion amounting to lack or excess of jurisdiction when it awarded the project.” It might precipitate for the Contract in question to be declared void by the Court.

## Dissent of Justice Vitug

According to Justice Jose C. Vitug, undisputed facts could form the basis for the resolution of certiorari or prohibition cases involving issues affecting the jurisdiction of the tribunal, the board and the officers. In the instant Petition, an extensive inquiry into the facts would essentially be required by the enormity of the factual disputes, which include those on the eligibility of Mega Pacific to participate in the bidding process, the “veracity and effectivity” of the testing, and the technical evaluation conducted by the DOST on the automated counting machine of the bidders. An insistence that the question of jurisdiction be resolved despite unsettled factual points would be inadequate to allow an intrusion by the Court.

The electoral process should be of paramount and immediate concern to every Filipino. The computerization or automation of our electoral process is just as important. Nevertheless, it would have also been unwise for the Court to take on the case precipitately; after all we have been without it for decades. The opinions expressed by the justices, collectively and individually, should indeed give compelling reasons for the Commission 01 Elections to perhaps take notice and, on its own, to re-examine forthwith the assailed bidding process.

## The Tinga Dissent

Justice Dante O. Tinga maintained that no constitutional provision, letter or statute was alleged to have been violated. The Court nullified the Contract for an automated election system, simply on the ground that 1 awarding it, Comelec had supposedly violated its bidding rules and engendered an unfounded apprehension that the counting machines would not work on election day. On the other hand, not one of the losing bidder joined in the Petition, as neither they nor the petitioner questioned the fairness of the price tag for the machines

The Court allegedly disregarded the following fundamental postulates, on the basis of which this case should have been decided.

First. The instant original Petition was one for prohibition and mandamus under Rule 65 of the 1997 Rules of Civil Procedure. Prohibition is an extraordinary writ directed against any tribunal, corporation, board, officer or person — whether exercising judicial, quasi-judicial or ministerial functions — commanding the respondent to desist from further proceedings when these are without or in excess of the respondent’s jurisdiction. Such writ is also mandated when those proceedings are attended by grave abuse of discretion amounting to lack or excess of jurisdiction, and there is no appeal; or no other plain, speedy, and adequate remedy in the ordinary course of law.

Mandamus, on the other hand, is an extraordinary writ commanding a tribunal, a corporation, a board, an officer or a person — immediately or at some other specified time — to do the act required to be done, when the respondent unlawfully neglects the performance of an act that the law specifically enjoins as a duty resulting from an office, a trust, or a station. The required act is also mandated by this writ when the respondent excludes another from the use and enjoyment of a right or an office to which the latter is entitled; and there is no other plain, speedy and adequate remedy in the ordinary course of law.

Second. Justice Tinga opined that in deciding the instant case, the Court should consider only the undisputed or admitted facts and resolve only the specific questions raised by the parties.²² The Court was not a repository of remedies or a “super-legal-aid bureau.” It could not grant relief for every perceived violation of the law or, worse, on the basis of prophetic wisdom.

Third. Justice Tinga further pointed out that it was not the Court’s function to ensure actively that the automation would successfully be implemented; or that the elections would be free of fraud, violence, terrorism and other threats to the sanctity of the ballot. This duty lay primarily with Comelec.

Fourth. The Court constantly underscored the importance of giving Comelec considerable latitude in adopting means and methods that would ensure the accomplishment of the objective for which it had been created — to promote free, orderly, honest, peaceful and credible elections. Thus, in the past the Court prudently declined to interfere with the exercise by the Commission of the latter’s administrative functions, absent any showing of grave abuse of discretion.

Justice Tinga agreed with the respondents that the petitioners failed to exhaust — or better still, avail themselves of — the administrative remedies outlined in RA 9184.

At no time during the entire bidding process did the petitioners question the determination of the Comelec Bids and Awards Committee that the Mega Pacific Consortium was eligible to bid. Consequently finalized was the Committee’s determination — subsequently adopted by the Comelec — that MPC was eligible to bid.

It was noteworthy that the protest mechanism outlined in RA 9184, which allowed protests of the BAC’s decisions “in all stages of procurement,” reinforced and even institutionalized the exhaustion doctrine insofar as public bidding was concerned. The petitioners could have easily asked for a reconsideration the moment the BAC determined that MPC was eligible to bid. Failing to do so, they could have filed a protest with the Comelec en banc itself. They did neither.

The majority opinion posited that it would have been futile for the petitioners to protest/appeal the BAC Report to the Comelec chair, since the Commission had already priorly approved it. Justice Tinga argued, however, that they could have appealed directly to the Comelec en banc. After all, matters of this nature had to be decided by it as a collegial body. ‘To state that it would not act on the appeal was to state uncharitably that it would disregard its duty to respond in accordance with the Code of Ethical Conduct. The Court’s statement that the Comelec en banc made it impossible for the petitioner to make use of the administrative remedy was simply baseless.

Justice Tinga also called attention to the fact ‘that the petitioners had wasted nearly five months from the time the BAC Report was released on April 21, 2003 before they filed the instant Petition on August 6, 206 The significant time gap precluded the availability of i exceptions to the exhaustion doctrine. Thus, the premature invocation of the Court’s judicial power became fatal to their cause of action.

The Dissenting Opinion highlighted the supposed participation of MPC, the “consortium,” in the bidding

Justice Tinga enumerated the following documents on record:

1. The March 7, 2003 letter from MPET’s president, Willy Yu, to the Comelec Bids and Awards Committee — a letter stating that six companies had agreed to form a consortium to bid for the project. These were the Mega Pacific eSolutions, Inc.; Election.com Ltd.; ePLDT; SK C&C; WeSolv Open Computing, Inc. (a subsidiary of Fujitsu Philippines, Inc.); and OracleSystem (Philippines), Inc.

2. Agreements among the members of the consortium:

a. Memorandum of Agreement (MOA) between MPEI and WeSolv Open Computing, Inc.

b. Memorandum of Agreement between MPEI and SK C&C

c. Teaming Agreement between MPEI and Election.com Ltd.

d. Agreement between MPEI and ePLDT

These documents all bore execution and notarization dates prior to the submission by MPC of its bid documents on March 10, 2003. The fair assumption was that the letter of MPEI’s president on behalf of MPC and the Agreements between MPEI and the members of the consortium had already been submitted to (Comelec when the BAC evaluated the bids, and that the poll body had acted upon the BAC’s recommendation and accordingly resolved to award the Contract to MPC. Any contrary conclusion had no basis in fact and was founded on pure conjecture.

Furthermore, nowhere in the Request for Propos (RFP) was it required that a single written agreement to prove the existence of a joint venture be executed by its members. Indeed, the intention to be jointly and severally liable may be evidenced not only by a single joint venture agreement, but by supplementary documents executed by the parties signifying such intention.

‘The MPEI-WeSolv MOA authorized Mega Pacific to negotiate and sign the Contract with Comelec, provided that for products and services it would supply G the project, WeSolv would be jointly and severally liable with MPEI The MPEI-SK C&C MOA contained similar provisions.

The REP itself had adverted to a “particular tract,’ not to the “entire Project” or “joint venture The clear signification was that all the members of t joint venture need not be solidarily liable for the ent project or joint venture; it was sufficient that the le company and the member in charge of a particular tract or aspect of the joint venture had agreed to solidarily liable.

Clearly, whatever perceived deficiencies there in the Supplementary Contracts entered into by and the other members of the consortium as regards their joint and several undertakings were cured — or better still, prevented from arising — by the provisions from their respective Agreements. Such Agreements established that each member of MPC was solidarily liable with the lead company, MPEI, albeit only for the particular contract or aspect of the joint venture of which it was in charge.

Moreover, the Contract provided several options that Comelec could take in case of MPC’s breach or nonperformance of the material terms thereof. Significantly, MPEI posted a performance bond that amounted to 20 percent of the bid offer’ against which Comelec could execute in case of breach.

On the premise that the Contract was between (Comelec and MPEI, not MPC, the Court dismissed the respondent’ use of the Contract as basis for the enforcement of the Commission’s claims against the consortium.* But the Court did so because MPEI, as lead member of the consortium, was empowered to represent WeSolv and SK C&C which, along with MPEI itself, represented 90 percent of the total consortium interest. Thus, because the Contract was executed by MPEI as the authorized representative of the key members of MPC, it was as if the latter itself had been the named party thereto.

From the foregoing, it was clear that the absence a single, formal joint-venture agreement among all the members of the joint venture did not preclude Comelec from enforcing their liability in case of breach In any event, it could still have enforced their liability under the general provisions of the Civil Code on part nership, as correctly pointed out in the Memorandum of the Office of the Solicitor General.

It should be recalled that MPEI, SK C&C, WeSolv Election.com.com and ePLDT represented themselves and, or allowed themselves to be represented as partners and members of MPC for purposes of bidding for the project! They were therefore liable to Comelec to the extent that it relied upon such representations.’ Their liability as partners was solidary with respect to everything cha goal) to the partnership under certain conditions. This was inescapable fact, judging from the language of their own contracts inter se or from the provisions of the Civil Code.

According to the documents it submitted to substantiate its eligibility, MPEI had been incorporated only on February 27, 2003. Thus, it was not able to submit the required financial documents — the Audited Financial Statements for the last three years, the Balance She the Income Statement, and the Statement of Cash Flow.

The failure of MPEI to submit its financial documents because of its newly acquired corporate personality should not have, by itself, disqualified MPC. The purpose of requiring the submission of the financial documents was to determine the financial soundness of the bidder, as well as its capacity to perform the Contract if its bid was accepted. This purpose could have well been attained by examining the financial documents submitted by the other members of the joint venture. It was emphasized that SK C&C, ePLDT and WeSolv had submitted the required financial documents. Moreover, MPEI had a paid-in capital of P300,000,000 — an amount that was substantially over and above the 10 percent equity, based on the total project cost required by the RFP.

The collective nature of the undertaking of the members of MPC, their contribution of assets and sharing of risks, and the community of their interests in the performance of the Contract all led to the reasonable conclusion that their collective qualifications should have been the basis for evaluating their eligibility. Practical wisdom dictated this approach, because the sheer enormity of the project rendered improbable the ability of any single entity, by itself, to undertake and to comply with all the eligibility requirements of such a project. As emphasized by the OSG, the RFP had precisely allowed bids from manufacturers, suppliers and/or distributors that formed themselves into a joint venture, in recognition of the virtual impossibility that a single entity would be able to respond to the Invitation to Bid.

Noticeably, the petitioners failed to contest the qualifications of the members of the consortium in any of their pleadings. The Decision — uncharacteristically silent on this matter, except for its general objection to the inability of MPEI to submit some of the finance documents required by the RFP — seemingly conceded that they were eligible and qualified to perform the Contract.

It is well to note that all the 1,991 ACMs supplied by MPC under the Contract were found to have satisfied the mandatory requirements of the AES, to wit: (a) the use of appropriate ballots; (b) a stand-alone machine that could count votes and an automated system that could consolidate the results immediately; (c) provision for audit trails; (d) minimum human intervention; af (e) adequate safeguards or security measures.

As stated in the BAC Report, the failed MPC were mere formalities in certain documentary requirements and were attributable to the software that could have been readily corrected by reprogramming. Such failed marks were not material in nature but were, at worst, merely optional features of the System. The RFP clearly authorized the BAC to waive any informal nonconformity or irregularity in a bid that did not constitute a material deviation, provided that such waiver did not prejudice or affect the relative ranking of any bidder.

As regards the issue relating to the accuracy rating of 99.9995 percent mandated for the counting machine by the RFP, there was no competent evidence on record that the Comelec had waived or changed the prescribed accuracy rating. In fact, in the Contract between it and MPEI, the same accuracy rating of 99.9995 percent was required. Also in its letter dated October 24, 2003, DOST clarified that upon further verification, it had found that “except for 1 ACM (with an accuracy rating of 99.998%), all of the 456 machines (including the retested 9 units) that were tested by the DOST (as of October 20, 2003) have an accuracy rating of 100 percent provided that the ballots are shaded correctly and fed into the ACMs following the right orientation.” Notably, the DOST Report itself stated that the machines were 100 percent accurate.

It should be emphasized that the DOST Report showed that the tested ACMs of MPC had generated audit trails that reflected the exact date and time of the ‘start and the end of the counting of ballots per precinct and also regularly generated hard and soft copies of the audit trail.“ Republic Act No. 8436 merely required that the AES should have “provisions for au trail,” which the ACMs — as tested — were able to comply with.

As to the inability of the machines to detect previously downloaded data and to prevent these from being rendered into the system, suffice it to say that this was neither a mandatory nor an optional feature of the AES under RA 8436. In any case, such a requirement was deemed satisfied, upon DOST's final favorable evaluation.

In view of the foregoing arguments, Justice Tinga deemed plainly injudicious the majority’s position that the Comelec should have conducted rebidding of the project. Such procedure would have been warranted on) if no bid had been received or was qualified as the lowest calculated and responsive bid. But there were more than 50 bidders for the project, out of which MPC qualified for having offered the lowest calculated and responsive bid.

A new bidding of the project would not have served any further purpose, because the original one had actually drawn the participation of as many bidders as were realistically possible; and considering the enormity of the project, a re-bidding thereof would not have attracted new bidders. Besides, none of the losing ones questioned the process undertaken by the BAC. ‘The logical conclusion was that they had conceded the eligibility and qualifications of MPC and deferred to (Comelec's decision to award the Contract to that consortium.

The most crucial point raised against the respondents was the alleged nonsubmission of the consortium Agreements before the bidding deadline. The ponencia called attention to this fact no less than five times. But the assertion — which was one of fact — was debunked by the consortium Agreements themselves, which had been notarized not later than March 9, 2003, or before the bidding deadline. To ignore the public character of those documents was to ascribe bad faith unfairly to Comelec.

As to the fact that MPEI was made party to the Contract with Comelec, it was simply because MPEI had been authorized to sign on behalf of the other consortium members.

In sum, Justices Puno, Panganiban (ponente), Quisumbing, Santiago, Gutierrez, Carpio, Martinez, Morales and Callejo voted to grant the Petition. Chief Justice Davide and Justices Vitug, Corona, Azcuna and Tinga opted to dismiss it. The result was a 9-5 tally. There was one vacancy in the Court then.

## Resolution of the Motion for Reconsideration

On January 28, 2004, Comelec filed a Motion for Reconsideration, while the private respondents filed Omnibus Motion dated January 26, 2004.

Essentially, the movants raised the same procedural and substantive issues already exhaustively discussed and definitively passed upon in the Decision. No compelling reasons were adduced to justify the modification or reversal of the Court’s findings and conclusion” those issues.

With respect to their contention that the Decision was “grossly unfair” because it “exposed them to possible criminal prosecution,” the Court clarified that had made no prejudgment of their criminal liability, precisely directed the Ombudsman to determine criminal liability — if any — of the public official conspiring private individuals, if any), after conducting the appropriate proceedings and observing due process.

The public respondents further complained that while the Court had directed that their criminal and civil liabilities (if any) be determined by the ombudsman and the solicitor general, it had not given the same directive in regard to the officials involved in the Amari and the Piatco cases.

The Court held that the misplaced outbursts merely showed that the Comelec commissioners and other officials concerned had “not realized the gravity of their misdeeds.” While the Contracts in the Amari and the Piatco cases were mainly commercial in nature, here — as already explained in the Decision — the “illegal, hasty and imprudent actions of the Commission have not only desecrated legal and jurisprudential norms [governing contracts and public biddings,] but have also cast serious doubts upon the poll body’s ability and capacity to conduct automated elections. Truly, the pith and soul of democracy — credible, orderly, and peaceful elections — have been put in jeopardy by the illegal and abusive acts of Comelec.”

Also, in the Amari and the Piatco cases, there was no irregular disbursement of public funds. Here, however, the government had earmarked P1.3 billion for the automation of the ballot counting and canvassing, and Comelec had already irregularly paid nearly P850 million thereof to private respondents. Certainly, public interest required the recovery of this enormous sum, and the punishment of those who might be criminally responsible therefore.

Besides, the public officials involved in the Amari se had already been charged criminally. In any event, even without being expressly directed by the Court, the ombudsman — on his own — was constitutionally mandated to investigate any possible criminal liability of the officials concerned, whenever and wherever it might arise.

The Court also emphasized that contrary to the movants’ insinuation, the Decision per se had not prohibited the automation of the elections. It had merely voided the assailed Resolution and Contract for having been made and executed with inexplicable haste; in grave abuse of discretion; and in clear violation of law, of settled jurisprudence, and also of Comelec’s own bidding rules and requirements.

The Court further ruled that the prayer of the private respondents for a new hearing had no merit, they had been given more than sufficient opportunity to explain their causes through their kilometric pleadings in and their exhaustive presentations during the Oral Argument.

Finally, the public respondent’s request for clarification “on the effect of herein Decision on the Resolution from Congress providing for [the] implementation of automation of elections on [a] selective basis” Resolution that the President had allegedly “not signed” — could not be answered, since it did not involve issue raised in this case, and since the Court would f render advisory opinions.

For the foregoing reasons, the Motions were DENIED with finality.
