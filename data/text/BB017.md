# Transparency, Unanimity & Diversity -- Ch. 7: Ty v. Trampe: Validity of Increases in Real Estate Taxes

Here are the facts of that case.

## Facts

On January 6, 1994, the municipal assessor of Pasig, Metro Manila (now Pasig City) sent petitioners a Notice of Assessment of their real properties located therein. It was based on a new Schedule of Market Values prepared by the assessor and approved by the finance secretary. At bottom, the new assessments increased real estate taxes from 418 to 570 percent.

On March 29, 1994, petitioners filed, before the Regional Trial Court of the National Capital Judicial Region, a Petition to declare the new increased tax assessments null and void and to enjoin the collection of real estate taxes based on said assessments on the ground that the assessor had not followed the requirements of Presidential Decree (PD) 921. Ina Decision dated July 14, 1994, then Judge Aurelio C.

Trampe (now retired) denied the Petition on the ground that Republic Act (RA) 7160, otherwise known as the Local Government Code of 1991, had already repealed the Decree. |

## The Main Issue

Did the Local Government Code of 1991 repeal the provisions of PD 921, thus validating the increased real estate taxes being imposed and collected in the Municipality (now City) of Pasig?

## The Court's Ruling

No, the Local Government Code of 1991 did not repeal PD 921. It was undisputed that the new Schedule of Market Values and Assessments had been prepared solely by the assessor in accordance with RA 7160, but in contravention of PD 921 which required, in the preparation of the Schedule, joint action by all the city and the municipal assessors in the Metropolitan Manila area.

It is obvious from Section 534 of RA 7160 that PD 921 was neither expressly nor impliedly repealed by the former.

RA 7160 has a repealing provision (Section 534) which enumerates the statutes revoked. If the intention of the members of the legislature were to abrogate PD 921, they would have included it in that Section, as when they expressly stated that several other presidential decrees had no more force and effect. Hence, any repeal or modification of PD 921 was possible only under paragraph “f” of said Section 534, which reads: “(f) All general and special laws, acts, city charters, decrees, executive orders, proclamations and administrative regulations, part or parts thereof which are inconsistent with any of the provisions of the Code are hereby repealed or modified accordingly.”

This particular provision partakes of the nature of a general repealing provision. It is a basic rule of statutory construction that repeals by implication are not favored or allowed, unless the two laws are convincingly and unambiguously demonstrated to be so clearly repugnant and patently inconsistent that they cannot coexist. This principle is based on the rationale that the will of the legislature cannot be overturned by the judicial function of construction and interpretation. The function of a court is not to take the place of Congress in repealing statutes, but to try to harmonize seeming conflicts in the laws and to resolve doubts in favor of their validity and coexistence.

RA 7160 and PD 921 are two laws not coextensive or mutually inclusive in their scope and purpose. While the former covers all governmental functions delegated to local government units all over the country, the latter embraces only the Metropolitan Manila area and is limited to the administration of financial services therein, especially the assessment and the collection of real estate tax.

In ruling out an implied repeal, the Court compared the two laws in this wise:

“Presidential Decree No. 921 was promulgated on 12 April 1976, with the aim of, inter alia, evolving ‘a progressive revenue raising program that will not unduly burden the tax-payers... ‘in Metropolitan Manila.? Hence, it provided for the ‘administration of local financial services in Metropolitan Manila’ only, and for this purpose, divided the area into four Local Treasury and Assessment Districts, regulated the duties and functions of the treasurers and assessors in the cities and municipalities in said area and spelled out the process of assessing, imposing and distributing the proceeds of real estate taxes therein.

“Upon the other hand, Republic Act No. 7160, otherwise known and cited as the ‘Local Government Code of 1991,” took effect on 01 January 1992.° It declared ‘genuine and meaningful local autonomy’ as a policy of the state. Such policy was meant to decentralize government ‘powers, authority, responsibilities and resources’ from the national government to the local government units ‘to enable them to attain their fullest development as self-reliant communities and make them more effective partners in the attainment of national goals.’ In the formulation and implementation of policies and measures on local autonomy, ‘(l)ocal government units may group themselves, consolidate or coordinate their efforts, services, and resources for purposes commonly beneficial to them.”

“From the above, it is clear that the two laws are not co-extensive and mutually inclusive in their scope and purpose. While R.A. 7160 covers almost all governmental functions delegated to local government units all over the country, P.D. 921 embraces only the Metropolitan Manila area and is limited to the administration of financial services therein, especially the assessment and collection of real estate (and some other local) taxes.

“Coming down to specifics, Sec. 9 of P.D. 921 requires that the schedule of values of real properties in the Metropolitan Manila area shall be prepared jointly by the city assessors in the districts created therein; while Sec. 212 of R.A. 7160 states that the schedule shall be prepared ‘by the provincial, city and municipal assessors of the municipalities within the Metropolitan Manila Area for the different classes of real property situated in their respective local government units for enactment by ordinance of the sanggunian concerned. x x x.’

“It is obvious that harmony in these provisions is not only possible, but in fact desirable, necessary and consistent with the legislative intent and policy.”

For its cavalier disregard of several doctrines when it rendered its judgment, the lower court was chided in the Epilogue thus:

“Finally, this Tribunal would be remiss in its duty as guardian of the judicial branch if we let pass unnoticed the ease by which the Respondent Judge consigned ‘to the statutes’ graveyard’ a legislative enactment ‘together with the (three) decisions of the Supreme Court’ promulgated jointly and unanimously en banc. An elementary regard for the sacredness of laws and the stability of judicial doctrines laid down by superior authority should have constrained him to be more circumspect in rendering his decision and to spell out carefully and precisely the reasons for his decision to invalidate such acts, instead of imperiously decreeing an implied repeal. He knows or should have known the legal precedents against implied repeals. The Respondent Judge, in his Decision, did not even make an attempt to try to reconcile or harmonize the laws involved. Instead, he just unceremoniously swept them and this Court’s decisions into the dustbin of ‘judicial history.’ In his future acts and decisions, he is admonished to be more judicious in setting aside established laws, doctrines and precedents.”
