# Battles in the Supreme Court -- Ch. 7: OTHER CONSTITUTIONAL BATTLES
Date:       1998-11-01

Source:     Battles in the Supreme Court, Supreme Court Printing Services — ch. 7 (batch-04 split)
By: Artemio V. Panganiban

Aside from the foregoing cases, there are a number of other controversial cases involving the interpretation of the Constitution.

## The "Filipino First" Policy

Manila Prince Hotel v. GSIS, written by Justice Bellosillo, set the mechanism for the enforcement of the "Filipino First" policy enshrined in Article XII, Section 10, par. 2 of the 1987 Constitution, which states:

"In the grant of rights, privileges and concessions covering the national economy and patrimony, the State shall give preference to qualified Filipinos."

There was no dispute in the Court that the above clause "is a mandatory, positive command which is complete in itself and which needs no further guidelines or implementing laws or rules for its enforcement." We were also one in interpreting national patrimony as referring not only to the rich natural resources of the country, but also to the cultural heritage of Filipinos. And we said that Manila Hotel was "a living testimonial of Philippine heritage" and "a historic relic that has hosted many of the most important events in the short history of the Philippines as a nation"; thus, it was a part of our cultural heritage. This being so, the sale of 51 percent of the shares of the hotel company, shares that belonged to the government, was a state action subject to the constitutional command.

The bone of contention was the degree of preference that should be accorded to the "qualified Filipino." The majority believed that, even after having lost to a foreigner in a public bidding, the qualified Filipino still had the right to match the foreigner's offer and thereby win the auction. It operationally defined the policy in this wise:

"In the instant case, where a foreign firm submits the highest bid in a public bidding concerning the grant of rights, privileges and concessions covering the national economy and patrimony, thereby exceeding the bid of a Filipino, there is no question that the Filipino will have to be allowed to match the bid of the foreign entity. And if the Filipino matches the bid of a foreign firm, the award should go to the Filipino."

Justice Padilla, in his Concurring Opinion, expressed the same view in this manner:

"In the field of public bidding in the acquisition of things that pertain to the national patrimony, preference to qualified Filipinos must allow a qualified Filipino to match or equal the higher bid of a non- Filipino; the preference shall not operate only when the bids of the qualified Filipino and the non-Filipino are equal, in which case, the award should undisputedly be made to the qualified Filipino. The Constitutional preference should give the qualified Filipino an opportunity to match or equal the higher bid of the non-Filipino bidder if the preference of the qualified Filipino bidder is to be significant at all."

In contrast, Justice Puno, in his well-articulated Dissent, explained that Article XII, Section 10 of the Constitution, was "pro-Filipino and not anti-alien." The right of preference of the Filipino bidder would arise only if his bid tied that of the foreign bidder.

I joined Justice Puno in his Dissent, even as I expressed my own in this wise:

"1. The majority contends the Constitution should be interpreted to mean that, after a bidding process is concluded, the losing Filipino bidder should be given the right to equal the highest foreign bid, and thus to win. However, the Constitution [Sec. 10 (2), Art. XIII simply states that 'in the grant of rights xxx covering the national economy and patrimony, the State shall give preference to qualified Filipinos. The majority concedes that there is no law defining the extent or degree of such preference. Specifically, no statute empowers a losing Filipino bidder to increase his bid and equal that of the winning foreigner. In the absence of such empowering law, the majority's strained interpretation, I respectfully submit, constitutes unadulterated judicial legislation, which makes bidding a ridiculous sham where no Filipino can lose and where no foreigner can win. Only in the Philippines!

"2. Aside from being prohibited by the Constitution, such judicial legislation is shortsighted and, viewed properly, gravely prejudicial to long-term Filipino interests. It encourages other countries - in the guise of reverse comity or worse, unabashed retaliation — to discriminate against us in their own jurisdictions by authorizing their own nationals to similarly equal and defeat the higher bids of Filipino enterprises solely, while on the other hand, allowing similar bids of other foreigners to remain unchallenged by their nationals. The majority's thesis will thus marginalize Filipinos as pariahs in the global marketplace with absolutely no chance of winning any bidding outside our country. Even authoritarian ago found out that unfairness, greed and isolation are self-defeating and in the long term, self-destructing.

"The moral lesson here is simple. Do not do unto others what you do not want others to do unto you.

“3. In the absence of a law specifying the degree or extent of the 'Filipino First' policy of the Constitution, the constitutional preference for the 'qualified Filipino' may be allowed only where all the bids are equal. In this manner, we put the Filipino ahead without self-destructing him and without being unfair to the foreigner.

"In short, the Constitution mandates a victory for the qualified Filipino only when the scores are tied. But not when the ball game is over and the foreigner clearly posted the highest score."

## Censorship of Religious Television Programs

Iglesia ni Cristo v. Court of Appeals is a showcase of balancing the exercise of police power by the State vis-a-vis that of the freedom of faith and of expression by a religious denomination. In this case, the Court was faced with the issue of whether the Board of Review for Moving Pictures and Television (now Movie & Television Review & Classification Board or MTRCB) had the power to review the religious television program of the Iglesia ni Cristo and, consequently, to prohibit the airing of certain shows on grounds that "they constitute an attack against other religions and that they are indecent, contrary to law and good customs."

Examining the powers of the Board as defined under PD 1986, specifically Section 3 thereof, the Court majority, led by Justice Puno, arrived at the conclusion that the Board clearly had the power "to screen, review and examine all television programs, including religious shows. In answer to the argument that such power contravened the constitutional long-standing rule that "the exercise of religious freedom can be regulated by the State when it will bring about the clear and present danger of some substantive evil which the State is duty-bound to prevent." It held that while religious freedom was given a preferred status, like all other rights guaranteed by the Constitution, it could be enjoyed only with a proper regard for the rights of others.

But in allowing the broadcast of the subject "Ang Iglesia ni Cristo" program series, which was banned by the Board and subsequently by the trial and the appellate courts, this Court declared that, to justify the prohibition of a broadcast, it was not enough that a certain establishment attacked or of PD 1986.

The dissenters focused on prior restraint. They opined that there could be no prior restraints on the exercise of free speech, expression or religion. According to Justice Melo, any restriction by the State on these rights could not be justified, until the exercise thereof traversed that point that would endanger order in civil society. The remedy of redress, submitted by Justice Padilla, lay in the courts of law, justice and equity. Justice Kapunan further opined that censorship was a means of regulation, and as a form of prior restraint, it was anathema to a democratic society, where the freedom of religion and that of expression were constitutionally guaranteed. State interference should be allowed only in extreme circumstances.

The late Chief Justice Teehankee, in American Bible Society v. City of Manila, enunciated the standard: "The sole justification for a prior restraint or limitation on the exercise of religious freedom is the existence of a grave and present danger of a character both grave and imminent of a serious evil to public safety, public morals, public health or any other legitimate public interest that the State has a right (and duty) to prevent."

Accordingly, Justice Kapunan opined that the government, under the guise of its regulatory powers prescribed under PD 1986, could not interfere with the exercise of religious expression by requiring the submission for review of video tapes of religious programs before their public viewing, absent any showing of a compelling state interest overriding the constitutional protection of the freedom of religion and of expression.

Recognizing, however, the non-absolute character of religious freedom and, at the same time, the need to minimize restriction, Justice Kapunan proposed that subsequent sanctions be imposed only in clearly proven violations of law or excesses of protected speech. But he also pointed out that the determination of any violation or excess was a judicial function, not an administrative one exercised by an executive agency.

Justice Kapunan further criticized the reliance of the majority on Sotto v. Ruiz219 in their justification of the power of the MTRCB to review religious programs on the basis of its possession of the appropriate expertise, viz.:

"As has been said, the performance of the duty of determining whether a publication contains printed matter of a libelous character rests with the Director of Posts and involves the exercise of his judgment and discretion. Every intendment of the law is in favor of the correctness of his action. The rule is (and we go only to those cases coming from the United States Supreme Court and pertaining to the United States Postmaster General that the courts will not interfere with the decision of the Director of Posts unless clearly of the opinion that it was wrong."

In effect, Sotto placed upon the producer or exhibitor or her film or program was constitutionally protected: Such pronouncement ran counter to the fundamental principle that any act restraining speech was presumed invalid; thus, the burden of overthrowing this presumption rested on the censors.

Justice Mendoza shared the same view that administrative censorship was directly contrary to the fundamental legal tenet that until and unless speech was found by the courts to be unprotected, its expression must be allowed. He specified what might be subject to censorship: "a narrow class of cases involving pornography, excessive violence, and danger to national security." For this purpose, he conceded that the requirement to submit films and TV programs for review, prior to public exhibition or broadcasting, did not constitute prior restraint. Rather, it was a valid means of effectively enabling the State to bar the showing of unprotected films and programs in order to safeguard legitimate public interests. He insisted, though, that the power of the MTRCB was limited to deciding whether to seek from the court a prohibition of the public showing or broadcasting of a certain material in the interest of protecting morality, good order and public safety.

In my own Separate Opinion, which concurred with the majority in granting the petition, I pointed out that the statute (PD 1986) actually provided the MTRCB, in its exercise of its review power, an appropriate standard - contemporary Philippine cultural values. This standard could be used in determining whether a film or a video program would be classified as immoral, indecent, contrary to law or good customs, or injurious to the prestige of the country or its people. Meanwhile, the "clear and present danger" test could be used in determining whether the material being reviewed would encourage violence or the commission of a crime or wrong. I wrote in part:

"x x x. In exercising its prerogatives, the MTRCB cannot act absolutely or whimsically. It must act prudently. And it can do so ONLY if it exercises its powers of review and prohibition according to a standard and /or a limit.

"I believe that the phrase 'with a dangerous tendency' in Sec. 3-c of P.D. 1986 should be struck down as an unconstitutional standard. This is martial law vintage and should be replaced with the more libertarian 'clear and present danger rule' which is eloquently explained by JJ Kapunan, Puno and Mendoza (and which explanation I shall not repeat here.

"Having said that, may I respectfully point out however that there is an even more appropriate standard in the Philippine context proffered by the law itself, and that is 'contemporary Philippine cultural values.' This standard under the law should be used in determining whether a film or video program is '(a) immoral, (b) indecent, (c) contrary to law and/or good customs, and (d) injurious to the prestige of the Republic of the Philippines or its people.' On the other hand, when the question is whether the material being reviewed encourages the commission of violence or of a wrong or crime' per the enumeration contained in Sec. 3-c, the 'clear and present danger' principle should be applied as the standard in place of the 'dangerous tendency' rule.

"Just a word edgewise about cultural values. Our cultural ideals and core values of galang, pagbabahala, pananagutan, balikatan, malasakit, asal, halaga, diwa, damdamin, dangal, kapwa, pakikitungo, hiya, delikadesa, awa, tiwala, maka-Diyos, maka-tao, maka-buhay and so forth define us as a people, as Filipinos. We are who and what we are because of these values and ideals. They delimit the areas of individual and social behavior and conduct deemed acceptable or tolerable, and ultimately they determine the way we as individuals uniquely conduct our relationships and express ourselves. According to Mr. Justice Kapunan, applying contemporary Filipino values to religious thought and expression will permit an overarching into a constitutionally protected area, and provides the MTRCB with a veiled excuse for clamping down against unorthodox religious thought and expression. But such fear is highly speculative and totally unsupported by empirical evidence. I would like to add that where a mode of religious expression runs counter to such core values, serious questions have to be raised about the ultimate redeeming worth of such expression. An example is in order. Not too long ago, the so-called 'Children of God' blew into town, and, under the guise of proselytizing, practised 'flirty-fishing' (free sex). I wonder how many of us will simply sit on our hands if these 'Children' were to telecast their religious programs for OUR children to watch, or conduct seminars over the airwaves on the hows of free sex xxx. Another example: satanic cults involve blood sacrifices xxx. In brief, I am in agreement with the ponencia that the practice of religion cannot be totally abandoned in the marketplace and governed by the policy of laissez faire."

Justice Vitug aptly summed up the focal point of the majority in upholding the prerogative of the MTRCB to preview a film or a program:

"x x x. I certainly do not think that prior censorship should altogether be rejected just because sanctions can later be imposed. Regulating the exercise of a right is not necessarily anathema to it; in fact, it can safeguard and secure that right.

"x x x. I find it more prudent to have a deferment of an exhibition that may be perceived to be contrary to decency, morality, good customs or the law until, at least, the courts are given an opportunity to pass upon the matter than rely merely on the availability of retribution for actual injury sustained. A delay is not too high a price to pay for a possible damage to society that may well turn out to be incalculable and lasting."

## The Right to Privacy

In its October 13, 1997 issue, Time magazine tolled "the death of privacy" on its front cover. Bylined by Joshua Quittner, the accompanying story lamented that "[olur right to be left alone has disappeared." The people's right to privacy is invaded daily, whether through credit cards, e-mail, telephones, ATM machines, surveillance cameras in buildings and parking lots, supermarket scanners, hospital and hotel records, police blotters and even commercial satellites.

This invasion must have been in the mind of Justice Puno when, in Ople v. Torres, 21 he invalidated Administrative Order No. 308 (AO 308), which had been issued by President Fidel V. Ramos on December 12, 1996 to establish a "National Computerized Identification Reference System." Justice Puno argued that "the right to privacy is recognized and enshrined in several provisions of our Constitution" and statutes, like the Civil Code, Anti-Wire Tapping Law (RA 4200), Secrecy of Bank Deposits Act (RA 1405) and Intellectual Property Code (RA 8293).

Justice Puno further opined that the government failed "to show that AO No. 308 is justified by some compelling state interest." Contending that the national ID system "will put our people's right to privacy in clear and present danger," he bewailed that the lack of control over the use of the "vast reservoir of personal information constitutes a covert invitation to misuse, a temptation that may be just too great for some of our authorities to resist."

Rejecting the notion that AO 308 merely implemented the Administrative Code of 1987, Justice Puno wrote that said Order involved a subject "that is not appropriate to be covered by an administrative order."

Dissenting, Justices Mendoza and Kapunan argued that there was nothing in AO 308 that encroached on the right to privacy, because said issuance merely provided for the use of data already given by citizens to various government offices by reason of existing laws. The aforementioned dissenters cited the various kinds of quantifiable information gathered by the National Statistics Office (under CA 591), as well as the data required by the Land Transportation Office (under RA 4136) for the issuance of drivers' licenses and by the Department of Foreign Affairs (under RA 8239) for the processing of passports. Justice Mendoza further argued that AO 308 did not violate the freedom of thought and of conscience either. Nothing in it afforded any basis for believing that the data gathered would be used, beyond identification purposes, as "instruments of thought control."

I made it clear in my Separate Opinion that I was voting to grant the petition, only on the ground that the "subject matter contained in AO 308 is beyond the powers of the President to regulate without a legislative enactment," and that the issue — "whether a national ID system is an infringement of the constitutional right to privacy or of the freedom of thought" - would become justiciable only "after Congress passes, if ever, a law to this effect."

In the end, 8 justices voted to grant the petition and 6 voted against. Of the 8, only 4 gave their unqualified agreement to the ponencia of Justice Puno. Concurring "in the result," Justice Davide joined me in my Separate Opinion that the petition should be granted only on the ground that there was need for a legislative enactment. Justice Regalado simply concurred "in the result," while Justice Vitug wrote a Separate Opinion stating that it was "indispensible and appropriate to have the matter specifically addressed by Congress xxx."

Left undecided in the end was the issue of whether to violate the right to privacy and the freedom of thought and conscience. This issue may be raised again in a proper suit when and if the appropriate legislation is approved by Congress.
