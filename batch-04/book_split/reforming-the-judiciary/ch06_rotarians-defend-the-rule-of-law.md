# Chapter 6: Rotarians Defend the Rule of Law

In my last address during our Rotary District Conference in March 2000, I explained that Supreme Court justices had limited public appearances. Considering the nature of our work, we prefer to keep a low profile and to act quietly on matters officially brought to us by litigants.

## Perfect Rotary Attendance

Prior to my Supreme Court stint, I enjoyed perfect Rotary attendance for 12 years, from 1983 to 1995. But because of the grueling work schedule in the Court, I could not maintain my 100 percent Rotary presence. To be current in my judicial load, I have to work long days and nights seven days a week. For two reasons - namely, the low-profile nature of our job and our heavy workload - I had to decline many speaking invitations and attendance at public functions.

But District Governor Rudy Estrellado and Conference Chairman Boy Villareal were quite insistent and rather relentless in their invitation. They even sent my esteemed club president, Ed Tumangan, to visit me personally in my chambers to convince me to appear on this rostrum today. President Ed reminded me that during my address two years ago, Rotary International was represented by now RI President Richard King who, according to him, wanted me to make an encore today. Ed, who is a lawyer with outstanding credentials, suggested that I talk about the judicial reforms being instituted by the Supreme Court, with the hope that, perhaps, Rotarians might be persuaded to extend their support and lend their usual helping hand in implementing them.

On this basis, I agreed to the invitation. After all, according to the "ABCs of Rotary," authored by past RI President Cliff Dochterman, "Rotary is an organization of business and professional persons united worldwide, who provide humanitarian service, encourage high ethical standards in all vocations and help build goodwill and peace in the world." This objective of encouraging high ethical standards makes Rotary so relevant to the judiciary. Like RI, the Supreme Court enforces judicial ethics to the hilt.

Furthermore, the "ABCs of Rotary" also states that a "world-minded Rotarian defends the rule of law and order to preserve the liberty of the individual x x x." This common passion for the rule of law unites Rotarians with magistrates. It is towards this Rotary ideal to defend the rule of law that I now update you on the judicial reform program of the Philippine judiciary.

## The Judicial Reform Program

Let me start by stressing that this program was initiated on November 30, 1998, when the Honorable Hilario G. Davide J.r took his oath as Chief Justice of our country. The "Davide Watch" - as the project is popularly known — envisions "a judiciary that is independent, effective and efficient and worthy of public trust and confidence; and x x x a legal profession that provides quality, ethical, accessible and cost effective legal service to our people." It aims to improve the vocations of judges and of lawyers who are both under the direct supervision of the High Court.

Fellow Rotarians, let me stress that the "Davide Watch" is not merely a broad collection of motherhood statements and principles. Rather, it is backed up by a definite development plan implemented day after day, pursuant to an ongoing Action Program for Judicial Reforms (APJR) and monitored by a full-time Program Management Office (PMO)'. Notably, the program is actively supported not only by the Philippine government itself and local donor groups, but also by international development bodies like the United Nations Development Fund (UNDP), the World Bank, the Asia Foundation, UNICEF, the Asian Development Bank; and by government-to-government assistance from several countries including the European Union, Japan, Canada, Australia, the United States and the Netherlands.

As proof of its commitment, sincerity and determination to carry out these reforms, the Supreme Court has taken unprecedented steps to discipline members of the judiciary who fall short of the exacting standards demanded by our oaths of office.

Thus, for the first time in its 100 years of history, the Supreme Court has censured one of its own members, recently dismissed from the service a Court of Appeals justice and suspended the presiding magistrate of the Sandiganbayan from his administrative functions; not to mention the removal, the suspension, the fining and the other disciplinary sanctions imposed on many lower court judges and judicial personnel for misconduct, dishonesty, immorality, incompetence, unreasonable delay in the adjudication of cases and other administrative infractions.

But the disciplinary action and the strict supervision imposed on judges and lawyers are but one area of concern in a diverse and extensive spectrum of judicial reforms now being undertaken by the Supreme Court. There are many others. More than 50 of them have been highlighted during the various activities lined up to celebrate the centennial of the highest tribunal of our country. The exact 100th birthday of the Supreme Court was on June 11, 2001, but our celebrations - superintended by our Centenary Executive Committee which I have the honor of chairing - have been extended up to June 11 of this year.

## Coverage of Judicial Reforms

The judicial reforms that I speak of are pervasive, exhaustive and wide-ranging. They begin with law students and law schools and involve such topics as how to improve the curriculum, upgrade the bar examinations, grant financial assistance to bright but poor law students, and provide professorial chairs and endowments to law colleges. The reforms extend to the rigid screening and appointment of only the best and the brightest to the judiciary, the strict enforcement of professional and judicial ethics, the provision of periodic continuing legal education for both judges and lawyers, and the rigorous monitoring of the performance of all judicial personnel.

It has been said that to whom much is given, much is expected. But in the judiciary, the converse is also true: from whom much is expected, so also should much be given. In other words, if we expect to recruit the best and the brightest to join the judiciary and to keep them there permanently and honestly, we must also be willing to compensate them properly and supply them with the necessary tools to enable them to dispense quality justice speedily, efficiently and consistently. Thus, for the entire judiciary, the Supreme Court is asking for exemption from the Salary Standardization Law and for the appropriation of a lump sum budget, which the Court should be given the authority to chop down into line items as it may deem proper. In this manner, the High Tribunal can increase judges' compensation by simply realigning expenses without need for additional funding.

All told, the Supreme Court is trying its best to institute reforms, modernize procedures and speed up the delivery of quality justice by a corps of competent, dedicated, honest and courageous judges. To be sure, I have written three books over the last three years, discussing in detail the progress of the foregoing pursuits. Understandably, I cannot expound on all of these reforms in the twenty minutes given to me for this speech. Let me, however, mention four in which Rotarians may be especially interested to assist.

## "Zero Backlog" in the Supreme Court

First, let me discuss our Zero Backlog project. One of the major complaints of our people is the long and lethargic process of obtaining justice in the Philippines. Our Constitution gives the Supreme Court twenty-four (24) months within which to decide cases; the Court of Appeals, twelve (12) months; and the lower courts, three (3) months. Let me emphasize that these periods are counted from the time the last pleading is filed by the parties, not from the time a case is started with the filing of a complaint or petition. Through its Zero Backlog program, the Supreme Court - since I joined it in 1995 —has been able to reduce its dormant cases significantly from about 2,000 in that year to less than 50 at the moment.

Following the Supreme Court's lead, the Court of Appeals, under the leadership of Presiding Justice Alicia Austria-Martinez and with the concurrence of all the appellate justices, has recently adopted its own program to solve its backlog of cases. It is estimated that in two years the CA will be able to eradicate its cases that have long been in idle slumber.

For the trial courts, our new court administrator, Justice Presbitero J. Velasco Jr. —who, you will be happy to know, is a Rotarian himself — is now in the process of devising a strategy for stemming the increasing load of unresolved cases in the lower courts.

In my book Transparency, Unanimity & Diversity (2000) published in November 2000, I estimated - using figures given by the Office of the Court Administrator — that cases filed in the regional trial courts had outstripped cases decided, by about 6,800 per year for the five-year period 1995-1999. Worse, in the first-level courts, which include municipal courts and metropolitan trial courts, case inflow exceeded case outflow by almost 65,000 cases every year for the same five-year period (1995-1999). This simply means that in the lower courts, the delay in case adjudication has become alarmingly longer. In short, the problem has worsened over the years.

Indeed, the hands of our fellow Rotarian-Justice Velasco are full these days. And he himself — since his appointment as court administrator — has not been able to maintain his Rotary attendance. Our consolation is that he is at work, solving this problem of delayed justice in the lower courts.

## Computerization Program

Second, let me discuss the Supreme Court's Computerization Program, which is actively being monitored by another Rotarian - Atty. John Ivan Uy, chief of the Management Information Systems Office (MISO) of the Supreme Court. Recently, the Court purchased 1,500 personal computers to be distributed to all courts nationwide. These computers will be equipped with Lex Libris, the computer software that contains decisions of the Supreme Court from 1901 . With the infusion of these computers, trial courts will hopefully graduate from the outdated and cumbersome method of manual typewriting to e-age encoding and Internet surfing. Considering the availability of the facility to readily and conveniently access the latest jurisprudence, judges should be able to improve the quality of their decisions.

The judiciary also has a homepage - www.supremecourt.gov.ph - through which the latest decisions and actions of the Supreme Court may be accessed. Also, the High Court has computerized its internal administrative processes, research facilities and case tracking system.

But the computerization program goes far beyond the provision of computers to enhance the word processing, decision-writing and networking capabilities of our judges.

Courts are spread out all over the country, and the task of overseeing them is indubitably daunting. Through the computerization of the courts, the awesome task of managing their caseloads could be monitored closely and systematically. From terminals in Manila, it would be easy to see the daily calendars of all trial courts and to determine whether cases are moving speedily and satisfactorily. The Office of the Court Administrator would be able to closely supervise judges and efficiently monitor their case disposition. Indeed, computerization is a major breakthrough in the problem of delayed justice in our lower courts.

Beyond the foregoing, Congress, through the initiative of Sen. Aquilino Pimentel Jr., is mulling over the creation in the near future of pilot "e-courts" in Metro Manila and Cebu.' These courts will be ultramodern — they will be equipped with computer-aided transcription machines, video equipment, websites and even cyberspace linkup.

The Supreme Court has embarked not just on computerization, but also on the construction of better courthouses. Many lawyers, litigants, witnesses and plain citizens have chuckled at our unsanitary and oftentimes s u b h u m a n courtrooms. Trials are sometimes held in small, squalid and humid salas. Hence, the construction of decent and reasonably dignified Halls of Justice is being fast-tracked. Rotarians in our district probably know that the City of Manila does not even have its own Hall of Justice, which is still under construction on the former Jai-Alai building site on Taft Avenue.

At bottom, the judicial reform program envisions the year 2010 when, hopefully, we shall enter the age of paperless courts pioneered by countries like Singapore and Australia.

## Education Program

Third, let me also assure you that the Supreme Court is aware of the need to keep up with the new economic, commercial and scientific trends in the world. During the last three years, it has updated the Rules of Court, especially the Rules on Civil and Criminal Procedures. Recently, it has promulgated the all-new Rules on Electronic Evidence, Corporate Rehabilitation, Intra-Corporate Controversies, Family Disputes, Infringement of Intellectual Proper, Juveniles in Conflict with the Law, and others.

So, too, the High Court is conducting continuing live-in judicial seminars to educate judges on the paradigms of the new economy like deregulation, privatization and trade liberalization; as well as on the new bio-sciences, biotechnology, DNA, cloning and stem cells. Another Rotarian, PP Tito Gupit, is actively assisting the Supreme Court and the Philippine Judicial Academy in enhancing our education program for judges, lawyers and judicial personnel.

## Alternative Dispute Resolution Methods

Fourth, let me briefly discuss what we in the Supreme Court refer to as "Alternative Dispute Resolution Methods" (or ADRs). Filipinos are a litigious people. It seems that no one is satisfied until every problem or conflict is resolved by means of a full-blown litigation all the way up to the Supreme Court. Every little controversy is riddled with a "see-you-in-court" threat. That is why our court dockets are clogged. It is not surprising to find municipal trial courts loaded with 6,000 cases, mostly problems on collections of bounced checks.

To change this attitude, the Supreme Court is encouraging our people to use negotiation, arbitration and mediation as alternative modes of settling disputes. These are really not new methods, but we are calling attention to and promoting them, especially in matters where the continued good relations of litigants must be maintained after the controversy is resolved. Usually, such matters are those involving relatives and neighbors; and topics like marriage, family relations and commercial disputes.

Last year, in connection with our centenary celebrations, the Supreme Court through its education arm, the Philippine Judicial Academy, trained about 400 mediators. In March-April of 2001, they were put to work on actual court cases pending in regional trial courts in the National Capital Region, Cebu and Davao. Lo and behold, they were able to settle or, more precisely, to get the parties to agree to settle their disputes peacefully in more than 80 percent of the 1,000 cases referred to them. Some of these controversies had dragged on for over 10 years but, in less than two weeks of mediation, the parties agreed to compromise their cases. Actively at work in this area is another member of our organization, even if from another district, PP Eduardo "Dindo" de los Angeles.

The foregoing are only four of the more than 50 reform projects being undertaken to improve the dispensation of justice in our country. As I said earlier, I do not have the time to discuss all of these projects. Suffice it to say that the Philippine Supreme Court's program has attracted a lot of support and commendation internationally and has become the envy of many countries.

## 9th Conference of Chief Justices

On October 4-8, 2001, in Christchurch, New Zealand, I attended on behalf of Chief Justice Davide, who was indisposed at that time, the 9th Conference of Chief Justices of Asia and the Pacific. During that Conference, the Philippines became a role model for the formulation and the implementation of a judicial upgrading program. In a speech delivered by Dato Param Cumaraswamy, the UN special rapporteur on the independence of judges, the Philippine judiciary was praised for its reform project and strong independence. In a similar speech before the same forum, a World Bank executive, Mr. Anthony Toft, lauded the "Davide Watch" as worthy of support by the international donor community.

After that session on judicial reform in Christchurch, I was literally mobbed by the other delegates who extended their felicitations and asked these questions: "How were you able to secure the endorsement and the support of the UN and the World Bank? Can you advise us on how to get assistance from international donors?"

I was, of course, never more proud of being a Filipino jurist because of our highly acclaimed judicial reform program. However, I believe the Supreme Court will be even prouder and more honored if our own people, led by the dedicated and ever-supportive Rotarians, will likewise lend a hand in implementing our program. As I close this address, let me reiterate my invitation to Rotarians and rotary clubs to help the Supreme Court in implementing its judicial reform program, so that the aspirations behind our endeavors may finally be realized. With your active cooperation, our High Court will be able to bring about positive changes in the judicial system and to speedily deliver quality justice that our people deserve.

Maraming salamat po.
