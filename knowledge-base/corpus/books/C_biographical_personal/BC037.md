# Leadership by Example: The Davide Standard -- Ch. 4: Chief Justice Davide: An Introduction

During the past several days, the media — both broadcast and print — have heralded the life story of our guest of honor. Newspapers, TV and radio stations have given us a ringside glimpse of our guest speaker’s life and background: (1) that he hails from Cebu, got his elementary and secondary education there, and finished his law degree at the University of the Philippines; (2) that he was elected as an opposition member of the Batasang Pambansa during the rapacious Marcos years; (3) that he was a member of both the Constitutional Convention of 1971 and the Constitutional Commission of 1986 and is thus a seasoned constitutionalist; (4) that he served as Comelec chair and headed the fact-finding commission that thoroughly investigated the almost-successful 1989 coup attempt; (5) that he is happily married to the vivacious and charming Virginia Jimenea-Perez and has three sons and two daughters with her; and finally, (6) that tears of joy flowed from the eyes of his 95-year old father as he learned of the appointment of his namesake to the highest judicial office of our Republic.

All of these you already know, and therefore I will not repeat them. Rather, let me introduce our speaker the way I have known him during our three years together in the highest court of the land. I was fortunate to have sat with him not only during our en banc meetings in the Court, but also during our sessions in the Divisions to which I had been assigned — the Court’s Third and then First Divisions which he had chaired.

Our guest of honor acts and lives simply and transparently. He does not drink, does not smoke, does not gamble, and rarely attends social occasions outside official functions. His favorite sport and exercise is brisk walking early in the morning. It is easy to get along with him because he is humble and soft-spoken, almost self-deprecating but at the same time self-actualized. He is aware of who he is and of what he can do or cannot do. He has a vision of what needs to be done and a clear concept of how to accomplish it. He has no cravings for money or for the luxuries that money can buy, because he has been blessed with an abundance of the things that money cannot buy, like integrity, wisdom, humility, patriotism and a keen sense of righteousness.

Indeed, our guest of honor is a man of many talents and qualities. But in my view, two things stand out in his character as a jurist. First, his THOROUGHNESS and, second, his MORAL COURAGE.

1. His thoroughness and meticulousness is demonstrated by his ponencias, which are loaded with relevant details. He is an avid and fast reader with an amazing capacity to absorb and internalize facts as well as to analyze legal provisions. He is a tireless worker who starts his day upon waking at 3:30 a.m. and is among the first to arrive in the Court at 6:30 a.m. And he works ceaselessly until evening. He is always prepared — whether to defend his positions or to challenge those of others. Being thorough means not only being prepared for what is on the agenda for the day, but also remembering what was discussed or agreed upon in the past. Indeed, he has an elephantine to memory and a gluttonous appetite for the minutiae of judicial work which he can digest even more easily. He impressed me during one of our deliberations during which he quoted from memory some footnotes and asides from my own ponencias. He dissects issues with a surgeon’s scalpel and moves his pen with laser-like precision, resulting in decisions and opinions that are almost flawless and always to the point.

2. Let me now discuss his moral courage. People often criticize elected policymakers for their lack of political will — that dogged willingness to implement laws and policies regardless of their adverse effects on strong and well-connected vested groups. What political will is to a sovereign head, moral courage is to a magistrate. A judge may have memorized all the statutes and regulations; he may know by heart the Constitution and the codes; he may even have imprinted on his brain the UN Charter and the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. But if he has no moral courage — if he does not have the willingness to battle the establishment; to carry on in spite of seemingly insurmountable opposition; to be a beacon for the weak, the oppressed and the disadvantaged; and to stand for his conviction against all odds, even against all his colleagues and the entire government machinery put together — he is really nothing.

And moral courage our guest has an abundance of. It seems to me that he was born with it and that he thrives in it. To have moral courage means not only theoretical bravery in battle but, a priori, a purity of spirit and a clarity of conscience. For only he who is pure of heart and clear of mind, possessed of integrity beyond suspicion and with no skeletons to hide, can afford to challenge the gods. But our guest has not only challenged the gods; he himself has, in the process, become the loftiest of the Olympian “gods of Padre Faura.”

Ladies and gentlemen, it is my honor and privilege to introduce the Chief Justice of the Republic of the Philippines, the Honorable Hilario G. Davide Jr.

## Summary

Chapter 4 of 'Leadership by Example: The Davide Standard' reproduced the speech with which Justice Panganiban introduced Chief Justice Hilario G. Davide Jr. as guest of honor. He began by listing the six biographical facts the broadcast and print media had already heralded -- Davide's Cebu schooling and law degree from the University of the Philippines; his election as an opposition member of the Batasang Pambansa during the Marcos years; his membership in both the 1971 Constitutional Convention and the 1986 Constitutional Commission, making him a seasoned constitutionalist; his service as Comelec chair and as head of the commission that investigated the almost-successful 1989 coup attempt; his marriage to Virginia Jimenea-Perez and their five children; and the tears of joy of his 95-year old father -- and then declined to repeat them, choosing instead to introduce the speaker as he had known him during three years together in the Court, in en banc meetings and in the Third and First Divisions Davide chaired. He described a man who lives simply and transparently, who does not drink, smoke or gamble, rarely attends social occasions outside official functions, walks briskly in the early morning, and is humble and soft-spoken yet self-actualized, with no craving for money because he is rich in integrity, wisdom, humility, patriotism and a sense of righteousness. Two things, he said, stood out in Davide's character as a jurist. The first was thoroughness: ponencias loaded with relevant detail, fast and avid reading, a day begun at 3:30 a.m. and an arrival at the Court by 6:30 a.m., an elephantine memory that once quoted back footnotes from CJP's own ponencias, and a pen of laser-like precision. The second was moral courage, which he defined as being to a magistrate what political will is to a sovereign head -- the willingness to battle the establishment, to be a beacon for the weak, and to stand by conviction against all odds -- and which he traced to purity of spirit and clarity of conscience. He closed by saying that the guest had not merely challenged the gods but had become the loftiest of the Olympian gods of Padre Faura.
