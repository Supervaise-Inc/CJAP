# Leveling the Playing Field -- Ch. 18: Sanlakas v. Executive Secretary: Constitutionality of a “State of Rebellion”

By: Artemio V. Panganiban

On May 1, 2001, “an angry and violent mob armed with explosives, firearms, bladed weapons, clubs, stones and other deadly weapons assaulted and attempted to break into Malacañang.” As one of the measures to suppress this uprising, sometimes referred to as “EDSA 3,” President Gloria Macapagal Arroyo declared a “state of rebellion” through Proclamation No. 38 and General Order No. 1. Five days thereafter, she lifted her own declaration. Thus, because of their mootness, the Petitions in Lacson v. Perez — challenging her constitutional authority to issue the declaration — was dismissed by the Court.

## The Facts

On July 27, 2003, another armed confrontation — the “Oakwood incident,” took place. In the wee hours of that day, some three hundred officers and enlisted men of the Armed Forces of the Philippines (AFP) stormed into the Oakwood Premiere Apartments in the financial center of Makati City. They demanded, among other things, the resignation of the President, the secretary of the Department of National Defense, and the chief of the Philippine National Police. Consequently, the Chief Executive issued Proclamation No. 427 and General Order No. 4, both declaring a state of rebellion and calling out the AFP “to suppress the rebellion.”

By the evening of July 27, 2003, the Oakwood incident ended and the soldiers agreed to return to their barracks. However, the state of rebellion was lifted only on August 1, 2003, through Proclamation No. 435. In the interim, four Petitions were filed before the Supreme Court challenging the constitutionality of Proclamation No. 427 and General Order No. 4. The suits were consolidated and will be collectively referred to in this book as Sanlakas v. Executive Secretary.

## “Capable of Repetition Yet Evading Review”

Mr. Justice Dante O. Tinga, to whom the Petitions had been raffled, agreed with the Office of the Solicitor General (OSG) that the consolidated cases had been tendered moot by the issuance of Proclamation No. 43 declaring that the state of rebellion had already "ceased to exist” Nevertheless, he wrote a ponencia tackling i merits of the controversy, because the constitutional question raised was “capable of repetition yet evading review.”

“To prevent similar questions from reemerging,” he urged the Court “to seize this opportunity to f lay to rest the validity of the declaration of a state of rebellion in the exercise of the President’s calling out power x x x.”

## The Ruling

The Tinga ponencia held that Section 18 of Article VII of the Constitution “grants the President, as Commander-in-Chief, a sequence of graduated powers. From the most to the least benign, these are as follows: the calling out power, the power to suspend the privilege of the writ of habeas corpus, and the power to declare martial law. In the exercise of the latter two powers, the Constitution requires the concurrence of two conditions, namely, an actual invasion or rebellion, and that public safety requires the exercise of such power.”

However, as observed in Integrated Bar of the Philippines v. Zamora, "[t]hese conditions are not required in the exercise of the calling out power. The only criterion is that “whenever it becomes necessary, the President may call the armed forces ‘to prevent or suppress lawless violence, invasion or rebellion.” Section 18 of Article VII does not expressly prohibit the President from declaring a state of rebellion. Furthermore, the Constitution vests upon the President not only commander-in-chief powers but, first and foremost, executive powers.

Tracing the historical roots of the powers to the US Constitution, Justice Tinga held that “the Commander-in-Chief powers are broad enough as it is and become more so when taken together with the provision on executive power and the presidential oath of office. Thus, the plenitude of the powers of the presidency equips the occupant with the means to address exigencies or threats which undermine the very existence of government or the integrity of the State.”

The ‘Tinga ponencia cited Marvos v. Manglapus, which ruled that the President “has x x x unstated residual powers which are implied from the grant of executive power and which are necessary for her to comply with her ties under the Constitution. The powers of the Pre dent are not limited to what are expressly enumerated in the article on the Executive Department and in scattered provisions of the Constitution. This is so, notwithstanding the avowed intent of the members of the Constitutional Commission of 1986 to limit the powers of the President as a reaction to the abuses under the regime of Mr. Marcos, for the result was a limitation of specific powers of the President, particularly those relating to the commander-in-chief clause, but not a diminution of the general grant of executive power.”

The conclusion was thus inevitable: the President’s authority to declare a state of rebellion sprang from her powers as Chief Executive and, at the same time, drew strength from her commander-in-chief powers, which were purely executive powers vested in her by Sections 1 and 18 of Article VII of the Constitution.

The Tinga ponencia further held that “in calling out the armed forces, a declaration of a state of rebellion is an utter superfluity. At most, it only gives notice to the nation that such a state exists and that the armed forces may be called to prevent or suppress it x x x. This Court finds that such a declaration is devoid of any legal significance. For all legal intents, the declaration is deemed not written.”

It also clarified that the declaration of a state of rebellion did not diminish or violate constitutionally pro-tested rights. If a state of martial law would not suspend the operation of the Constitution or automatically suspend the privilege of the writ of habeas corpus, then with more reason would a simple declaration of a state of rebellion not bring about those conditions.

In conclusion, the ponencia disposed thus: “WHEREFORE, the Petitions are hereby DISMISSED.”

## The Separate Opinions

## The Vitug Opinion

Justice Jose C. Vitug wrote a two-page Separate Opinion “in complete agreement” with the ponencia. But he found it “necessary to emphasize” that the proclamation of a state of rebellion does not countenance the commission of acts “violative of fundamental rights.”

## My Separate Opinion

Joined by Justices Quisumbing and Callejo, I also wrote a Separate Opinion arguing that the Petitions should be dismissed, but only on the ground of mootness.

The subsequent lifting of the questioned issuances by the President rendered the Petitions moot; thus, the latter should be dismissed on that ground, I said. There was no more extant proclamation or order that could be declared valid or void. Nothing would be cane breathing life into a dead issue.

Moreover, without a justiciable controversy, the Petitions had become pleas for declaratory relief, over which the Supreme Court had no original jurisdiction.

On the theory that the state-of-rebellion issue “capable of repetition yet evading review,” the question could indeed still be resolved even after the lifting of the Proclamation and Order, provided that the party raising it in a proper case was and/or continued to be prejudiced as a direct result of their issuance.

In the present case, the petitioners did not show that they had been or continued to be directly and pecuniarily prejudiced by the Proclamation and Order. If this controversy had emanated from an appealed judgment from a lower tribunal, then the Supreme Court could have still passed upon the issue on the theory that “it is capable of repetition yet evading review.” In this instance, the case would not have been an original action for declaratory relief. In short, the theory of “capable of repetition yet evading review” may be invoked only when the Supreme Court has jurisdiction over the subject matter.

The mootness of the Petitions swept aside the necessity of ruling on the validity of Proclamation No. 427 and General Order No. 4. In the wake of such mootness, the constitutionality issue ceased to be the lis moa or the unavoidable question in the resolution of the case.

## The Santiago Opinion

Justice Consuelo Ynares-Santiago wrote a Separate Opinion, which argued that the presidential declaration was “null and void for having been issued with grave abuse of discretion.”

She contended that the assailed Proclamation an Order had been used to justify a “search and destroy operation” conducted by the military “from July 27 to August 1, 2003.” On that basis, “Ramon Cardenas, as assistant executive secretary in the previous administration was arrested, presented to the media in handcuffs and brought for inquest proceedings before the Department of Justice (DOJ) in the morning of July 28.” Other prominent personalities, like “Senator Gringo Honasan,” “Senator Loi Ejercito” and “Mayor JV Ejercito — were also being linked to the attempted uprising.”

Justice Santiago further contended:

“The majority ignored the fact that the ‘state of rebellion’ declared by the President was in effect five after the peaceful surrender of the militant group.”

“The declaration of a state of rebellion does not have any legal meaning or consequence. The declaration does not give the President any extra powers. It does not have any good purpose.”

“The purpose of the declaration and its duration as far as the overeager authorities were concerned was only to give legal cover to effect warrantless arrests even if the ‘state of rebellion’ or the instances stated in Rule 113 of Section 5 of the Rules are absent or no longer exist.”

## The Gutierrez Opinion

Like Justice Santiago, Justice Angelina SandovalGutierrez submitted a Separate Opinion that voted to grant, not dismiss, the Petitions. ‘The latter opined that Proclamation No. 427 and General Order No. 4 were unconstitutional on the following grounds:

1. There was no provision in the Constitution granting the President any authority to declare a state of rebellion. “Adopting an unorthodox measure unbounded and not canalized by the language of the Constitution is dangerous.”

“In a society which adheres to the rule of law, resorting to extra-constitutional measures is unnecessary where the law has provided everything for any emergency or contingency.”

2. The US cases cited in the Tinga ponencia were not applicable to this case. Quoted hereunder is a portion of her Opinion explaining her argument:

“Clearly, the trail of U.S. jurisprudence does not support the view that the ‘Executive and Commander-in-Chief clauses’ of the Constitution grant the President such broad power as to give her the option of disregarding the other restrictive provisions of the Constitution. The purpose of the Constitution is not only to grant power, but to keep it from getting out of hand. The policy should be — where the Constitution has laid down specific procedures on how the President should deal with a crisis, it is imperative that he must follow those procedures in meeting the crisis. These procedures serve as limitations to what would otherwise be an unbounded exercise of power.

## Epilogue

With the rendition of the Decision and the rare Opinions, was it clear that the Supreme Court was upholding the constitutionality of the presidential declaration of a state of rebellion?

Judging by the voting of the justices, the answer does not appear to be in the affirmative. Note that only four of them — Justices Vitug, Carpio, Corona and Morales — fully concurred with Justice Tinga. Three — Chief Justice Davide and Justices Puno and Martinez — concurred only “in the result.” On the other hand, two justices — Quisumbing and Callejo — joined me in my Sepa rare Opinion that the Petitions should be dismissed, but only on the ground of mootness. ‘Two others — Santiago and Gutierrez — dissented totally and voted to grant the Petitions. One justice, Azcuna, was on leave. ‘There was one vacancy in the Court.

In sum, the Petitions were dismissed by a vote of 11 to 2, with one abstention. Only five, however — Justices Tinga, Vitug, Carpio, Corona and Morales — voted expressly in favor of the constitutionality of those Petitions. Contrary to the expressed wishes of the ponente, there was sufficient reason to believe that the constitutionality question had not been definitively laid to rest.
