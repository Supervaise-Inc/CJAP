# Love God, Serve Man -- Ch. 11: Lay Empowerment in Building a Community of Believers

(Editor's note: From January 20, 1991 to February 17, 1991, the Catholic Church convened the Second Plenary Council of the Philippines [PCP II] for the purpose of "renewing" the Church and of approving new laws and directions to make it more relevant to the 21st century. For the first time in its 2,000 - year history, the Church invited lay people to participate in its law-making. The author, who was then secretary of the Council of the Laity of the Philippines - the federation of all Catholic lay organizations - was one of these lay delegates. In this speech before the National Convention of Dominican and Siena Schools in the country held on November 27, 1992 at the Siena College in Quezon City, Attorney Panganiban spoke on the role of lay people in upbuilding the Church as seen by PCP II).

I accepted with great hesitancy this mission of speaking before you today. Although I have had the pleasure of talking before civic groups like the Rotary, Jaycees, Lions, etc., before lawyers and other professionals, before business and political organizations and even before the masses in Plaza Miranda and Plaza Moriones (in Manila), I seldom accept speaking engagement before educators, nuns and priests.

I believe I have a cogent reason for my hesitancy. A survey conducted not too long ago revealed that the professionals regarded with utmost credibility were the men and women of cloth - the priests and nuns. They were first; and teachers, second. At the bottom of the totem pole were the politicians last; and lawyers, second to the last.

Which reminds me of a story. You know, ballooning has become a favorite sport in Europe. For those of you who have traveled to Europe, you probably have noticed huge balloons in the sky with wooden or plastic carts attached to them where about four people can ride. These are kept afloat by hot air that is propelled by a jet flame pointed upwards to the lower opening of the giant ballon. During the cold war, these were a favorite means of escape of fugitives in communist regimes to the Free World. Anyway, two ladies indulged in their favorite balloon one early morning. They enjoyed their ride so much they did not realize they were floating over international boundaries. In Europe, especially in small countries like Belgium, Luxembourg and Liechtenstein, boundaries could be crossed by balloons in a few hours. So after several hours, our lady balloonists suddenly realized they were lost. They lowered their balloon to ask for information. Fortunately, they saw a man below. When they were only a few meters above him, they shouted:

"Sir, we have been riding in our balloon for many hours. We were blown here by a strong wind and we are lost. Can you please tell us where we are?"

"My dear ladies," replied the man, "you are in a wooden cart attached to a balloon!"

The ladies retorted, "Sir, you must be a lawyer!"

"That is correct. But how did you know that?

And the ladies thundered back, "Sir, we know for certain that you are a lawyer because what you just said was very accurate but entirely useless to us."

This lamentation about lawyers being useless hair-splitters and consummate talkers has hounded us over the centuries. It is not surprising that one epitaph in an English cemetery reads as follows, "Here lies lawyer John Quill; he lies still."

But you teachers belong to a noble profession. For your influence is eternal. That is why you are regarded with such high credibility. What you teach the young is repeated by them to their children, and their children to their children's children. That is why, of the many professions He could be, Jesus chose to be a teacher. Remember, He was called "Rabbi," which in Hebrew means teacher.

## Building a Community of Believers

I was asked by Sister Ma. Aurelia Navarro, O.P., superintendent of the 26 O.P. Siena Schools in the Philippines, to speak on "Lay Empowerment in Building a Community of Believers" as decreed by the Second Plenary Council of the Philippines or PCP II and by the Second Vatican Council or Vatican II, for short.

The first question which may be asked is, who are the lay faithful? According to the Vatican II document entitled Lumen Gentium (3), laity refers to all who have been baptized in the Catholic Church, except those in Holy Orders and those who belong to a religious state approved by the Church. More simply, a lay person is one who is baptized according to the rites of our Church but who is not a priest, a religious, a brother or a deacon.

Vatican II and PCP II now call our Church the "People of God." If indeed the Church is "God's People," then the laity certainly make up the overwhelming 99 percent of its membership.

## Two Fundamental Aspects

of Lay Empowerment PCP II (405) points to two fundamental aspects of the lay vocation, namely, (1) the full belonging of the lay faithful to the Church and its mystery and (2) the lay vocation is secular in character. Let me discuss the first aspect. The lay faithful are as much members of the Church as the hierarchy. For many decades,

lay involvement in the Church was regarded only as a participation in the apostolate of the hierarchy. But PCP II is clear: the lay faithful are not second-class members. They are not mere deputies or assistants or helpers of priests. They are co-workers and collaborators in the service of the Church. In fact, they are equal in dignity and in mission to the clergy.

This equality does not do away with the ecclesiastical hierarchy. But this hierarchy is a hierarchy of service and not of excellence. As the PCP document says: "A person is not a better Christian simply because he is a bishop, presbyter or deacon. The Christian dignity of the ordained is measured by the sincerity and genuineness of their service rather than by the office they hold." (PCP II, 96).

Because of this equality, the lay people no longer need any specific mandate from the hierarchy in order to fulfill their mission as Christians - which is, to be evangelized and thereafter, to evangelize others. They are no longer merely the objects, but are themselves the subjects or participants of evangelization. Hence, the various Catholic lay organizations like the Council of the Laity of the Philippines, Catholic Women's League, Apostleship of Prayer, etc., are no longer called "mandated" organizations, but only "traditional" organizations because they no longer need a specific mandate or order or instruction from the hierarchy to be able to organize and to function.

## Lay Faithful Shall Remain Secular

The second fundamental aspect of the lay vocation is its secular character. Lumen Gentium (31) and Christifideles Laici (15) require the lay faithful "to seek the Kingdom of God by engaging in temporal affairs and ordering them according to the plan of God... (hence) the world becomes the place and the means for the lay faithful to fulfill their Christian vocation." There are

many areas, particularly in the temporal sphere, where an empowered laity could do wonders, and where, by their peculiar circumstances, the clergy are restricted. These include politics, government, business, industry and finance. It is in these fields where Christ can be more effectively brought by the witness and fervor of lay people. Because this is their world; this is their milieu.

The lesson here is that we, the lay faithful, cannot retreat from our milieu simply because it is sinful or dirty. Lay people do not leave the state in which they were living at the time they were called by the Lord (1 Cor. 7:20). Hence, lawyers must stay as lawyers and bring other lawyers to Christ. Christians cannot shun politics because it is dirty. They must stay there, clean it up and bring forth the Gospel of Christ. Teachers must find their holiness and spirituality in the world of teaching. Incidentally, the Scriptures (in the Epistle of James, Chapter 3) say "not many of you should become teachers, my brothers; you should realize that those of us who do so will be called to stricter account." That is why your superior credibility entails a greater responsibility. Hence, you are subject to stricter account.

In this connection, I remember that two years ago, my daughter Mabel talked to me and said, "Daddy, I wish to talk with you." She explained that she felt the unmistakable call of our Lord for her to be a numerary in the Opus Dei. After listening to a 20-minute lecture on what Opus Dei does and what a numerary's role is, I faintly responded, "Hija, bakit hindi ka na lang maging madre? It is difficult to be a numerary because you practically assume the religious vows of poverty, chastity and obedience, and yet you stay in the world, unprotected by the nuns' habit and the reverence accorded to them. Besides, it is difficult to keep the holiness of the religious while surrounded by the lust and greed of the lay environment."

"But Daddy," pleaded Mabel, "the Lord called me to remain lay, not to be a religious."

I did not understand then that those called to be numeraries do not lose their lay character and that they must perform their apostolic mission amidst the noise and banging of the world.

## What Does It Mean to be Empowered?

Now that we know the meaning of being "lay," the next question is, what does it mean to be "empowered" and from whom does this "empowerment" come from?

Again, we turn to Vatican II and PCP II. By their baptism, strengthened by their confirmation, the lay faithful are incorporated into Christ. Thus, they derive their empowerment direct from Christ. To quote Lumen Gentium again, they "share in the priestly, prophetic and kingly office of Christ." (LG 31) By their baptism, the lay faithful enter and constitute the bigger part of the Church, becoming, to quote the first book of Peter, Chapter 2, "a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation," a people whom God has set apart to proclaim His greatness.

"It is clear (a) that the apostolate of the laity is of divine right; (b) that it is Christ who calls the laity to full participation in the life of the Church and full commitment to (its) mission, and (c) that this call is by virtue of Baptism and Confirmation." (PCP II, 408).

## To be Christ to Others

By their empowerment, the lay faithful are not elevated in status. Their responsibility to be evangelized and their corresponding duty to evangelize is merely emphasized. To paraphrase Sta. Teresa of Avila, the laity are given the work of being Christ to others, so that others may be Christ to still many others.

Because of this empowerment, we now witness a new breed of lay leaders who are forming and leading new movements, employing new methods, and displaying new zeal in bringing Christ in the charismatic communities, the Focolare, Christian family movement, marriage encounter, parish renewal experience (PREX) and other spirited lay organizations.

Yes, brothers and sisters, it is uplifting to know of our lay empowerment. After all, Jesus was himself a lay person. And the first Filipino saint, St. Lorenzo Ruiz, was himself a layman. PCP II itself was an outstanding example of lay empowerment, because, for the first time in the almost 2,000-year history of our Church, lay men and women were invited and actually participated in the lawmaking of our Church. Prior to PCP II, and even during Vatican II, only bishops participated and voted in plenary councils. But during PCP II, laymen, and for that matter, the priests and the religious, debated and voted even if only in a "consultative" way during the deliberations.

## Effective Exercise

of Lay Empowerment But it is one thing to possess empowerment. It is quite another to use it effectively. What indeed is the object of knowing lay empowerment if it is not effectively used for evangelization, for building a community of believers - the very reason for which such empowerment was bequeathed by our Lord? Similarly, of what use is a lamp if it is covered and hidden in the night? As our Lord exhorted His disciples in His Sermon on the Mount: "You are the light of the world. A city set on a hill cannot be hidden. Men do not light a lamp and then put it under a bushel basket. They set it on a stand where it gives light to all in the house. In the same way, your light must shine before men so that they may see goodness in your acts and give praise to your heavenly Father." (Matt.5:14-16).

And so we ask the question: what must lay people have or

do to enable them to exercise their empowerment more effectively? Answer: there are four things; namely, (1) genuine conversion of mind and heart, (2) renewed catechesis, (3) renewed worship and (4) renewed social apostolate. Permit me to explain each of these.

Genuine Conversion of Mind and Heart Conversion is a turning away from our worldly ways to the ways of Christ. It is a "cal to a change of heart, metanoia, a profound transformation of one's way of life so that it would be in accord with the Gospel." It is speaking with Jesus' words, loving with His love and living with His life. Conversion often takes a whole lifetime; it is a day-to-day grinding, grasping and following of Christ. Normally, it starts in childhood catechism, through high school and college apologetics. Sometimes however, we hear of instant conversions, especially among charismatic and marriage encounter graduates. Almost instantly, by God's miracle, a sinner suddenly repents, a prodigal son returns, a philandering husband reforms. This is not really unusual. Such was the case of the apostles Peter, Andrew, James and John who immediately abandoned their nets when they heard the call of Jesus. Of St. Paul on that historic road to Damascus. And of the repentant thief to whom the gentle Jesus, gasping for His last words, said, "I assure you, this day you will be with me in paradise." (Luke 23:43)

These "instant" conversions remind me of a conversation between a new instant convert and a traditional Catholic. Asked the latter: "Now, Johnny, you said you were converted to Christ after you attended a Life in the Spirit seminar. Tell me, in what year of the Gregorian calendar was Jesus born?"

"Gee, I do not know," was the timid reply of Johnny.

"Well, Johnny, you say you are a Christian. Can you tell me

what nationality Jesus would have had under present citizenship tules?" the traditional Catholic (who was probably a lawyer) asked again.

"I am sorry, I do not know either," Johnny answered, with no small amount of embarrassment.

"I'll ask you one more question. What was the age of Jesus when he died?"

"I am sorry. I do not know either."

The traditional Catholic raised his forefinger and mockingly pointed it to Johnny, "If you do not know all these simple things, how can you claim to be a Christian?"

This time, Johnny gulped for a moment and replied, "Before my encounter with Jesus, I was a womanizer, an inveterate gambler and a habitual drunkard. But after I met Him and accepted Him as my Lord and Savior, I stopped drinking and gambling. And now there's only one woman in my life - my beautiful wife whom the Lord has given me to love and to cherish till death do us part."

A genuine conversion of heart Johnny had, but he also needs to educate his mind. His dramatic encounter with our Lord changed his ways. He turned away from his sins and by his new life, he now gives witness for Christ. This conversion will be shortlived, however, if he does not continuously nourish his mind with knowledge of Christ; which brings us to the second essential.

## Renewed Catechesis

The second element of effective lay empowerment is renewed catechesis. Please note it is not catechism but catechesis. Renewed catechesis in turn has four characteristics, namely - first, Christo-centric; second, rooted in the Word of God; third, authentically Filipino; and fourth, systematic.

To be Christo-centric means to place Christ at the center of our life, our work, our study, our everyday activity. The sacraments, the saints, the Blessed Mother are all subordinate to Him and are to be invoked only if they lead us to Him. Jesus was not just a onceupon-a-time historical figure who walked the earth 2,000 years ago. His command, "Come follow me," is as true today as it was true two millenniums ago. He is alive here and now. Yes, Jesus lives! Jesus rules! Jesus reigns! Jesus sees us! He hears us! He feels with us! Here and now! Amongst us. Within us. In this very hall. Amen?

To put Christ in the center necessarily means to be rooted in the Word of God because the Bible is the major source of our knowledge about Him. Through the Bible, we learn of the life and teachings of Jesus. The Bible indeed must be the primary book of catechesis. The newspapers recently reported that the Holy See will be issuing a new book of catechesis soon. Let us all look forward to getting a copy.

Catechesis must be authentically Filipino. It must underlie and mix with our unique culture, times and needs. It is not merely an academic exercise but must respond to the challenges of daily living. It must answer the burning questions of the day, like the imposition of the death penalty, the right to life of the unborn and the untangling of the debt burden of our country.

Finally, catechesis must be systematic. What is taught in grade school cannot be the same as that in college. It must be presented in an orderly and programmed manner. As teachers, you are more familiar with this because you have systematic syllabi and lesson plans for your daily work.

## Renewed Worship

The third element of effective lay empowerment (or evangelization) is renewed worship. Our worship is done in three ways: it is personal, it is familial, it is communal. It is conversation with

God, asking Him not only to grant our petitions but praying that we learn to listen to His voice; to know His will for us in our day-today activities. It is conversing with Him about our greatest worries, like whether to change jobs or not, or about such mundane topics as where to park or what road to take to avoid traffic. I used to believe that God was someone apart - so powerful and so great that I could not bother Him with my little problems. But now I know that our God is a personal God, a caring God. There is no problem so big He cannot solve it. There is no or concern so small He does know it. As the Psalmist sings, "O, Lord, You have probed me and You know me; You know when I sit and when I stand; You understand my thoughts from afar." (Ps. 139:1-2).

Many times we use God to achieve our own selfish designs. We first determine by ourselves what we want and then demand it from Him. But Jesus taught us to pray, "Thy will be done on earth as it is in heaven." And we reply to Him: "Yes Lord, my prayer is that Your will, not mine, be done in my life. Let my life be a continuous worship of you, in the church, in my home, in my office and in my leisure. Amen."

## Renewed Social Apostolate

The fourth and final element is a renewed social apostolate. We cannot go to heaven alone. We need to bring our fellows. We are called to promote human development, justice and peace. As the evangelist Matthew records, during the final judgment, God will not ask us how many digits there were in our bank book, or how many Mercedeses and BMWs we owned, or how many PhDs we obtained, or how high our position was in our government. He will ask what we did for the least of our brethren: "Did you feed the hungry... clothe the naked... shelter the homeless... heal the sick... and comfort the prisoners?" (Cf. Matt. 25:31-46).

As the song goes, "We fought the fight, we ran the race. Then at last we'll see Jesus face to face. Our eyes will clear, we'll

fin'lly see that it's only what we've done for Christ that will last eternally."

Brothers and sisters, it is not enough to know that we, the laity, have been empowered by Christ through our baptism. What is even more important is whether we exercise our empowerment effectively; whether we have undergone and continue to undergo genuine conversion, renewed catechesis, renewed worship and renewed social apostolate. All these four elements must be present in our lives. As PCP II declared, catechesis without social apostolate will not transform. Without worship, catechesis will become mere indoctrination. On the other hand, worship without catechesis will degenerate into formalism and even superstition; and social apostolate without catechesis and worship is mere activism much akin to civic work.

So far, we have discussed firstly, the two aspects of lay empowerment; namely, that the laity is as much a part of the Church as the hierarchy and that the laity must continue with its secular work; and secondly, the four essentials needed for the effective exercise of lay empowerment; namely, genuine conversion, renewed catechesis, renewed worship and renewed social apostolate.

Four Aspects of the Laity's

Role and Life Now, the final question may be asked: in what areas of life is the laity being called to exercise effectively its lay empowerment?

Answer: The lay faithful are called by Vatican II and by PCP II to serve, evangelize and transform their families and the world. In more detail, according to PCP II, we the laity, are called firstly to a Community of Families; secondly, we are called to Christian presence in the World; thirdly, we are called to service and evangelization; and, fourthly, we are called to social transformation.

## Called to a Community of Families

"The family is the Church in the home. It is the primary

community of Christ's disciples whose members are bound together not only by ties of flesh and blood but by grace and obedience to the Father's will... The family is where the journey in faith-life is initiated and guided towards maturity." (PCP II, 421).

In the family is taught the equal obligation of both husband and wife to fidelity as well as to responsible procreation and education of children. Upon the solidarity and unity of the family rests the future of the Church and of mankind itself. How true indeed, the Church is really a family of families.

Called to Christian Presence The second and larger area of lay empowerment is the community at large, the entire world.

"In the struggles and joys of their day-to-day living, in the realities and activities of the people, the laity provides the world with a variety of ways of living and sharing our faith. Through them and in their situations, the Church finds and fulfills her mission in the world. Their insight and experiences, their social and political choices, if in accord with the Gospel, effectively shape our society and our world to the image of the Kingdom." (PCP II, 425).

"How the lay faithful live with conflict, how they accept pain, and even find joy in the midst of their pain because they have the Lord to cling to for solace and liberation, intensify our hope in the redemptive power of God's

love. Where people suffer and celebrate, there Christ is embraced in His cross and resurrection." (PCP II, 426).

In short, we are called to live our faith, to show the world by our example, not just by words, that Christ is alive and acting in and through us. As St. Paul wrote: "It is no longer I who lives in my mortal body, but Christ who dwells in me." (Cf. Gal. 2:20). Amen.

Called to Service and Evangelization The third area where the lay are called is in service and evangelization.

All Christians, as I have already said, have a duty to be evangelized and to evangelize others. "The laity's field of evangelizing activity is the vast and complex world of education, politics, society and economics, as well as the world of culture, of the sciences and arts, of international life, of the mass media."

When a person follows the Ten Commandments, like where he does not worship strange gods, he does not steal, he does not covet his neighbor's wife, etc., we say he is good. In Tagalog, "siya'y mabait." Or simply, "may bait-Pinoy siya." But is bait-Pinoy enough? Is it enough merely to follow the negative commandments? The "thou shall not?"

No, our Lord Himself answered this question in Matthew 25. We must look after our neighbors, and tend to the poor and afflicted. In short, we are called to live with His life and to love with His love. Now, this is not easy to do. He loved us, sinners and tormentors that we are, so much that He laid down His life for us. We are called to do no less. Hindi lang po bait-Pinoy ang kailangan kundi "buting-Pinoy." It is not

enough to refrain from doing evil. We must do what is good for our neighbors.

In more direct Pilipino, according to Msgr. Bayani Valenzuela, chaplain of the University of the Philippines, "Hindi po tayo tatanggapin ni San Pedro sa langit kung tayo'y may bait-Pinoy lang. Hahanapin din po niya ang ating buting-Pinoy."

Called to Social

Transformation The fourth and final area of lay empowerment is the call to social transformation, the call for liberation and redemption from all the sinfulness of the temporal life. The Church must be wherever there is injustice, wherever there is apprehension, wherever there is sinfulness. As Christ said, "I did not come for the self-righteous. I have come for sinners."

Prior to Vatican II, life was relatively simple. Ang sabi po ay ito: noon, ang gawa daw po ng pari ay magbinyag, magkasal at magburol ng patay. At ang gawa naman ng laiko ay magdasal at magbayad. In other words, Church life referred to strictly "spiritual" matters only. It was dichotomized from daily community life. Under PCP II, however, Christian presence permeates every beat of life. We are Christians during worship in the Church and in the home as much as we are Christians in the office, in our professions, in the government and even in our leisure. This is why during the last elections, the Church actively participated through the Parish Pastoral Council For Responsible Voting or PPCRV in safeguarding and sanctifying the election process. As PCP II says, "Laymen and women in responsible positions in our society must help form the civic conscience of the voting population and work to explicitly promote the election to public office of leaders of true integrity."

88% of Catholics are Unchurched, Uninvolved, Untouched Let me conclude this speech with some figures. Statistics show that a majority of parish priests minister to about 40,000 to 50,000 parishioners on the average. Studies by the Catholic Bishops' Conference of the Philippines further show that only two percent of all Catholics are active in church-related affairs, and only 10 percent are considered regular churchgoers or "stockholders" of the Church. Of those who partake of the sacraments, most are women, the "highly audible-visible laity." The majority of men may be termed as the "reluctant laity." Approximately 8 percent are the "invisible laity" - unchurched, uninvolved and untouched by the parish.

These 88 percent who are unchurched, uninvolved and untouched by the parish pose the great challenge to all of us today. It is obvious that the vast majority of our people need to be reevangelized and to be given the opportunity to be touched by God's saving and liberating Word. We in this hall, educators and teachers - the successors in the profession of Christ - are the empowered laity. We are immersed in day-to-day conversion of heart and mind, in renewed catechesis, worship and social apostolate. Our Lord is calling on us to be evangelizers who form, inform and transform. Our Lord Jesus is calling on all of us in this hall to go forth and announce His gospel. By our baptism, Christ has empowered us, the laity, to reach out to these 88 percent of our Catholic population who are unchurched, uninvolved and untouched by the parish. Are we going to answer God's challenge?

I thank you.

## Summary

Chapter 11 of 'Love God, Serve Man' records CJP's November 27, 1992 speech at Siena College, Quezon City, before the National Convention of Dominican and Siena Schools, on 'Lay Empowerment in Building a Community of Believers' as decreed by PCP II and Vatican II. He opened by confessing that he rarely addressed educators, nuns and priests, since a survey ranked them highest in credibility while lawyers stood second to last -- a point he illustrated with the joke of the lost balloonists told their exact position 'accurate but entirely useless,' and the epitaph 'Here lies lawyer John Quill; he lies still.' Defining the laity from Lumen Gentium as all the baptized outside Holy Orders and the religious state, he noted that in a Church now called the 'People of God' they form 99 percent of the membership. He then set out PCP II's two fundamental aspects of the lay vocation: full belonging, so that lay people are 'not second-class members' but co-workers equal in dignity and mission, the remaining hierarchy being 'a hierarchy of service and not of excellence'; and secular character, so that lawyers stay lawyers, teachers find holiness in teaching, and 'Christians cannot shun politics because it is dirty' -- a point he illustrated with his daughter Mabel's call to be an Opus Dei numerary, which he first misunderstood. Empowerment, he explained, comes directly from Christ through baptism and confirmation, giving the laity a share in His 'priestly, prophetic and kingly office'; but possessing it is not using it, and its effective exercise requires four things together -- genuine conversion of mind and heart, renewed catechesis (Christo-centric, biblical, authentically Filipino, systematic), renewed worship, and renewed social apostolate -- since each is deficient without the others. He listed the four areas of the lay calling, insisted that 'bait-Pinoy' must become 'buting-Pinoy,' and closed with CBCP figures showing 88 percent of Catholics 'unchurched, uninvolved and untouched by the parish,' challenging the teachers before him, 'the successors in the profession of Christ,' to answer God's challenge.
