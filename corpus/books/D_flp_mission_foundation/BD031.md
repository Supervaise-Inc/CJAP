# Leadership by Example: The Davide Standard -- Ch. 1: Conscience of Society

Today we commemorate the retirement of Mr. Justice Antonio M. Martinez. To borrow the words of Chief Justice Andres R. Narvasa, he will now return to the land of the living after being judicially entombed for one year and three months in the highest court of the land. In many senses Mr. Justice Martinez is lucky, for he can once more enjoy a normal life, delight in the company of friends, meet his favorite people, savor his grandchildren, smell the flowers any day, and regain his freedom from silence which every member of this Court is pledged to observe. In short, starting tomorrow he will be liberated from the exile, the quietude and the 4 backbreaking work demanded by life on Mt. Olympus. i No university on earth teaches a course on how to be a Supreme Court justice. As author Anthony Lewis wrote in his book Gideon’s Trumpet (1966, p. 217), “a justice brings to the task only his character, his intellect, his education, his experience, his human understanding, his imagination.” Thereafter, he relies on his creativity, innovation, reflection and prayers. Career jurists who ascended to the Supreme Court through well-earned promotions in the judicial hierarchy are in a sense lucky, because their adjustments to the lonely i perches on this tribunal were not sudden or unexpected, unlike those of us who were picked straight i] from private practice or from the academe or from the nonjudicial branches of government.

But even career magistrates tell me that something does happen when they don the robes of this Court. All at once, new appointees to the Court feel a new beginning. Immediately they realize the god-like finality of their words and actions, for their decisions, whether right or wrong, are not reviewed, modified or Hil reversed by any other agency of government. Instead, they are immortalized in books to be perused, criticized, damned or acclaimed many years after they have passed from this world.

Members of Congress are called upon by their constituents to account for their actions; while cabinet members must, day by day, merit the trust and coni fidence of the President, who himself is accountable directly to the people. Hence, to the elected, the current popular sentiment is the powerful catalyst and barometer of their success or failure. On the other hand, magistrates must live in a world of reason where logic, evidence and conscience are their only yardsticks.

The permanency of tenure and the independence accorded to justices by our Constitution partly explain why they are able to withstand temporary political passions and rages. Indeed, the freedom to decide according to their consciences and intellects, without fear of political vengeance, enables them to write and do things which others may think to be correct, but have no courage to write or act on by themselves because of fear of electoral rejection or presidential rebuff. It is also these rare luxuries of tenure and independence that excise jurists from parochial ties and prejudices and help them to decide objectively and consistently according to principles and credos.

Indeed, the search for principles and doctrines is the very essence and bedrock of the work of a jurist. A litigation is won not because one side gets more votes from the people, but because it is upheld by the law through a process of reasoning. Neither are decisions the products of political bargainings like “I vote with you in this case on condition that you vote with me in this other case.” In the Supreme Court, cases are won or lost not because of popular palatability or compromise or friendship, not to mention pecuniary considerations, but because of reasoned arguments proceeding from legal principles and precedents.

This is not to say that the Court is averse to public opinion. In fact, I daresay that the Court is effective only as long as it retains the faith and trust of the public. Without the support of the populace, the Court becomes useless, as it cannot enforce by itself its own pronouncements. It does not command the army or the police or, for that matter, the “raging mob”; neither does it have the money to grease political patronage.

But there is a difference between the shifting winds of public emotion and the long-term public trust in the institution. While critics and pressure groups may rant from time to time against some decisions or opinions, the Court must enjoy enough residual respect and esteem, with the constant hope that one day it will prove to be right after all. And even if its level-headed critics may disagree, they should respect it enough to concede its good faith and likewise the wisdom, the probity and the diligence with which its i members have done their work. They must realize that, in the very nature of our system of government, the Court’s function is not to mirror popular ideas but in fact to teach and to open vistas to enlightened opinion.

The Court, as Justice Frankfurter once said, is “the conscience of society.” In this light, the test of the magistrates’ mettle is their capacity to stand by their convictions when the mob rages against them, threatens their families, and chants slogans of hate. For that is the nature of our conscience — it prevents us from doing the wrong thing just because it is easy, convenient and pleasurable; but it impels us to do what is right, no matter how painful and difficult.

Such is the role of the Supreme Court — to uphold Mi the basic norms of society and to defend the fundamental rights of the people as enshrined in the Constitution and the laws, notwithstanding tyrants, lynch mobs and even the best intentioned but ill-informed crusaders. The Court does not lust for the brute power of the executive. Neither does it desire to have the patronage of the legislature. It prays only for courage, integrity and sagacity, for these are its only tools with which to fulfill its work and to deserve the people’s trust.

Regrettably, Mr. Justice Martinez has had a stint in the Court that was all too brief. We his colleagues shall miss him and his cadenced responses to the challenges that had faced us. We shall pine for his scholarly ponencias, his industry and his passion for truth and fairness. His stint may have been too brief, but he has certainly enriched jurisprudence in our land. He has definitely left his unmistakable imprint in my memory of great jurists.

I thank you.

## Summary

Chapter 1 of 'Leadership by Example: The Davide Standard' reproduces Panganiban's tribute at the retirement of Mr. Justice Antonio M. Martinez, opening with Chief Justice Andres R. Narvasa's image of a justice returning to the land of the living after being judicially entombed, and listing the ordinary liberties the retiring magistrate regains, including release from the silence every member of the Court is pledged to observe. From that occasion he turns to the nature of the office: no university on earth teaches a course on how to be a Supreme Court justice, and, quoting Anthony Lewis's 'Gideon's Trumpet,' a justice brings only his character, intellect, education, experience, human understanding and imagination, relying thereafter on creativity, innovation, reflection and prayers. He notes that career jurists adjust more easily than those picked straight from private practice, the academe or the nonjudicial branches, yet all feel at once the god-like finality of decisions no other agency may review. Where elected officials answer to current popular sentiment, magistrates must live in a world of reason where logic, evidence and conscience are their only yardsticks, a discipline made possible by the permanency of tenure and the independence the Constitution grants. The search for principles and doctrines, he argues, is the essence and bedrock of a jurist's work, and cases are won by reasoned argument rather than by popularity, compromise, friendship or vote-trading. Conceding that the Court is effective only while it keeps the public's faith, since it commands neither army, police nor money, he distinguishes the shifting winds of public emotion from long-term institutional trust and insists that the Court's function is not to mirror popular ideas but to teach and open vistas to enlightened opinion. Invoking Frankfurter's phrase that the Court is 'the conscience of society,' he defines the test of a magistrate's mettle as standing by convictions when the mob rages, and closes by mourning Martinez's too-brief stint and praising his scholarly ponencias and passion for truth and fairness.
