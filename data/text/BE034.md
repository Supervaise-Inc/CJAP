Ethics is based on the premise that the ability to do something does not equate with entitlement to do so. Ability is not equivalent to permissibility. “Between ‘I can do it’ and ‘I may do it’ is that divide known as morality or ethics.”

Thus, the ability of a man to fire a gun does not entitle him to kill another human; the ability of a woman to give birth does not give her the right to murder her offspring or, more controversially in some countries, the right to abort the fetus in her womb.

## Ethical Questions in the Biosciences

By the same token, I ask these questions in reference to our subject today: Will our ability to genetically modify organisms and plants entitle us to increase food supply to feed more adequately the world’s burgeoning population? Will the development of technology for genetically re-engineering human cells automatically empower scientists to improve medical assistance to mankind?

Even more pointedly, will the capacity to intrude upon and manipulate the human embryo allow the unrestricted use of the new sciences in the guise of scientifically alleviating or curing certain maladies like diabetes, cancer, and Alzheimer’s or Parkinson’s disease? Should the functions of science be limited at all? And the ultimate question: would it be ethical to clone humans?

## Sources of Ethics

As the developments in the biotechnological revolution accelerate to the speed of light, the foregoing questions become more complex; and the need for satisfactory answers, more urgent. In this effort, religion, philosophy and law take center stage. The response from country to country, people to people, and person to person — depending on their intellectual orientation spiritual direction, economic situation, social standing cultural milieu, and sometimes even medical condition Basically, however, the answers come from three separate sources: (1) religious belief, (2) human nature and (3) human covenant.

## Influence of Religion on Ethical Standards

Since time immemorial, religion has always exerted an enduring influence on the regulation of human conduct. From ancient voodooism to the pluralistic deism of the Greeks, Romans, Incas, Aztecs and Chinese, people have always invoked superhuman powers to buttress their (relationships. More pervadingly, the three great Mosaic religions — Jewish, Christian and Muslim — have all looked up to the Ten Commandments as guides to spiritual and ethical comportment. “Thou shall not kill,” “Thou shall not steal,” and “Thou shall not covet thy neighbor's wife” govern the relationship not just between the creator and the created, but amongst the created themselves. “Love God with all your spirit, with all your heart and with all your strength” and “Love one another ‘as much as I love you” sum up the Christian tenets on moral and ethical behavior.

Thus, from the simple negative “Thou shall not lull” to the more complex positive “Love one another” evolved the pro-life and anti-abortion Catholic theology of protecting life at its earliest inception, when the sperm unites with the ovum. In that moment in time, life is treated — to be loved and cared for, never to be killed (r maimed in any manner. Pope John Paul II states it quite clearly: “The mere probability that the human person is involved would suffice to justify an absolutely clear prohibition of any intervention aimed at killing a human embryo.”

The avid pro-life advocates are even more fervent. Citing Psalm 139 — Yahweh “knew [me] even before was born in [my] mother’s womb” — they reject all forms of artificial birth control. Thus, they shun the diaphragm, the condom, the pill and the like, on the ground that they prevent conception that the Lord has willed.

## Philosophy as a Source of Ethics

Philosophy as a source of ethics necessarily starts with an understanding of human ends or goals.

James Watson, the Nobel laureate who is credited with the discovery of the DNA structure, theorizes that to satisfy these ends or goals, one must simply respond to human needs and wants like food, education and health.. However, any in-depth discussion of needs, wants, interests and desires eventually leads to Jeremy Bentham’s utilitarianism, which in turn boils down to the pursuit of pleasure and the escape from pain.

The insistence of many on rationality indeed leads to human dignity rather than the mere satisfaction of physical wants and emotional needs as the defining human attribute. Accordingly, the healthy liver of a car accident victim in a state of coma cannot just be surgically excised and implanted to replace the terminally diseased organ of a revered political leader or a great scientist, whose new lease on life may be worth much more to the nation and to humanity than the unlamented life of the young reckless driver. This apparently lesser mortal has basic human rights flowing from his nature and dignity as a person — rights that no one can take away from him without his consent, even in the guise of promoting the far greater good. This is the view of scholastic philosophers like St. Thomas Aquinas and St. Ignatius of Loyola.

The notion of fundamental individual human rights as the ultimate bases of ethical principles catapults liberal democracy into being the most accepted political system of our time. The length and breadth of constitutional law is based on the premise that the awesome might of the entire state apparatus, even when propelled by the overwhelming vote of the popular majority, cannot prevail over the fundamental human rights of a single individual. Ethics protects what ought to be done or undone for this individual, regardless of the utilitarian reality espoused by society.

But even the most dedicated exponents of human rights cannot escape the criticism that these rights abound and oftentimes conflict with one another. The right to life alone is livid with controversy — is it the right of the unborn or the unconceived, or is it that of the putative mother over her body? When the issue of genetic technology is thrown in, the individual’s claimed procreative liberty collides frontally with the scientist's vaunted right to re-engineer people genetically.

## Human Covenant as the Authoritative Source of Ethics

Because of the proliferation of claims to human rights entitlement, and the monumental confusion in the intellectual marketplace, there is a third proposition that the truly authoritative source of ethics is human covenant.

In the international field, the most famous of these covenants is the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. In my country, as in many others, this UN Document has gained a constitutional mandate and is thus enforceable by our courts as a “generally accepted principle of international law.” Other examples of enforceable human agreements would be the Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights; the Covenant on Civil and Political Rights; and the new Covenant on the Rights of the Child.

On a purely national level, various codes of ethics for the different professions are judicially enforced. In my country, there is even an Ethical Standards Law governing the conduct of all government officials and employees.

## Conclusion

The ultra rapid bioscientific and biotechnological developments teeter on the production of food, medicine and other basic necessities. Oftentimes, they involve human life itself — its creation, prolongation and enjoyment. The debate on the extent and the consequences of these aspects has led to the polarization of two basic groups.

The first is the freewheeling, liberal and libertarian camp that refuses to place limitations on these developments. It aligns the sciences alongside free markets and other economic icons of deregulation and privatization, arguing that the search for the greater good will democratically filter into a healthy exchange of information and research. Supposedly, the superior ideas of science will eventually prevail.

The second camp calls for restraint and restrictions based on religious, philosophical, moral, environmental, social and cultural concerns that sometimes border on Eugenics.

What is the world to do in the face of this polarization Dr. Francis Fukuyama, in his best-selling and thought-provoking book Our Post Human Future, proposes regulation — “particularly international regulation.” He adds, though, that there are less daunting approaches “ranging from self-regulation by industry or scientific community with minimal government oversight to formal regulation by a government agency.”

On the other hand, EINSHAC proposes the creation of an International Science and Technology Reference Forum or I/STREF as a solution. This project will be explained in detail later on by Dr. Franklin M. Zweig. But in summary, it calls for the formation by EINSHAC of a private, voluntary, non-binding group of respected and science-knowledgeable jurists who would sit together with law-oriented scientists. National tribunals - or the parties themselves in potential biotechnology litigation — may refer their consultas or problems to I/STREF. While legally non-binding, an opinion would be rendered by the Forum after study and deliberation. The opinion or “judgment” would be so persuasive that it would be accepted by all concerned.

But whether or not we abide by Fukuyama’s proposal or with EINSHAC’s ultimate solution — I/STREF — we must all agree that the ethical aspects of the new sciences and technologies are worth discussing in every forum of the dawning bio-age.
