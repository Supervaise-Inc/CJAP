# Reforming the Judiciary -- Ch. 16: Estrada v. Sandiganbayan: The Anti-Plunder Law Is Constitutional

On March 2, 2001, the Supreme Court - in Estrada v. Desierto and Estrada v. Arroyo - unanimously upheld the legitimacy of Gloria Macapagal Arroyo’s ascension to the Presidency on January 20, 2001. Moreover, the Court ruled that former President Joseph Ejercito Estrada did not enjoy immunity from suit. It reasoned that “unlawful acts of public officials are not acts of the State and the officer who acts illegally is not acting as such but stands on the same footing as any trespasser.

Thereafter, the Office of the Ombudsman charged the former president with plunder, a non-bailable crime under RA 7080. In a bid to shortcut his detention and obtain immediate acquittal, President Estrada challenged the constitutionality of this law in Estrada v. Sandiganbayan.

In the December 2001 issue of Kilosbayan magazine, former Senate President Jovito R. Salonga wrote an easy-to-understand summary of the Court's Decision and the Separate Opinions of the Justices. With his permission, I am reprinting his well-written summary as Chapter 16 of this book.

## Understanding the Decision of the Supreme Court vs. ERAP on the Anti- Plunder Law

by

Jovito R. Salonga

Last November 21, 2001, despite — or because of - the demonstrations near the Supreme Court premises and the overflow of text messages saying that the Supreme Court would strike down the Anti-Plunder Law as unconstitutional, the High Court decided to announce its decision to uphold the law by a 10-4-1 vote and thereby discourage further speculations on the subject. The decision was supposed to be promulgated one day later (Tuesday, November 22) but the Supreme to be Court must have felt that something decisive had to be done to stop the so-called "fearless forecasts" by and some tabloid journalists who smear justices on the say-so of rumormongers - a deplorable practice that does not enrich our impoverished democracy "but debases it” (Today, November 23, 2001).

The lawyers of former President Erap Estrada promptly announced they would file a motion for reconsideration even though they had not yet read the majority opinion. But Senator Edgardo Angara, a proErap solon whose knowledge of the workings of the Supreme Court cannot be disputed, was quoted as saying such a move would be futile.

Whether futile or not, it might be instructive and helpful for the ordinary Filipino to understand why Erap, a former senator, lost the case and why the ten justices of the Supreme Court voted the way they did - and, of course, why the four justices dissented. In my view, the possibility of reversal or a substantial modification of the decision of the High Court is quite remote.

## The Background of Its Enactment

Former President Estrada was a member of the Philippine Senate and a 6-month old member of the Liberal Party when I filed the Anti-Plunder Act (Senate Bill No. 733) in August 1988. I drafted the bill, chiefly on the basis of our experience in the Presidential Commission on Good Government, of which I was the first chair (February 1986 to March 1987) and pursuant to its second task under the law of its creation (Executive Order No. 1): namely, "the adoption of safeguards to insure the amassing of ill-gotten wealth shall not be repeated and the institution of adequate measures" to prevent its occurrence. It was also in compliance with the constitutional mandate that "the State shall maintain honesty and integrity in the public service and take positive and effective measures against graft and corruption." (Art. II, Sec. 27 of the Constitution).

What impelled me to file the measure was the fact that plunder or massive looting, sometimes known as kleptocracy, was not punished in our statute books. The crimes of malversation of public funds, falsification, theft, extortion, bribery and similar offenses under the Revised Penal Code were clearly inadequate to cope with the magnitude of corruption and thievery which the PCGG had uncovered under the Marcos years. The government found it necessary to file around 80 separate complaints against the Marcoses and their coconspirators, for various offenses. For that reason, the overall conspiracy had to be cut up into simple criminal charges as required by law and proved one by one, thereby causing unnecessary delay. Under the proposed bill, an overall charge of plunder, covering a series or combination of criminal offenses, would be legally sufficient. More importantly, in the hands of prosecutors and judges of competence and integrity, it would advance the cause of speedy justice to the whole nation and to the affected parties - the aggrieved as well as the accused.

When asked by the media the other day why he voted for the Anti-Plunder Law, Erap said it was a "party bill." The truth must be said: it was unanimously supported by the first post-EDSA senators without regard to party affiliation. In fact, I requested the following senators in August 1988 to sign the bill as coauthors: Rene A. V. Saguisag, Alberto G. Romulo, Teofisto Guingona, Jr., Orlando Mercado and Wigberto Tañada. Of the five, three were Liberals, one (Romulo) was a Lakas and the last (Tañada) was an Independent. Senator Erap Estrada could have opposed it if he had wanted to, but he did not. In fact, he voted in favor of the bill not once but several times. It was his good friend, Senator Tañada, the Chairman of the Committee on Constitutional Amendments, Revision of Codes and Laws, who sponsored the bill on June ,5 1989, while I presided over the proceedings. The record shows that Tañada was interpellated by Senators Ernesto Maceda, Neptali Gonzales, Teofisto Guingona, Vicente Paterno, and Alberto G. Romulo on June 5, 1989. Amendments were introduced on June 6, and the bill was approved on second reading on the same date by the senators who were present (including Erap). It was approved on third reading on July 25, 1989 by all the nineteen (19) senators who were present, including Senator Estrada. When the House of Representatives passed a slightly different version of the bill, based on the original measure which had been filed by Rep. Lorna V. Yap, at my request, and sponsored by Cong. Pablo Garcia, the chair of the Committee on Justice, the House officials requested a conference on the disagreeing provisions of the two bills. We in the Senate agreed and appointed the following senators as conferees: Tañada, Rene Saguisag, Neptali Gonzales, Jose Lina and Juan Ponce Enrile. The Conference Committee recommended that the Senate bill, in consolidation with the House bill, be approved in accordance with the reconciled version, a copy of which was furnished to all senators. The Conference Committee Report was approved on May 27, 1991 by all the members of the Senate who were present, including Senator Estrada. Presented to President Cory Aquino on June 13, 1991, it was approved on June 13, 1991 and is now known as Republic Act No. 7080, the Anti-Plunder Law.

Seven years after he and the other senators cast their final vote for the Anti-Plunder Law, Estrada was elected president. Two and a half-years later, he was ousted from Malacañang Palace by People Power II. Former VP Gloria Macapagal Arroyo was sworn into office as president by Chief Justice Davide, Jr., on January 20, 2001. Former President Estrada, who was charged with having committed four counts of plunder by the Ombudsman, was scheduled to be arraigned (that is, plead guilty or not guilty) on July 10, 2001. When his Motion to Quash the plunder charge - allegedly because (1) it was "based on an unconstitutional law" and (2) the information charges more than one offense - was denied on July 9 by the 3rd Division of the Sandiganbayan, Estrada, through his lawyers, immediately filed a 40-page certiorari and prohibition petition with the Supreme Court under Rule 65 of the Rules of Court, alleging that the Anti-Plunder Law was unconstitutional. He and his co-accused asked for a temporary restraining order (TRO) to block or postpone the arraignment. Because there was no TRO from the High Court, the Sandiganbayan proceeded with the arraignment in the afternoon of July 10, 2001. Due to his refusal to plead guilty or not guilty, the 3rd Division of Sandiganbayan, headed by Justice Anacleto Badoy, entered a plea of not guilty for Estrada, in line with Philippine rules on criminal procedure.

The key provisions of the Anti-Plunder Law claimed by Estrada's lawyers to be defective for being vague and overbroad are:

Definition of the Crime of Plunder; Penalties.- Any public officer who, by himself or in connivance with members of his family, relatives by affinity or consanguinity, business associates, subordinates or other persons, amasses, accumulates or acquires ill-gotten wealth through a combination or series of overt or criminal acts as described in Section 1 (d) hereof in the aggregate amount or total value of at least fifty million pesos (P50,000,000.00) shall be guilty of the crime of plunder and shall be punished by reclusion perpetua to death. Any person who participated with the said public officer in the commission of an offense contributing to the crime of plunder shall likewise be punished for such offense. In the imposition of penalties, the degree of participation and the attendance of mitigating and extenuating circumstances, as provided by the Revised Penal Code, shall be considered by the court. The court shall declare any and all ill-gotten wealth and their interests and other incomes and assets including the properties and shares of stocks derived from the deposit or investment thereof forfeited in favor of the State. (As amended by Sec. 12, R No. 7659).

The term "ill-gotten" wealth is defined in §1 (d) as follows:

"Ill-gotten wealth" means any asset, property, business enterprise or material possession of any person within the purview of Section Two (2) hereof, acquired by him directly or indirectly through dummies, nominees, agents, subordinates and/or business associates by any combination or series of the following means or similar schemes:

1) Through misappropriation, conversion, misuse or malversation of public funds or raids on the public treasury.

2) By receiving, directly or indirectly, any commission, gift, share, percentage, kickbacks or any other form of pecuniary benefit from any person and/or entity in connection with any government contract or project or by reason of the office or position of the public officer concerned;

3) By the illegal or fraudulent conveyance or disposition of assets belonging to the National Government or any of its subdivisions, agencies or instrumentalities or government-owned or controlled corporations and their subsidiaries;

4) By obtaining, receiving or accepting directly or indirectly any shares of stock, equity or any other form of interest or participation including the promise of future employment in any business enterprise or undertaking;

5) By establishing agricultural, industrial or commercial monopolies or other combinations and / or implementation of decrees and orders intended to benefit particular persons or special interests; or

6) By taking undue advantage of official position, authority, relationship, connection or influence to unjustly enrich himself or themselves at the expense and to the damage and prejudice of the Filipino people and the Republic of the Philippines.

Section 4 of the said law states:

Rule of Evidence. - For purposes of establishing the crime of plunder, it shall not be necessary to prove each and every criminal act done by the accused in furtherance of the scheme or conspiracy to amass, accumulate or acquire ill-gotten wealth, it being sufficient to establish beyond reasonable doubt a pattern of overt or criminal acts indicative of the overall unlawful scheme or conspiracy.

## The Plunder Charge Against Erap Estrada

The Amended Information against Estrada alleges that in violation of the Anti-Plunder Law, petitioner - during his incumbency as president from June 1998 to January 2001 - willfully and criminally amassed wealth in the total amount of more than four (4) billion pesos, more or less, through "a combination or series of overt or criminal acts," to wit: (1) by receiving or collecting the total amount of P545 million, more or less, from illegal gambling by himself and / or in connivance with his co-accused, in exchange for protection from illegal gambling; (2) by misappropriating, converting, or misusing by himself or in connivance with his coaccused, public funds amounting to P130 million, more or less, representing a portion of the share of the Province of Ilocos Sur in the tobacco excise tax; (3) by ordering the GSIS and the SSS to buy shares of stock of the Belle Corp., worth P1.102 billion and P744.6 million respectively, or the total amount of P1.84 billion, for which he received as commission the amount of P189.7 million, more or less from Belle Corp.; and (4) by unjustly enriching himself from commissions, gifts, shares, percentages, and kickbacks in the amount of P3.23 billion which he deposited in the Equitable-PCI Bank under the name of "Jose Velarde."

## The Arguments and the Issues

During the oral arguments before the Supreme Court on September 18, 2001, the issues were defined by the High Court, as follows: whether (1) the Anti-Plunder Law (RA 7080) is unconstitutional for being vague; (2) the law requires less evidence for proving the predicate crimes of plunder and therefore violates the rights of the accused to due process; and (3) whether plunder, as defined in RA 7080, is a malum prohibitum (that is, an act that is wrong because prohibited by law, as distinguished from a malum in se, an act that is essentially evil, whether prohibited or not) and fi so, whether it is within the power of Congress to so classify it. Arguing for the petitioner Estrada was Atty. Pacifico Agabin, former U.P. law dean. Arguing for the respondents were Solicitor-General Simeon Marcelo and Ombudsman Aniano Desierto. They were given ten (10) days, ending September 28, to submit their respective memoranda, which they did.

## The Decision

The Decision was penned by Justice Josue N. Bellosillo, who had been unjustly and ruthlessly smeared by zealous texters. The other nine (9) justices - Chief Justice Davide, Jose Melo, Reynato Puno, Vicente Mendoza, Jose Vitug, Artemio Panganiban, Leonardo Quisumbing, Arturo Buena, and Sabino de Leon, concurred. Two of them issued separate

concurring opinions - namely, Justices Vicente V. Mendoza and Artemio Panganiban. Four justices - Santiago M. Kapunan, Bernardo P. Pardo, Consuelo Ynares-Santiago, and Angelina Sandoval-Gutierrez - dissented. The last three were all appointees of the former president. But each one of the four dissenters issued a separate opinion. Justice Antonio T. Carpio abstained, on the ground that he was one of the complainants against Estrada before the Ombudsman.

This article will explain in plain English the Bellosillo Decision and the two concurring opinions of Justices Mendoza and Panganiban. The dissenting opinions, led by Justice Kapunan - who would have penned the Decision of the Court had the side of unconstitutionality won in the voting - will be referred to from time to time so we can better appreciate the clash of competing ideas.

## Justice Bellosillo's Decision

1. The principle is that a legislative measure is presumed to be in harmony with the Constitution, based on the idea that one branch of the government should not encroach upon the duties and powers of another. Courts, therefore, proceed with restraint and act with caution in determining whether a statute is in accordance with the Constitution. They must first ascertain whether an interpretation is fairly possible to sidestep the question of constitutionality. The onerous task of rebutting the presumption of constitutionality weighs heavily on the party challenging the validity of the statue. To doubt is to sustain, as tersely put by Justice Malcolm.

2. Contrary to the dissenting opinion of Justice Kapunan, the Anti-Plunder Law contains ascertainable standards and well-defined parameters which would enable the accused to determine the nature of his violation. Section 2 of the law is sufficiently explicit: (1) The offender is a public officer who acts by himself or in connivance with members of his family, relatives or associates; (2) he amassed or acquired ill-gotten wealth through a combination or series of acts as described above; (3) the total value of said ill-gotten wealth is at least P50 million. In fact, the Amended Information closely follows the language of the law.

3. Estrada's contention that the words "combination" and "series" found in Section 2 of the law defining plunder is vague and hence violative of due process is untenable. Words of a statute are to be interpreted in their natural, plain and ordinary acceptance unless it is evident that the legislature intended a technical or special legal meaning to those words. That Congress intended the words "combination" and "series" to be understood in their popular meanings is evident from the legislative deliberations on the bill (quoting the deliberations of the Bicameral Committee on Justice, May 7, 1991 and the deliberations in the Senate on June 6, 1989). Thus, when the law speaks of combination, it is referring to at least two acts falling under different categories, e.g., raids on the public treasury, on the one hand, and extortion, on the other. But to constitute a series, there must be two or more acts falling under the same category or enumeration, e.g., malversation of public funds committed by the same public officer on different dates.

4. The test in determining whether a criminal statute is void for uncertainty is whether the language conveys a sufficiently definite warning as to the proscribed conduct when measured by common understanding and practice.

5. Petitioner Estrada cannot feign ignorance of what the Anti-Plunder Law is all about. Being one of the Senators who voted for its passage, petitioner must be aware that the law was extensively deliberated upon by the Senate and its appropriate committees by reason of which he even registered his affirmative vote "with full knowledge of its legal implications and sound constitutional anchorage." This part of the Decision embodies, or comes pretty close to, the doctrine of estoppel, which would bar a legislator - who was present during the deliberations, witnessed the debate, and voted for the passage of the bill - from assailing the validity of the law on the ground that he did not know - or did not understand - its purpose, its effects and consequences.

6. Section 4 of the Anti-Plunder Law does not circumvent the requirement that the prosecution must prove beyond reasonable doubt the predicate acts constituting the crime of plunder. As may be found in the exchange of October 9, 1990 between Rep. Albano and Rep. Pablo Garcia, the chairman of the Committee on Justice in the House, and the explanation of the Senate President during the Committee deliberations in the Senate on November 15, 1998 (cited in the Sandiganbayan Resolution of July 9, 2001), what the prosecution needs to prove beyond reasonable doubt is only a sufficient number of acts to form a combination or series which would constitute a pattern and involving an amount of at least P50 million. Thus, if the accused in a criminal case for plunder is charged with having committed 50 raids on the public treasury, he need not prove all these 50 raids, it being sufficient to prove by pattern two or more of the raids beyond reasonable doubt, provided only that they amount to more than P50 million .

7. A reading of Section 2 in conjunction with Sec. 4 (Rule of Evidence) leads us to the logical conclusion that "pattern" inheres in the very acts of accumulating, amassing or acquiring ill-gotten wealth. Such a pattern arises where the prosecution is able to prove beyond reasonable doubt the predicate acts of plunder as defined in Section 2 of the law.

8. As to the validity of the Death Penalty Law (RA 7659) which amended the Anti-Plunder Law by classifying plunder as a heinous offense warranting the death penalty in proper cases, it is now too late in the day to resurrect this long dead issue. The case of People v. Echegaray (prom. Feb. 7, 1997) has already settled this matter.

9. The Senior Justice explained the purposes and the significance of the Anti-Plunder Law in these moving words:

"Our nation has been racked by scandals of corruption and obscene profligacy of officials in high places which have shaken its very foundation. The anatomy of graft and corruption has become more elaborate in the corridors of time as unscrupulous people relentlessly contrive more and more ingenious ways to bilk the coffers of government. Drastic and radical measures are imperative to fight the increasingly sophisticated, extraordinarily methodical and catastrophic looting of the national treasury.

"Such is the Plunder Law, especially designed to disentangle those ghastly issues of grand scale corruption which, if left unchecked, will spread like a malignant tumor and ultimately consume the moral and institutional fiber of our nation.

"The Plunder Law, indeed, is a living testament to the will of the legislature to ultimately eradicate the scourge and thus secure society against the avarice and other venalities in public office."

The SC's decision concluded: "These are times that try men's souls. In the checkered history of this nation, few issues of national importance can equal the amount of interest and passion generated by the petitioner's ignominious fall from the highest office, and his eventual prosecution and trial under a virginal statute.

"The continuing saga has driven a wedge of dissension among our people that may linger for a long time. Only by responding to the clarion call for patriotism, to rise above factionalism and prejudices, shall we emerge triumphant in the midst of ferment."

## Justice Mendoza's Concurring Opinion

Repeatedly, Justice Bellosillo cites the concurring opinion of Justice Vicente Mendoza, as did the other justices. This deferential attitude is understandable. Justice Mendoza, who had been teaching Constitutional Law in the U.P. before his elevation to the Supreme Court, studied the case in depth and wrote a scholarly opinion that reads like a lecture on Constitutional Law, Criminal Law, Remedial Law and Statutory Construction. Parts of this opinion will probably be cited in law review articles and quoted by lawyers, law professors, law students and textbook writers in discussing the various aspects of this first plunder case against a former president.

Let us now deal with the Mendoza Opinion item by item, so we can appreciate it better (Quotation marks are omitted and paraphrasing is used for clarity.)

1. On the wholesale attack of Estrada on the validity of the entire statute, not just Section 2 in relation to Section 1 (d) (1) and (2) of the Anti-Plunder Law. - The(se) other provisions of the statute are irrelevant to this case. What relevance do questions regarding the establishment of monopolies and combinations or the ownerships of stocks in a business enterprise, or fraudulent dispositions of government property, have to the criminal prosecution of petitioner (Estrada) when they are not even mentioned in the amended information filed against him? Why should it be important to inquire whether the phrase "overt act" in Section 1 (d) and Section 2 means the same thing as the phrase "criminal act" as used in the same provisions when the acts imputed to the petitioner in the Amended Information are criminal acts? Had the provisions of the Revised Penal Code been subjected to this kind of line-by-line scrutiny, it is doubtful if we would have the jurisprudence on penal law that we have today.

Two justifications are advanced for this "facial challenge" - or a challenge "on its face" - to the validity of the entire statute: (1) the statute comes within the specific prohibitions of the Constitution, hence the need for strict scrutiny and a narrow er scope for the presumption of constitutionality of a statute; and (2) the Anti-Plunder Law is vague and overbroad. There is no basis for such claims (citing U.S. and Philippine cases).

Strict scrutiny is used today to test the validity of laws dealing with the regulation of speech, gender or race and facial challenges are allowed for this purpose. But criminal statues, like the Anti-Plunder Law, while subject to strict construction, are not subject to strict scrutiny. The two (strict construction and strict scrutiny) are not the same. The rule of strict construction is a rule of legal hermeneutics (art or science of interpretation) which deals with the parsing - that is, analyzing a sentence or words of a sentence - of statutes to determine the intent of the legislature. On the other hand, strict scrutiny is a standard of judicial review for determining the quality and the amount of governmental interest brought to justify the regulation of fundamental freedoms.

Thus, under deferential review, laws are upheld if they rationally further a legitimate governmental interest, without serious inquiry into the substantiality of such interest and examining the alternative means by which the objectives could be achieved. Under intermediate review, the substantiality of the governmental interest is looked into and the availability of less restrictive alternatives is considered. Under strict scrutiny, the focus is on the presence of compelling, rather than substantial, governmental interest and on the absence of less restrictive means for achieving that interest (Stone, Content-Neutral Restrictions, 54 Univ. of Chi. Law Rev. 46, 50-53 (1987). Considering these degrees of strictness in the review of statutes, how many criminal laws can survive the test of strict scrutiny? How many can pass muster, if, as petitioner would have it, such statutes are not to be presumed constitutional? Above all, what would happen to the problem of graft and corruption in government if criminal laws are upheld only if they survive the test of strict scrutiny?

A facial challenge is allowed to be made to a vague statue and to one which is overboard because of possible "chilling effect" upon protected speech. The rationale does not apply to penal statutes. In the area of criminal law, the law cannot take chances as in the area of free speech. The overbreadth and vagueness doctrines have special application only to free speech cases. They are inapt for testing the validity of penal statutes. In sum, the doctrines of strict scrutiny, overbreadth, and vagueness are analytical tools developed for testing "on their faces" statutes in free speech cases or, as they are called in American law, First Amendment cases. Consequently, there is no basis for petitioner's claim that this court review the AntiPlunder Law on its face and in its entirety, as contended by Estrada, Justices Kapunan, Ynares-Santiago and Sandoval-Gutierrez.

2. Anti-Plunder Law is neither vague nor overbroad. Anyone reading the law in relation to the charge cannot possibly be mistaken as to what the petitioner is accused of in the plunder case in the Sandiganbayan.

Regarding the words "combination," "series," and "pattern" (after citing the deliberations in the Bicameral Conference Committee and in the Senate) - Resort to the deliberations in Congress will readily reveal that the word "combination" includes at least two different overt or criminal acts listed in the Anti-Plunder Law, such as (1)  misappropriation and (2) taking advantage of official position. On the other hand, “series” is used when the offender commits the same overt or criminal act more than once. There is no plunder if only one act is proven, even if the ill-gotten wealth acquired thereby amounts to or exceeds P50 million.

A "pattern of overt or criminal acts" is required in Sec. 4 of the law to prove an unlawful scheme or conspiracy. In such a case, it is not necessary to prove each and every criminal act done in furtherance of the scheme or conspiracy so long as those proven show a pattern indicating the scheme or conspiracy. As Senate President Salonga explained, if there are 150 constitutive crimes charged, it is not necessary to prove beyond reasonable doubt all of them. If a pattern can be shown by proving, for example, 10 criminal acts, then that would be sufficient to secure conviction (Senate deliberations of Nov. 15, 1988). The State is thereby enabled by this device to deal with several acts constituting separate crimes as just one crime of plunder by allowing their prosecution by means of a single information because there is a common purpose for committing them - that of "amassing, accumulating or acquiring wealth through such overt or criminal acts." The pattern is the organizing principle that defines what would be discrete criminal acts into the single crime of plunder. As thus applied to petitioners, the Anti-Plunder Law presents only problems of statutory construction, not vagueness or overbreadth. Where the ambiguity is not latent and the legislative intention is discoverable with the aid of canons of construction, the void for vagueness doctrine has no application.

In the oft-cited case of Connaly v. General Constr. Co. (269 U.S. 385, 391- 1926), the test of vagueness was formulated by the U.S. Supreme Court as follows: "A statute which either forbids or requires the doing of an act in terms so vague that men of common intelligence must necessarily guess at its meaning and differ as to its application, violates the first essential of due process of law."

Holmes' test was that of the viewpoint of the bad man. In the Path of the Law, Holmes said: "If you want to know the law and nothing else, you must look at it as a bad man, who cares only for the material consequences which such knowledge enables him to predict, not as a good man who finds his reasons for conduct, whether inside or outside of it, in the vaguer sanctions of conscience."

Whether from the point of view of a man of common intelligence or from that of a bad man, there can be no mistaking the meaning of the Anti-Plunder Law as applied to petitioner Estrada.

3. Plunder, a malum in se requiring proof of mens rea, does not eliminate the element of criminal intent. - Plunder is a malum in se, requiring proof of criminal intent. Precisely because the constitutive crimes are mala in se, the element of mens rea ( a guilty mind or criminal intent) must be proven in a prosecution for plunder. It is noteworthy that the amended information alleges that the crime of plunder was committed "willfully, unlawfully, and criminally." Indeed, the application of mitigating and extenuating circumstances in the Revised Penal Code to prosecutions under the Anti-Plunder Law indicates quite clearly that mens rea is an element of plunder since the degree of responsibility of the offender is determined by his criminal intent. Finally, any doubt as to whether the crime of plunder is a malum in se must be deemed to have been resolved in the affirmative by the decision of Congress in 1993 to include it among the heinous crimes punishable by reclusion perpetua to death. The legislative declaration in RA 7659 that plunder is a heinous offense implies that it is a malum in se. For when the acts punished are inherently immoral or inherently wrong, they are mala in se and it does not matter that such acts are punished in a special law, especially since in the case of plunder, the predicate crimes are mala in se. Indeed, it would be absurd to treat prosecutions for plunder as though they are mere prosecutions for violations of the Bouncing Checks Law or of an ordinance against jaywalking, without regard to the inherent wrongness of the acts.

4. On the contention that the Anti-Plunder Law simply combined several existing crimes into a single one but the penalty is grossly disproportionate to the crimes combined.

Obviously, the legislature views plunder as a crime as serious as robbery with homicide or rape with homicide by punishing it with the same penalty - life imprisonment to death (citing the Explanatory Note, Senate Bill No. 733, which speaks of the gravity of the crime of plunder). Many other examples drawn from the Revised Penal Code and from special laws show that when special complex crimes are created out of existing crimes, the penalty for the new crime is heavier.

To recapitulate, the Anti-Plunder Law does not seek to regulate speech. It is a statute designed to combat graft and corruption, especially those committed by highly placed public officials. As conduct and not speech is its object, the Court cannot take chances by examining other provisions not before it without risking vital interests of society. Accordingly, such statute must be examined only "as applied" to the defendant and, if found valid as to him, the statute as a whole should not be declared unconstitutional for overbreadth or vagueness of its other provisions.

## Justice Panganiban's Separate (Concurring) Opinion

The separate opinion of Justice Panganiban is notable for its conciseness, unmistakable clarity, and incisive logic. The opinion is a point by point answer to the dissenters, led by Justice Kapunan, and discusses the issues one by one, as follows:

I. First issue - Void for Vagueness doctrine is not applicable

In two recent cases (Dans v. People, 285 SCRA 504; Sajul v. Sandiganbayan, GR 135294, Nov. 20, 2000), this Court has decreed that as long as a penal law can answer it is the basic query - "What is the violation" — constitutional. "Anything beyond this, the 'hows' and the 'whys' are evidentiary matters which the law cannot possibly disclose in view of the uniqueness of every case."

## Elements of Plunder

The Anti-Plunder Law more than adequately answers the question - what is the violation? Any law student using basic knowledge of criminal law will refer to the following elements of the crime: (1) The offender is a public officer acting by himself or in connivance with members of his family, relatives by affinity or consanguinity, business associates, subordinates or other persons; (2) The offender amasses, accumulates, or acquires ill-gotten wealth; (3) The aggregate amount or total value of the ill-gotten wealth is at least P50 million; (4) Such ill-gotten wealth has been acquired directly or indirectly through dummies, nominees, agents, subordinates and/or business associates by any combination or series of the means or similar schemes, as set forth in the law, such as (a) misappropriation, malversation of public funds or raids on the public treasury; (b) receiving directly or indirectly any commission, gift, share, percentage, kickbacks, or other form of pecuniary benefit by reason of the office or position of the public officer concerned; (c) by illegal or fraudulent conveyance of assets belonging to the government or its subdivisions or agencies; (d) by obtaining, receiving or accepting directly any shares of stock, equity, or any other form of interest or participation, etc. (e) by establishing agricultural, industrial or commercial monopolies, intended to benefit particular persons or special interests; (f) by taking advantage of official position, authority, relationship, connection, or influence to unjustly enrich himself or themselves at the expense and to the damage and prejudice of the Filipino people and the Republic of the Philippines.

## On the Absence of Definitions and the Role of Judicial Construction

Petitioner Estrada argues that there is still vagueness because of the absence of definitions of the term combination, series and pattern in the text of the law. But the absence of statutory definitions of words in a statute will not render the law "void for vagueness, the meanings of such words can be determined through the judicial function of construction.

Indeed, simple statutory construction, not a declaration of unconstitutionality, is the key to the allegedly vague words of the Anti-Plunder Law. And the most basic rule in statutory construction is to ascertain the meaning of a term from the legislative proceedings. Pervading the deliberations of the Bicameral Conference Committee on Justice held on May 7, 1991, was the common understanding of combination as a joining or combining of at least two dissimilar things or acts, and series as a repetition or recurrence of the same thing at least twice. As a matter of fact, the same understanding of those terms also prevailed during the Senate deliberations on Senate Bill No. 733 earlier held on June 6, 1989. The Records of those deliberations speak for themselves.

It is true that during the deliberations in the Senate, the late Senator Neptali Gonzales initially raised concerns over the alleged vagueness in the use of the terms combination and series during the session o f June 5, 1986. But the clarificatory remarks of Senate President Salonga and Senators Tanada and Romulo, which threw light on the matters in doubt, happened the following day, June 6. In brief, the misgivings voiced by Senator Gonzales (as quoted by Estrada's counsel and by Justice Kapunan in his dissent) were adequately addressed, answered and disposed of the following day. Thus, Senate Bill No. 733, defining and penalizing plunder, was passed and approved on third reading on July 25, 1989, with 19 affirmative votes (including [those of] Senators Gonzales, Tanada, Maceda and petitioner himself, without any negative vote or abstention. Indeed some of the sharpest legal minds in the country voted to approve the bill, even though it was bereft of statutory definitions. Likewise, it would be inconceivable for Senator Gonzales to have voted for the approval of the Bill had he believed that it was vague to the point of constitutional infirmity; or, at the very least, if he believed that his earlier reservations or misaprehensions were not fully satisfied.

## Specific Percentage to Constitute a Crime Not Always Necessary

The argument that, as the Anti-Plunder Law does not specify the minimum number of malefactors so an offense can be classified as plunder, is incongruous, if not totally misleading. As pointed out during the Oral Argument, the crime of plunder can be committed by a public officer acting alone, under Sec. 2 of the law. Hence the insistence on a mathematical specification is without basis. Even the RICO Law, to which Estrada's counsel (and Justice Kapunan) made repeated references, can likewise be violated by a single individual.

## Innocent Acts Not Penalized by An-Plunder Law

Petitioner Estrada insists that innocent acts are criminalized by RA 7080, because it allegedly penalizes combinations or series of acts enumerated under items y 4 and of Section 1(d) of the law, which reads: "(4) By obtaining or receiving or accepting directly or indirectly any shares of stock, equity or any other forms of interest or participation including the promise of future employment in any business enterprise or undertaking; (5) By establishing agricultural, industrial or commercial monopolies or other combinations and/or implementation of decrees and orders intended to benefit particular persons or special interests." The argument deserves scant attention, since items 4 and 5 were taken out of context and read in isolation instead of in relation to the other provisions of the same law, particularly Section 2. The above-enumerated acts, means or similar schemes, refer to the acquisition of ill-gotten wealth by a public officer, by himself or in connivance with others. Those acts are therefore not innocent. Neither are these prohibitions new or unfamiliar. Item 4 may be traced back to some of the prohibitions in the Anti-Graft Law (Section 3 (b) (c) (d) and (h) of RA 3019). On the other hand, the prohibited acts under Item 5 have antecedents in the Revised Penal Code's interdiction against monopolies and combinations in restraint of trade. Actually - and this is my own comment - the enumeration found in Section 2 of the law constitutes some of the favorite techniques of illegal accumulation used by Marcos, his associates and cronies from 1966 to 1985 (Salonga, Presidential Plunder, 2nd printing (2000) pp. 30-32).

## RA 7080 Does Not Suffer from Overbreadth

Petitioner Estrada, in alleging that RA 7080 suffers from "overbreadth," misconstrues the concept. In the very recent case of People v. Dela Piedra (January 24, 2001), we said: "A statute may be said to be overbroad where it operates to inhibit the exercise of individual freedoms affirmatively guaranteed by the Constitution, such as the freedom of speech or religion." Here, petitioner has not identified which of his constitutionally protected freedoms are allegedly being violated by the Anti-Plunder Law.

## Pattern of Overt or Criminal Acts Not an Essential Element of Plunder; RA 7080 Not Patterned After the RICO Law

Petitioner faults the statute for failing to provide a definition of the phrase "pattern of overt or criminal acts indicative of the overall unlawful scheme or conspiracy" used in Section 4of the law. This definition is crucial, he says, since pattern is an essential element of the crime of plunder. But a plain reading of the law debunks this contention. Such a pattern is not an essential or substantive element of the crime of plunder. It is possible to give full force and effect to RA 7080 without applying Section 4 - after all, the heading and text of Section 4 leave no room for doubt that it is not substantive in nature.

"Sec. 4. Rule of evidence.- For purposes of establishing the crime of plunder, it shall not be necessary to prove each and every criminal act done by the accused in furtherance of the scheme or conspiracy to amass, accumulate, or acquire ill-gotten wealth, it being sufficient to establish beyond reasonable doubt a pattern of the overall scheme or conspiracy."

As Chief Justice Davide pointed out during the Oral Argument, Section 2 in relation to Section 1 (d) deals with how the crime of plunder is committed, whereas Section 4 deals with how the crime is proved and is therefore not substantive but merely procedural. It may be disregarded or discarded if found defective or deficient, without impairing the rest of the statute.

Actually, the root of this problem may be traced to an observation of Rep. Pablo Garcia, chair of the House Committee on Justice, that RA 7080 had been patterned after the RICO Law. Petitioner's counsel - and Justice Kapunan's dissent - seized on this statement of Rep. Garcia (who, by the way, had no hand in the drafting of the bill but did a remarkable job of supporting it and steering its passage into law) and on the assertions of some American cases that a pattern of racketeering is a "key requirement" in the RICO Law and a "necessary element" of violations thereof. He then used these as the springboard for his vagueness attacks on RA 7080. Respondent Sandiganbayan correctly held that said legislation was essentially different from our AntiPlunder, as pointed out in its Resolution of July 9, 2001:

"However, the similarities extend only insofar as both laws penalize with severe penalties the commission by a single accused or multiple accused of a pattern of overt or criminal acts as one continuing crime. However, the legislative policies and objectives as well as the nature of the crimes penalized by the RICO and the Anti-Plunder Law are different."

In the case of RICO, legislative concern focused on the threat of continued racketeering activity, and that was why the pattern was imbued with such importance. "Congress was concerned in RICO with long-term criminal conduct," as stated by an American authority. However, in RA 7080, precisely because of the sheer magnitude of the crimes in question and their extremely deleterious effects on society, the legislative sentiment of great urgency - the immediate deterrence of such crimes - was incompatible with the RICO concept of "pattern" as con not ing either continuity over a subst antia l period of time or threat of continuity or repetition. The legislative intent and policy of RA 7080 centered on imposing a heavy penalty in order to achieve a strong, if not permanent, deterrent effect - the sooner the better (citing Record of the Senate on June 5, 1989). This conclusion is further bolstered by the fact that "pattern," in the RICO law context, is nowhere to be found in the language of RA 7080 or in the deliberations of Congress. The legislators aware of the were well RICO Act and could have adopted its concepts and definitions by adopting pattern as an essential element of the crime of plunder, if that were their intent, instead of relegating it to a mere procedural provision in Section 4.

The cases cited by Petitioner Estrada involving U.S. federal court decisions relative to the RICO Law did not at all arrive at a finding of unconstitutionality of the questioned statute. Reference to these U.S. cases is utterly misplaced, considering the substantial differences in the nature, policies and objectives between the RICO Law and the Anti-Plunder Law. The RICO Law does not create a new type of substantive crime since any acts which are punishable under the RICO Law are also punishable under existing federal and state statutes. Moreover, the purpose of the RICO law is "to seek the eradication of organized crime in the United States."

On the other hand, the Anti-Plunder Law creates an entirely new crime that may consist of both (a) criminal acts already punished by the Revised Code or special laws; and (b) acts that may be punishable by existing laws. Furthermore, unlike in the RICO Law, the motivation behind the enactment of the Anti-Plunder Law is "the need for a penal law that can adequately cope with the nature and magnitude of the corruption of the previous regime, in accordance with the constitutional duty of the State to take positive and effective measures against graft and corruption.

## Judiciary Empowered to Construe and Apply the Law

The power to declare what the law shall be is a legislative power. But the power to construe and apply the law is essentially judicial. A statute should be construed in the light of the objective to be achieved and the evil or mischief sought to be suppressed. A law is not a mere composition, but an end to be achieved; and its general purpose is a more important aid to its meaning than any rule of grammar may lay down. As can be learned from legislative deliberations, the AntiPlunder Law was enacted to curb the "despoliation of the National Treasury by some public officials who have held the levers of power" and to penalize "this predatory act which has reached unprecedented acts and has been developed by its practitioners to a high level of sophistication during the past dictatorial regime." Viewed broadly, "plunder involves not just plain thievery but economic depredation which affects not just private parties or personal interests but the nation as a whole." Invariably, plunder partakes of the nature of a "crime against national interest which must be stopped, and if possible stopped permanently" (citing Senator Tanada's sponsorship speech of June 5, 1989).

## No Clear Conflict with the Constitution

The law must be proven to be clearly and unequivocally repugnant to the Constitution before this Court may declare its unconstitutionality. To strike down the law, there must be a clear showing that what the fundamental law prohibits, the statute allows to be done. Of some terms in the law which are easily clarified by judicial construction, petitioner has merely managed to point out alleged ambiguities. Which is why the constitutional challenge to the Anti-Plunder Law must fail. For just as the accused is entitled to the presumption of innocence in the absence of proof beyond reasonable doubt, so must the law be accorded the presumption of constitutionality without the requisite quantum of proof.

II. Second issue - Quantum of Evidence Not Lowered by RA 7080

Petitioner Estrada 's counsel contends that the Anti-Plunder Law "eliminates proof of each and every component criminal act of plunder by the accused and limits itself to just establishing a pattern of overt or criminal acts indicative of unlawful scheme or conspiracy," without the necessity of establishing proof beyond reasonable doubt of each and every criminal act done by the accused. He concludes that RA 7080 has lowered the quantum of evidence required to secure a conviction under the challenged law. The argument is clearly erroneous.

First, it is against the common sense presumption that the legislature intended to enact a valid, sensible and just law. Second, it is contradicted by the Legislative Records that manifest the real intent behind Section 4, which is carefully expressed by the words of Senate President Salonga:

"Senate Pres. Salonga said that, if there are let's say 150 crimes all in all, criminal acts, whether bribery, misappropriation, malversation, extortion, you need not prove all of those beyond reasonable doubt. If you can prove by pattern, let's say 10, but each must be proved beyond reasonable doubt, you do not have to prove 150 crimes. That's the meaning of this." (Deliberations of the Committee, Nov. 15, 1988, cited in the July 9, 2001 Sandiganbayan Resolution).

This means that the prosecution's burden of proving the crime of plunder is, in actuality, much greater than in an ordinary criminal case, since the indicative pattern must also be proven beyond reasonable doubt, in addition to proving beyond reasonable doubt, in addition to proving beyond reasonable doubt the acts which constitute the combination or series under Section 2 of the Anti-Plunder Law citing the Sandiganbayan Resolution of July 9, 2001).

III. Third issue - the Constitutional Power of Congress to

Enact Mala Prohibita Laws

The argument of Petitioner Estrada's counsel is that RA 7080 "eliminated the element of mens rea from crimes which are mala in se and converted these crimes which are components of plunder into mala prohibita, thereby rendering it easier to prove since "the prosecution need not prove criminal intent," thus violating the due process and the equal protection clauses of the Constitution.

Regardless of whether plunder is classified as mala prohibita or in se, it is the prerogative of the legislature to determine whether certain acts are criminal irrespective of the actual intent of the perpetrator. The acts mentioned in Section 1 (d) of the Anti-Plunder Law - bribery, conversion, fraudulent conveyance, unjust enrichment and the like - cannot be committed without criminal intent. When we speak of plunder, we are referring essentially to two or more instances of mala in se constituting one malum prohibitum. There should be no difficulty if each of the predicate acts be proven beyond reasonable doubt as mala in se, even if the defense of lack of intent be taken away, as suggested by the solicitor general. In brief, the matter of classification is not really significant; the important thing is that the same burden of proof - proof beyond reasonable doubt - would apply.

## Consequence of the Supreme Court Decision in This Case

As a result of the Court’s decision, petitioner Estrada is now given the occasion to face squarely - and on the merits - the plunder charges hurled at him by the Ombudsman. He may now use the opportunity to show the courts and the Filipino people that he is indeed innocent of the heinous crime of plunder - to do so, not by resorting to mere legalisms, but by showing the sheer falsity of the offenses attributed to him. He is now accorded the opportunity to prove his clear conscience and lack of culpability, says Justice Panganiban in the concluding portion of his opinion.

## Summary

Chapter 16 of 'Reforming the Judiciary' presented the Supreme Court's decision in Estrada v. Sandiganbayan upholding the Anti-Plunder Law. CJP supplied only the opening frame: he recalled that on March 2, 2001 the Court had unanimously upheld Gloria Macapagal Arroyo's ascension and denied Estrada immunity, that the Ombudsman then charged him with plunder under RA 7080, and that Estrada, 'in a bid to shortcut his detention and obtain immediate acquittal,' attacked the law's constitutionality; he then reprinted, with permission, former Senate President Jovito R. Salonga's Kilosbayan article as the chapter proper. Salonga, who had drafted the measure as Senate Bill No. 733 in August 1988 out of his PCGG experience, recounted its unanimous passage by the post-EDSA Senate -- including several affirmative votes by Senator Estrada himself -- and set out the charge of more than four billion pesos in illegal-gambling protection money, tobacco excise funds, Belle Corp. commissions and the 'Jose Velarde' deposits. He then explained the 10-4-1 Decision opinion by opinion. Justice Bellosillo held the law presumed constitutional, its standards ascertainable, its words 'combination' and 'series' plain on the legislative record, its Section 4 no dispensation from proof beyond reasonable doubt, and Estrada estopped from claiming ignorance of a law he had voted for. Justice Mendoza concurred that strict scrutiny, overbreadth and vagueness are tools for free speech cases and inapt for penal statutes, which must be judged as applied, and that plunder is a malum in se requiring mens rea. Justice Panganiban's own Separate Opinion answered the dissenters point by point: a penal law suffices if it answers 'What is the violation?'; 'simple statutory construction, not a declaration of unconstitutionality, is the key' to the undefined terms; 'pattern' is procedural rather than an essential element, so RA 7080 is not a copy of RICO; and the quantum of evidence was not lowered but raised. The article closed by noting that Estrada could now meet the charges on the merits, 'not by resorting to mere legalisms, but by showing the sheer falsity of the offenses attributed to him.'
