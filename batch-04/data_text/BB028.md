# Leveling the Playing Field -- Ch. 3: Not Only Prosperity, But Also Peace
Date:       2004-12-01
Publisher:  Not stated in the file (Google Books lists the publisher as 'Artemio V. Panganiban, 2004')
Source:     Leveling the Playing Field, Not stated in the file (Google Books lists the publisher as 'Artemio V. Panganiban, 2004') — ch. 3 (batch-04 split)
By: Artemio V. Panganiban

I am happy to be reunited with the Legal Management Council of the Philippines (LMCP). As some of you may know, I was — prior to my appointment to the Supreme Court — a vice-president and board member of this association of corporate lawyers. It was also in this collegium that the late Senator Rene “Compañero” Cayetano, my LMCP contemporary, developed his wings of leadership.

Certainly, my memoir of our organization would not be complete without a remembrance of how my then LMCP colleagues helped me hone myself for eventual service in the judiciary. Consistent with this tradition, I hope that many of you would aspire to join our government and help in rebuilding our country. After all, you have already established your fame and accumulated your fortune as counsel of the largest companies operating here. It is time to devote a part of your time, talent and treasure to help eradicate poverty, to banish fear and to preserve freedom in our land.

In his letter of invitation to me, LMCP President Ariel F. Abonal wrote that, being lawyers for the corporate sector, LMCP members “would like to know how the Supreme Court decided cases involving the business community.” I believe that this request was prompted by some unfounded criticisms that the High Court had been interfering unreasonably in economic governance. Our critics claim that the Supreme Court (SC) has no commercial expertise and should therefore refrain from deciding cases that affect the economy.

## SC Has Decided Economic Cases for Over a Century

Please note, however, that commercial transactions have long been sources of litigation. Courts had been hearing and deciding controversies that involved the economic life of our people even before all of us — including our critics — were born. Suits spawned by the old Code of Commerce, which was introduced here by our Spanish colonizers, have been adjudged by our courts for more than a century.

Indeed, the judiciary has always been concerned with controversies surrounding the sale or the lease of properties — those involving the construction of building and other civil works, the reasonability of interest rates, the constitutionality of agreements relating to the country's natural resources — just as much as violations of criminal laws, the settlement of marital and family disputes, the partition of estates and the sharing of inheritance.

Recently added as subjects of judicial review have been issues spawned by the information age, like patenting inventions, copyrighting intellectual properties, and regulating new methods of communication. Even problems brought about by the dawning bioscientific age — those involving genetically modified organisms, in vitro fertilization, and cloning of living organisms — have already reached the courts in many countries.

If the judiciary were to be excluded from deciding these questions, who or what agency would then settle them? And by what laws and under what standards? Who would protect the parties and the public from economic tyrannies, excesses and abuses? The proposal to exclude economic issues from judicial review would simply not solve problems. Instead, it would merely create new ones with no answers.

## Controversies in All Branches of Knowledge Are Open to SC Review

True, jurists may not be physicians or engineers, but they are still called upon to resolve litigations relating to the causes of death in murder trials and to construction complaints in condominium purchases. They may not be computer geeks, but they must still take up questions arising from e-commerce and digitized software. By the same token, they may not be economists, but they must still decide cases arising from oil cartels, deregulation laws, and the privatization of government-run businesses.

In all these judicial controversies — whether criminal, civil or commercial in nature — it is up to the parties to argue their causes properly and, if necessary, to educate the courts on the technical aspects of their cases. While jurists may not have expertise in all fields of human knowledge, they do have a general understanding of these disciplines. As I have always said, judges are not merely walking indexes of the Constitution, the codes and the law books; they are also expected to have a working idea of all branches of human knowledge, including economics.

In any event, I respectfully submit that in tackling economic or commercial issues, the Supreme Court has been prudent, transparent and careful in its judgments. Let me support this general proposition with some specific guidelines and principles used by the High Court in deciding such controversies.

## SC Defers to Other Agencies of Government

First, contrary to the claims of its critics, the Court —— as a rule — is deferential to the actions taken by the other branches of government that have primary responsibility for the economic development of our country. Thus, in upholding the Philippine ratification of the treaty establishing the World Trade Organization (WTO), Tañada. Angara’ held that “this Court never forgets that the Senate, whose act is under review, is one of the two sovereign Houses of Congress and is thus entitled to great respect in its actions. It is itself a constitutional body, independent and coordinated, and its actions are presumed regular and done in good faith. Unless convincing proof and persuasive arguments are presented to overthrow such presumption, this Court will resolve every doubt in its favor.”

## SC Does Not Normally Interfere in Pure “Political Questions”

Second, the Supreme Court does not normally interfere in so-called “political questions”; meaning, it does not review the wisdom of the actions of the other branches of government. The Court does not take up issues that under the Constitution — are to be decided by the people in their sovereign capacity, or in regard to which full discretionary authority has been delegated either to the legislative or to the executive branch of government.’ Thus, it will not pass upon the merits of trade liberalization and economic globalization as economic policies. These are matters that the people have given the President and Congress to evaluate and decide. ‘To quote again from I: Tañada v. Angara, “as to whether the nation should join the worldwide march toward trade liberalization and economic globalization is a matter that out people should determine in electing their policymakers x x x. Let the people, through their duly authorized elected officers, make their free choice.”

In relation to this subject, may I raise the caveat that even if the question posed appears to be political in nature, the Court may still resolve it, if it entails a determination of unconstitutionality or of grave abuse of discretion. The question becomes justiciable when the Constitution provides conditions, limitations or restrictions in the exercise of a power vested in a specific branch or instrumentality. Here the Court reviews, not the wisdom involved in the exercise of the power, but whether — in such exercise — the conditions, limitations or restrictions imposed by the fundamental law have been obeyed.

## SC Does Not Interfere in Private Transactions

Third, the same laissez-faire policy is generally observed by courts in reviewing contracts in which both parties are private individuals (or nongovernmental entities), and in which the subject matter is a strictly commercial transaction. Thus, “neither the law nor the courts will extricate a party from an unwise or undesirable contract he or she entered into with all the required formalities and with full awareness of its consequence.”

One notable exception to this general principle is in regard to very high stipulated interest rates. Although the usury law has been suspended by Central Bank Circular No. 905, Series of 1982, the Court has used its equity power several times to strike down or to reduce “excessive, iniquitous, unconscionable, and exorbitant” interest rates and penalties — like in one case, 10 percent compounded monthly;? or in another, 66 percent per annum;° and very recently, 192 percent per annum.’

Let me stress again the principle that “parties are free to stipulate terms and conditions which they deem convenient, provided they are not contrary to law, morals, good customs, public order or public policy.”

## SC Respects Decisions of Lower Courts

Fourth, always accorded great respect are decisions rendered by lower tribunals, especially the Court of Appeals; and constitutionally mandated agencies, like the Commission on Elections, the Commission on Audit, the Commission on Civil Service and the Ombudsman. Unless shown to have been issued with glaring “reversible errors” or “grave abuse of discretion,” these judgments are generally upheld. Petitions to overturn them are routinely denied due course, via minute resolutions of the Court.

The same high regard is given to decisions of specialized agencies of government, like the National Labor Relations Commission, the Bureau of Land Management, the Monetary Board, the Philippine Ports Authority, the Energy Regulatory Commission, the Securities and Exchange Commission, and so on. In general, the Court defers to their specialized technical knowledge.

The fact that courts are vested with jurisdiction does not preclude prior resort to administrative agencies. When a matter calls for the specialized input of an administrative agency or relates to the regulatory authority conferred by statute upon it, then the doctrine of primary jurisdiction finds proper applicability.’ This doctrine, along with the principle of exhaustion of administrative remedies, is well-established in Philippine jurisprudence.

This policy on judicial restraint is consistent with the law of evidence, which presumes that official duty has been regularly performed.

## “Case and Controversy” Is Still Required

Fifth, contrary to some errant observations, the Court has not waived the requirement of “case and controversy.” Under the Constitution," the judicial power and duty of the courts to settle disputes cannot be exercised unless there is an actual or a threatened violation of a legally demandable and enforceable right. This principle applies even when the Court performs its duty to determine whether any instrumentality or agency of the government has acted with grave abuse of discretion.” Very recently, in Velarde v. SJS, the Supreme Court ruled that every petition must state “what specific legal right of the petitioner was violated by respondents; and what particular act or acts of the latter were in breach of its rights, the law or the Constitution.” Otherwise, a petition shall be dismissed for lack of “judicial controversy.” In short, the rendering of advisory opinions is beyond the constitutional mandate of the judiciary.

So, too, every complaint must state a cause of action. Conjectures or speculations cannot be the basis of a suit. Even in an action for declaratory relief, in which there has been “no actual breach of the instruments involved, or of the rights arising thereunder,” there must still be a legal transgression that is “impending, imminent or at least threatened.”

## “Locus Standi” and “Hierarchy of Courts” May Be Waived

While the requirements of justiciable controversy and cause of action cannot be waived, locus standi — the personal and substantial interest of the plaintiff — may be set aside when the controversy is of transcendental importance or paramount significance to the people.”

In line with the liberal policy of the Supreme Court on locus standi, even ordinary taxpayers have been allowed to initiate and prosecute actions before the Court." Such policy holds true especially in suits that seek to void errant contracts entered into by the government. The reason is that the parties with legal standing are usually not inclined to sue, because they stand to benefit from the agreements entered into. If ordinary citizens are not allowed to initiate these litigations, public interest would suffer, because no one else would bring suit. ‘The void agreements would thus be left unassailed.

The same liberal policy is observed in regard to the rule on the hierarchy of courts. The High ‘Tribunal gives due course to original actions involving urgent issues of transcendental importance, suits that should normally be commenced in the lower courts.

In short, procedural technicalities like locus standi and hierarchy of courts are waived by the Court when it is confronted with urgent or transcendental controversies.

## SC Is Protective of Public Contracts

Sixth, when one of the contracting parties is a government agency, or when the contract involves the expenditure of public funds, the Court is extra vigilant in protecting public interest. Here, the Court almost always leans on its constitutional duty'’ to strike down grave abuse of discretion. It thus invalidates agreements tainted with violations of the Constitution or the law. Let me now give some recent examples of agreements voided by the Supreme Court because of grave abuse of discretion.

1. In Chavez v. Public Estates Authority (PEA), the Joint Venture Agreement between PEA and Amari {Coastal Bay and Development Corporation was voided. The Court ruled that the Constitution banned the sale of reclaimed foreshore land to, and the reclamation of still submerged foreshore land by, a private corporation (Amari).

2. In Agan v. Philippine International Air Terminals Company (Piatco), the “Build-Operate-and-Transfer” Contract for the construction of Terminal III of the Manila International Airport was struck down. In giving Piatco “the exclusive right to operate a commercial passenger terminal within the Island of Luzon,” the government violated the anti-monopoly provisions of the fundamental law, and, in granting substantially more benefits to Piatco than those allowed by the bidding rules, the government transgressed the “fair competition essence” of the law.

3.  In Information Technology Foundation v. Comelec, the Contract for the supply of automated counting machines was set aside because, among others, it was awarded in contravention of the bidding rules laid down by the Commission on Elections, the awarding agency itself. The Court held that under the “revered axiom” of “leveling the playing field,” “each bidder must bid under the same conditions; and be subject to the same guidelines, so that the best offer or lowest bid may be determined, all other things being equal.” The Court added that “the substantive amendment of the contract bidded out - after the bidding process had been concluded - was violative of the x x x spirit and intent of RA 8436.”

4. In Jaworski v. PAGCOR, a Contract granting a private company (Sports and Games and Entertainment Corporation or SAGE) authority to operate on-line Internet gambling was voided, because the charter of the government-owned Philippine Amusement and Gaming Corporation (Pagcor) prohibited it from delegating its franchise to other entities.

## Contracts May Be Valid, But Still Be Ineffective

Still on this topic, many have asked why, in Metro Manila Development Authority v. Jancom Environmental Corporation, a Contract was held to be valid and perfected, yet not effective unless signed by the President of the Philippines. The answer is really simple. The Agreement contained all the essential requisites of a contract and underwent the “three distinct stages of negotiation, perfection and consummation.” Nonetheless, the Court ruled that the Contract had not become effective, because by its own provisions contained in Article 19 thereof, the “Contract shall become effective [only] upon approval by the President of the Philippines.”

May I also add that when a grave error in its decision is pointed out, the Supreme Court is not averse to reversing itself. Thus, in JG Summit Holdings, Inc. v. Court of Appeals the Court’s First Division unanimously ruled, inter alia, that “a shipyard is a public utility whose capitalization must be sixty percent (60%) Filipino-owned.” On reconsideration, however, the Court — “after carefully reviewing the applicable laws and jurisprudence” — ruled that a shipyard was NOT a public utility. Thus, it upheld the nght of a Japanese company (Kawasaki Heavy Industries Ltd. of Kobe, Japan) to bid and subsequently own more than forty percent of the Philippine Shipyard and Engineering Corporation, which in turn owned and operated a shipbuilding and repair facility in Subic Bay.”*

## To “Pass the Buck” Is to Neglect Duty

At this point, let me say that the lives of the justices would be much easier if they were simply to cop out and refuse to decide controversial economic issues. Indeed, the members of the Court would be spared from attack and would be able to bask in their comfort zones, if they were simply to wash their hands and refuse the responsibility of deciding economic questions.

For them to do so, however, would be to neglect their sworn obligation to uphold the Constitution. Our fundamental law has expressly imposed upon the Supreme Court and the entire judiciary the solemn duty, not just the power or authority, to settle all issues of grave abuse of discretion. ‘This duty cannot be evaded. Otherwise, the Court would be guilty of dereliction, of abandonment, of its avowed duty. When a government action is seriously alleged to have infringed the Constitution, it becomes not only the right — but in fact the duty — of - the judiciary to settle the dispute.”

## Conclusion

In closing, may I assure you, your principals and your clients, that the Supreme Court is keenly aware of its role in the economic development of our country. It knows that integrity, stability and predictability in judicial (decisions are needed to achieve the nation’s goal of prosperity and progress. Moreover, it joins our policymakers in their desire to attract investments, both local and foreign; to elevate the gross national product, the gross in-(ternational reserves, the per capita income of our people, the stock market index and other growth indicators.

It is my fervent hope, however, that our critics and friends alike would constantly realize that the Supreme Court has a sworn duty to uphold the Constitution and the rule of law at all times. ‘This is its primary contribution to good governance. Finally, I pray that everyone would understand that the Supreme Court safeguards not only food but also freedom; not only jobs but also justice; not only indulgences but also integrity; not only development but also democracy; not only prosperity but also peace.

I thank you.
