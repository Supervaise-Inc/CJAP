# Transparency, Unanimity & Diversity -- Ch. 8: First Philippine International Bank v. Court of Appeals: Honoring a Perfected Contract

I was uplifted intellectually and morally by the enthusiastic support that my fourteen colleagues gave me in unanimously carrying my first ponencia in Ty v. Trampe. Encouraged and motivated, I promised myself that I would work hard to reduce my initial case load of about a thousand cases’ to a more manageable level. I had written Ty v. Trampe in December 1995; in the following month I wrote sixteen other decisions, but I believe the most significant was First Philippine International: Bank v. Court of Appeals.

## Facts

Producers Bank of the Philippines, later renamed First Philippine International Bank, acquired six parcels of land previously owned by BYME Investment and Development Corporation. The original plaintiffs in the trial court, Demetrio Demetria and Jose O. Janolo, wanted to purchase the property. They met with the bank’s Property Management Department manager, Mercurio Rivera, to initiate negotiations for that purpose. In August 1987, Janolo, following the advice of Rivera, made a formal purchase offer of P3.5 million through a letter dated August 30, 1987. Rivera answered, stating that the bank’s price was B5.5 million. On September 17, 1987, Janolo made a counter offer of P4.25 million. On September 28, 1987, plaintiffs met with Luis Co, the senior vice president of the bank. Rivera and a BYME lawyer attended the meeting. On September 30, 1987, Janolo sent the bank a letter stating that, in accordance with their agreement during the September 28, 1987 meeting, he was accepting the latter’s offer to sell the parcels of land at P5.5 million.

On November 4, 1987, the regular conservator of the bank (which had been placed under conservatorship by the Central Bank) was replaced by acting Conservator Leonida T. Encarnacion, who wrote to Demetria stating that “their proposal to buy” was being studied by a “newly created committee.”

Thereafter, Demetria and Janolo made numerous demands, insisting that there was already a perfected sale agreement. The bank rejected these demands, arguing that Rivera’s dealings were unauthorized. The bank refused to receive either the payment or the letter and, instead, openly advertised the parcels of land for sale. Thus, on May 16, 1988, plaintiffs filed a suit for specific performance.

On July 11, 1992, during the pendency of the above proceedings in the Court of Appeals, Henry Co and several other stockholders of the bank filed with the RTC a derivative suit against the plaintiffs to declare any perfected sale of the property as unenforceable.

## Main Issues

1. Did the derivative suit constitute forum shopping?

2. Was there a perfected contract of sale?

3. Was the Bank Conservator’s repudiation sufficient to overturn the contract, assuming it had been perfected?

## Ruling

1. Yes, there was forum shopping. Forum shopping obtains whenever, as a result of an adverse opinion in one forum, a party seeks a favorable opinion in another, other than by appeal or certiorari. The principle applies not only to suits filed in the courts, but also to litigations commenced in the courts while an administrative proceeding is pending.

In the instant case, there thus existed an identity of parties — or at least such parties who represented the same interests in both actions — as well as an identity of rights asserted and relief prayed for, the relief being founded on the same facts. The identity of the two preceding particulars was between the action before the CA and later this Court, on the one hand, and the derivative one filed with the RTC, on the other. This identity was such that any judgment rendered in the other action would, regardless of which party was successful, amount to res judicata in the action under consideration.

2. On the question whether there was a perfected contract, it was beyond cavil that the object of the transaction was the six (6) parcels of land owned by the bank, which clearly intended to sell the property. As testified to by Deputy Conservator Jose Entereso, the bank was looking for buyers of the property. Definitely, the plaintiffs wanted to purchase the property, and it was precisely for this purpose that they met with Rivera, manager of the Property Management Department of the bank, in early August 1987. The procedure for the sale of acquired assets, as well as the nature and the scope of his authority on the matter, was clearly delineated in his testimony. Moreover, from the evidence found by the Court of Appeals, it was obvious that he had apparent or implied authority to act for the Bank in the matter of selling its acquired assets.

Indeed, the case turned on the doctrine of apparent authority of officers to act on what the bank held out to the public as matters within their normal authority. Furthermore, the fact that during the September 28, 1987 meeting, the parties perfected a contract of sale - as found by the trial court and affirmed by the CA — was deemed by the Court to be “consistent with human experience, truth and good faith.”

3. No, under Section 28-A® of the Central Bank (CB) Law, a CB-appointed Conservator of a distressed bank could not repudiate the Contract of Sale validly entered into by management. The Court reasoned inter alia:

“x x x [While admittedly, the Central Bank law gives vast and far-reaching powers to the conservator of a bank, it must be pointed out that such powers must be related to the ‘(preservation of) the assets of the bank, (the reorganization of) the management thereof and (the restoration of) its viability.’ Such powers, enormous and extensive as they are, cannot extend to the post-facto repudiation of perfected transactions; otherwise they would infringe against the non-impairment clause of the Constitution.‘ If the legislature itself u cannot revoke an existing valid contract, how can it delegate such non-existent powers to the conservator under Section 28-A of said law?

“Obviously, therefore, Section 28-A merely gives the conservator power to revoke contracts that are, under existing law, deemed to be defective — i.e., void, voidable, unenforceable or rescissible. Hence, the conservator merely takes the place of a bank’s board of directors. What the said board cannot do — such as repudiating a contract validly entered into under the doctrine of implied authority — the conservator cannot do either. Ineluctably, his power is not unilateral and he cannot simply repudiate valid obligations of the bank. His authority would be only to bring court actions to assail such contracts — as he has already done so in the instant case. A contrary understanding of the law would simply not be permitted by the Constitution. Neither by common sense. To rule otherwise would be to enable a failing bank to become solvent, at the expense of third parties, by simply getting the conservator to unilaterally revoke all previous dealings which had in one way or another come to be considered unfavorable to the bank, yielding nothing to perfected contractual rights or vested interests of the third parties who had dealt with the bank.”

In its Epilogue, the Decision expressed its sympathy for the distressed situation of the bank. But respect for the “rule of law and blind justice” must be upheld. Said the Court: “We are not unmindful of the tenacious plea that the petitioner bank is operating abnormally under a government-appointed conservator and ‘there is need to rehabilitate the bank in order to get it back on its feet x x x as many people depend on (it) for investments, deposits and well as employment. As of June 1987, the bank’s overdraft with the Central Bank had already reached P1.023 billion.... and there were (other) offers to buy the subject properties for a substantial amount of money.”

“While we do not deny our sympathy for this distressed bank, at the same time, the Court cannot emotionally close its eyes to overriding considerations of substantive and procedural law, like respect for perfected contracts, non-impairment of obligations and sanctions against forum-shopping, which must be upheld under the rule of law and blind justice.

“This Court cannot just gloss over private respondent’s submission that, while the subject properties may currently command a much higher-price, it is equally true that at the time of the transaction in 1987, the price agreed upon of P5.5 million was reasonable, considering that the bank acquired these properties at a foreclosure sale for no more than P3.5 million. That the bank pro- crastinated and refused to honor its commitment to sell cannot now be used by it to promote its own advantage, to enable it to escape its binding obligation and to reap the benefits of the increase in land values. To rule in favor of the bank simply because the property in question has [exponentially] accelerated in price during the long period of litigation is to reward lawlessness and delays in the fulfillment of binding contracts. Certainly, the Court cannot stamp its imprimatur on such outrageous proposition.”

The case would have ended with that Epilogue. But the bank, unyielding in defeat, filed two Motions for Reconsideration, the second of which even blamed the Court for its financial malaise. In response, I quote our Resolution dated July, 17, 1996, disposing of the Second Motion for Reconsideration thus:

“Under paragraph 7 of the Resolution dated April 7, 1988 of the Court En Banc, ‘(w)here the Court has resolved to deny a motion for reconsideration and decrees the denial to be final, no motion for leave to file a second motion for reconsideration shall be entertained.’ Inasmuch as the (first) Motion for Reconsideration dated February 15, 1996 has been denied with finality per our Resolution dated March 4, 1996, it follows that the Omnibus Motion as well as the Second Motion for Reconsideration should be DENIED outright. Even the presence of ‘new grounds’ or ‘newly discovered errors’ are not sufficient grounds therefor, because ‘litigations must come to an end at some point’, i.e. in this case, after a Motion for Reconsideration is denied with finality. This Court stressed this principle in a Resolution in G.R. No. 109645, entitled Ortigas and Company Limited Partnership vs. Judge Tirso Velasco, et al., promulgated on March 4, 1996, as follows:

‘A second motion for reconsideration is forbidden except for extraordinary persuasive reasons, and only upon express leave first obtained. The propriety or acceptability of such a second motion for reconsideration is not contingent upon the averment of ‘new’ grounds to assail the judgment, i.e., grounds other than those therefor presented and rejected. Otherwise, attainment of finality of a judgment might be staved off indefinitely, depending on the party’s ingeniousness or cleverness in conceiving and formulating ‘additional flaws’ or ‘newly discovered errors’ therein, or thinking up some injury or prejudice to the rights of the movant for reconsideration. ‘Piecemeal’ impugnation of a judgment by successive motions for reconsideration is anathema, being precluded by the salutary axiom that a party seeking the setting aside of a judgment, act or proceeding must set out in his motion all the grounds therefor, and those not so included are deemed waived and cease to be available for subsequent motions.

‘For all litigation must come to an end at some point, in accordance with established rules of procedure and jurisprudence. As a matter of practice and policy, courts must dispose of every case as promptly as possible; and in fulfillment of their role in the administration of justice, they should brook no delay in the termination of cases by stratagems or maneuverings of parties or their lawyers. The Court recently had occasion to reaffirm these basic postulates in In Re Joaquin T. Borromeo, viz.:

‘It is x x x of the essence of the judicial function that at some point, litigation must end. Hence, after the procedures and processes for lawsuits have been undergone, and the mode of review set by law have been exhausted or terminated, no further ventilation of the same subject matter is allowed. To be sure, there may be, on the part of the losing parties, continuing dis-agreement with the verdict, and the conclusions therein embodied. This is of no moment, indeed, is to be expected, but it is not their will, but the Court’s, which must prevail; and, to repeat, public policy demands that at some definite time, _ the issues must be laid to rest and the court’s dispositions thereon

125 accorded absolute finality (with voluminous citations, including Garbo v. Court of Appeals, 226 SCRA 250, G.R. No. 100474, September 10, 1993; GSIS v. Gines, 219 SCRA 724, G.R. No. 85273, March 9, 1993; Gesulgon ¥. NLRC, 219 SCRA 561, G.R. 90349 March 5, 1993; Paramount Insurance Corporation v. Japson, 211 SCRA 879, G.R. No. 68073, July 29, 1992; Cachola v. CA, 208 SCRA 496, G.R. No. 97822, May 7, 1992; Enriquez v. C.A., 202 SCRA 487, GR. No. 83720, October 4, 1991; Alvendia v. IAC, 181 SCRA 252, G.R. No. 72138, January 22, 1990, etc.). As observed by this Court in Rheem of the Philippines v. Ferrer, a 1967 decision (20 SCRA 441, 444), a party ‘may think highly of his intellectual endowment. That is his privilege. And he may suffer frustration at what he feels is others’ lack of it. This is his misfortune. Some such frame of mind, however, should not be allowed to harden into a belief that he may attack a court's decision in words calculated to jettison the time-honored aphorism that courts are the temples of right.’

“Be that as it may, we still decided to take a last look at the case, particularly the matters raised in the proposed second motion for reconsideration, not to reverse the foregoing doctrines [or] even to make an exception thereto but to satisfy ourselves that justice is a{cc]orded all parties. After a meticulous review of said matters and a judicious deliberation by the Court, we have come to the ineluctable conclusion that our Decision should remain and the said motions be denied.

Petitioners’ proposed second motion for reconsideration basically raises four points: (1) there was no forum shopping, (2) neither was there a perfected nor enforceable contract of sale, (3) the Conservator had power to revoke acts of the previous management, and (4) the sale of the subject properties will create ‘grave injuries,’ particularly to petitioner bank which will ‘be made liable to pay capital gains tax of about P12.625 million (exclusive of documentary stamp tax) which is twice higher than the purchase price.’

“Anent petitioners’ insistence that there was no forum shopping, suffice it to say that the disquisition in pages 13 to 27 of the Decision is more than sufficient to refute this contention. We have already explained why Victronics is not applicable here and [we] will not repeat such explanation.

“On the question of whether or not there was an actual meeting of minds on the sale, we refer back to the facts of the case. While there could be some argument about the authority of Mercurio Rivera as well as the conclusiveness of the transaction up to his level, all such doubts were resolved during the meeting of the parties on September 28,

127 1987 attended by Luis Co, a senior management officer and stockholder of the Bank. The letter of Janolo dated September 30, 1987 ‘accepting’ the ‘offer’ of the Bank at the ‘total price of PESOS: FIVE MILLION FIVE HUNDRED THOUSAND (P5,500,000.00)’ swept away any such argument(s] or doubts about the perfection of the contract. Be it noted that this letter was never even replied to by the bank until it was too late, by the letter dated November 4, 1987 sent by Rivera to Demetria — which, late as it was, did not even refute the ‘acceptance’ of the ‘offer’ but merely said it was ‘under study yet as of this time by the newly-created committee for submission to the newly designated acting Conservator of the Bank.’ If indeed there was no offer made by Co and Rivera during the September 28, 1987 meeting, they or either one of them should have denied vehemently and immediately such ‘fact.’ This is the ordinary human reaction to a supposed misrepresentation.

“We are aware of the persistence of petitioners in contesting private respondent's version of what transpired during that meeting on September 28, 1987. However, both the trial court and the Court of Appeals were one in upholding such version. It is an age-old doctrine approximating immutability that findings of facts of the Court of Appeals affirming the trial court’s own findings are entitled to great respect and even finality. Petitioners have not presented any | ‘extraordinarily persuasive reason’ in their motion why we should abandon or make an exception from such firmly rooted rule.

“Petitioners’ arguments regarding the powers of the Conservator have also been sufficiently taken up in pages 44 to 48 of the Decision. We find no cogent reason to warrant a disturbance of our dictum therein.

“As to petitioners’ contention that the sale will expose the bank ‘to pay capital gains tax of about P12.625 million (exclusive of documentary stamp tax),’ we quote the arithmetics of private respondent in his comment:

‘Petitioners’ statement betrays its utter lack of candor in dealing with this Honorable Court. Both the bank and its acquired assets Manager, Mr. Rivera, should know from their long experience in the banking business that corporations, like petitioner bank, are not subject to the 5% capital gains tax on the sale of real property but to the 35% corporate income tax based on ordinary taxable gain under Section 24 (a) of the Tax Code, as amended. Thus, Section 21 (e) of the Tax Code provides for capital gains tax from sales of real property by individuals:

‘Capital gains from sales of real property. — The provisions of Section 33 (b) notwithstanding, capital gains presumed to have been realized from the sale, exchange or other disposition of real property located

129 in the Philippines classified as capital assets, including pacto de retro sales and other forms of conditional sales, by individuals, including estates and trust, shall be taxed at the rate of 5% based on the gross selling price or the fair market value prevailing at the time of sale whichever is higher, Provided, That the tax liability, if any, on gains from sales or other dispositions of real property to the government or any of its political subdivisions or agencies or to government-owned or controlled corporations shall be determined either under Section 21 (a) or under this subsection, at the option of the taxpayer.’

‘Under Section 24 (a) of the Tax Code, on the other hand, domestic corporations are subject only to a tax of 35% ‘upon the taxable income received during each taxable year from all sources within and without the Philippines.” These income include taxable gain realized in the sale of real property. x x x.

‘Thus, the Bank’s gain from the transaction is P1,466,736.00, representing the difference between the selling price of P5.5 million and the acquisition price of P3,033,264.00 made by the Bank during the foreclosure sale. The corporate income tax of 35% due on this taxable gain is P513,357.60, not P12.626 million as petitioners falsely claim in their omnibus and second motion for reconsideration. The amount of P513,357.60 does not include the exaggerated expenses claimed by the bank from 1986 to January 1996, totaling P19,105,695.11 consisting of ‘real estate taxes (P344,492.05), litigation and lawyers’ fees (P10,418,302.56) [!], security guards (P4,745.280.83), fencing costs (P2,074,643.09), sheriff's expenses (P163,666.95), Bureau of Internal Revenue (P30,940.00), survey expenses (P112,028.70) and others (P666,725.11).’ Second Motion for Reconsideration, p. 18)’

“In their reply, petitioners have not challenged the above ‘computation.’ Neither will this Court.

“Finally, Petitioner First Bank reiterates its plea to give consideration to its status as a ‘distressed bank,’ with accumulated losses of ‘approximately P1.344 billion’ as of December 31, 1995. The ‘loss of the subject properties,’ it continues, ‘will no doubt seriously impair the Bank’s capacity to pay its liabilities to the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas standing at P1.3 billion.’ It thus blames this Court because the Bank’s ‘death kn[e]ll has been tolled by [its] recent ruling.’ Such irresponsible finger pointing is completely misplaced and cannot evoke even the slightest consideration. The bank's almost bankrupt condition was caused by its own mismanagement and neglect, not

131 by this Court [or] its Decision. Neither should the bank now look to this Court for extra-legal solutions like the unceremonious setting aside of the sale of the subject property so it could cover its abysmal deficits and save it from its own imprudence. In our Decision, we have expressed ‘our sympathy for this distressed bank’ but such feeling cannot override our superior constitutional duty to dispense justice for all.”

## Summary

Chapter 8 of 'Transparency, Unanimity & Diversity' recounted First Philippine International Bank v. Court of Appeals, which CJP called the most significant of the sixteen decisions he wrote in the month after his unanimously supported first ponencia. Buyers Demetrio Demetria and Jose O. Janolo had negotiated for six parcels of land with Mercurio Rivera, the bank's Property Management Department manager, met senior vice president Luis Co on September 28, 1987, and accepted the bank's P5.5 million price by letter two days later; the bank, then under Central Bank conservatorship, later disowned Rivera's dealings, advertised the land for sale, and was sued for specific performance, while its stockholders filed a derivative suit in another court during the appeal. The Court held first that the derivative suit was forum shopping, since the parties, rights and relief were identical enough that judgment in one action would be res judicata in the other; second, that a contract of sale had been perfected at the September 28 meeting, because Rivera had apparent or implied authority and the lower courts' concurrent finding was consistent with human experience, truth and good faith; and third, that a conservator's powers, however vast, relate to preserving assets and restoring viability and cannot extend to post-facto repudiation of perfected transactions without infringing the non-impairment clause. Its Epilogue conceded sympathy for the distressed bank, whose overdraft had reached P1.023 billion, but refused to let that override perfected contracts and the sanctions against forum shopping, noting that rewarding the bank for procrastinating while land values rose would reward lawlessness and delay. When the bank filed a second motion for reconsideration blaming the Court for its malaise, the Resolution of July 17, 1996 denied it outright, restating that litigation must end at some point and that piecemeal impugnation of judgments is anathema, correcting the bank's tax arithmetic from P12.625 million to P513,357.60, and attributing its near-bankruptcy to its own mismanagement and neglect.
