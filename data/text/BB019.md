# Transparency, Unanimity & Diversity -- Ch. 9: The PCGG Cases: Recovery of Ill-Gotten Wealth
Date:       2000-11-01
Publisher:  Supreme Court Printing Services
Source:     Transparency, Unanimity & Diversity, Supreme Court Printing Services — ch. 9 (batch-04 split)
By: Artemio V. Panganiban

During my five years in the Court, I have written a number of unanimous decisions involving the Presidential Commission on Good Government (PCGG) and its crusade to recover alleged ill-gotten wealth of former President Ferdinand E. Marcos, his family members, nominees, cronies and associates. Perhaps the most significant was Chavez v. PCGG, which invalidated the Compromise Agreements entered into by the Marcos heirs and the PCGG.

Here are five unanimous PCGG cases:

A. First Philippine Holdings Corporation v. Sandiganbayan and Benjamin Romualdez,’ which held that the Sandiganbayan had jurisdiction to admit interventions of parties claiming interests in, sequestered properties, even if jurisdiction over conflicting claims of stock ownership were normally lodged in other tribunals or agencies. The Court explained that the authority of the PCGG and the Sandiganbayan over ill-gotten wealth was exclusive, plenary and all-embracing.

B. Republic v. Sandiganbayan and Dio Island Resort,’ which ruled that sequestration orders issued by a “PCGG representative and head of its task force” was void; only the Commission or its members could do so.

C. Republic v. Sandiganbayan and Provident International Resources, which upheld the Writ of Sequestration dated March 19, 1986, even if signed by only one PCGG commissioner, because it had been issued before the promulgation of the PCGG Rules and Regulations on April 11, 1986 which required the “authority of at least two commissioners.”

D. Republic v. Sandiganbayan and Imelda Cojuangeo,t which annulled the Writ of Sequestration dated May 9, 1986 signed by one PCGG commissioner after the PCGG Rules had been promulgated on April 11, 1986.

E. Chavez v. Presidential Commission on Good Government, which voided the “75-25” Compromise Agreement and Supplemental Agreement, both entered into between the Estate of President Marcos and the PCGG on December 28, 1993.

Let me now summarize these five cases.

## A. First Philippine Holdings Corporation v. Sandiganbayan and Benjamin Romualdez

## Facts

In Civil Case No. 0035 filed before the Sandiganbayan, PCGG prayed for the return, the reconveyance, the accounting and the restitution — with damages — of certain funds and properties that had allegedly been acquired by Spouses Benjamin and Juliet Romualdez, Trans-Middle East Equities Inc. (Equities) and Edilberto S. Narciso Jr. through “abuse of right and power and through unjust enrichment.” The Petition stated that part of these funds and properties were 6,299,177 sequestered shares of stock in the Philippine Commercial International Bank (PCIBank). Respondent spouses, as beneficial owners, allegedly acquired these shares in violation of the Anti- Graft and Corrupt Practices Act. Furthermore, such shares were purportedly purchased from First Philippine Holdings Corporation (First Philippines) by Romualdez, using Equities and Narciso as “his dummy buyers,” with “no or negligible ‘cash out.’” Thus, as “unexplained wealth,” they were subject to forfeiture in favor of the Republic of the Philippines.

The Sandiganbayan granted the Motion for Intervention filed by Equities, which claimed ownership of the shares, as well as the corresponding rights appurtenant to ownership, like the right to vote those shares and to receive dividends.

First Philippines filed its own “Motion for Leave’ 1o Intervene and to Admit Complaint in Intervention” in said Civil Case No. 0035 pending before the Sandiganbayan, alleging that the PCIBank shares had been “obtained x x x by means of fraud and acts contrary to law, morals, good customs and public policy, as well as in breach of fiduciary duty.” Thus, it further contended, their acquisition through a Deed of “Sale of Stocks and Escrow Agreement” dated May 24, 1984, was either voidable or void or unenforceable on any of said grounds.

## Issues

1. Should First Philippines (formerly Meralco Securities Corporation) be allowed to intervene and litigate its claim of ownership over the 6,299,177 sequestered PCIBank shares of stock in the pending Sandiganbayan case, which had been filed by the government for the reconveyance of those shares in its favor on the ground that they allegedly formed part of the “ill-gotten” wealth of Benjamin “Kokoy” Romualdez?

2. Did the Sandiganbayan have jurisdiction over the case?

## Ruling

1. Yes, First Philippines should be allowed to intervene. Section 2 of Rule 12 of the Rules of Court allows interventions in judicial proceedings. Intervention is a remedy by which a third party, not originally impleaded in a proceeding, becomes a litigant therein to enable him to protect or preserve a right or res that may be affected by such proceeding. Its purpose is “to settle in one action and by a single judgment the whole controversy (among) the persons involved.”

There was no doubt that petitioner had a legal interest in the shares which were the subject of the controversy. At the very least, it was “so situated as to be adversely affected by a distribution or disposition of the (sequestered shares) in the custody of the court.”

In the Second Amended Complaint in Civil Case No. 0035 before the Sandiganbayan, the PCGG prayed that the shares in question, among other properties, should be returned and reconveyed to it. Respondent Equities opposed on the ground that it was the true owner thereof. On the other hand, First Philippines claimed that the shares belonged to it, not to Romualdez or Equities. Clearly, therefore, petitioner would be adversely affected by any judgment distributing or disposing of the property, whether to PCGG or to Equities or to Romualdez.

Unquestionably, the shares were sequestered and were thus “in the custody of the court” because, by sequestration, properties are placed in the control of a court to preserve them and/or to prevent their sale, encumbrance or disposition, pending the determination of the legality or the illegality of their acquisition and their true ownership. No such final determination was possible unless the parties who had legitimate but conflicting claims were made parties to or, as in this case, allowed to intervene in the main action.

2. Yes, the Sandiganbayan had jurisdiction over the case. Its jurisdiction had been clarified in PCGG v. Pena et al., which was reiterated in six subsequent cases decided jointly. In these cases, the Court held that “the exclusive jurisdiction conferred on the Sandiganbayan: would evidently extend not only to the principal causes of action, i.e., the recovery of alleged ill-gotten wealth,’ but also to ‘all incidents arising from, incidental to, or related to, such cases,’ such as the dispute over the sale of the shares, the propriety of the issuance of ancillary writs or provisional remedies relative thereto, [and] the sequestration thereof, which may not be made the subject of separate actions or proceedings in another forum.”

In the face of this previous ruling, the inescapable conclusion was that the intervention must be allowed; otherwise, the Sandiganbayan would not be able to determine the ultimate owner of the shares under sequestration.

## B. Republic of the Philippines and PCGG v. Sandiganbayan and Dio Island Resort

## Facts

On April 14, 1986, a Sequestration Order was issued by Atty. Jose Tan Ramirez, a PCGG representative and head of its task force in Region VIII, against Dio Island Resort, Inc. and all its assets and properties.

On July 22, 1987, the Republic of the Philippines, through the Presidential Commission on Good Government (PCGG), filed with the Sandiganbayan a Complaint against Alfredo T. Romualdez, Ferdinand E. Marcos, Imelda R. Marcos and forty-six (46) other defendants seeking to recover alleged “ill-gotten wealth” acquired and accumulated by these defendants. Although Dio Island Resorts, Inc. was mentioned in the Complaint as among those corporations in which Alfredo T. Romualdez purportedly owned shares of stock, it was not impleaded as a party thereto.

In its Manifestation and Motion filed in the same case, Dio Island asked the Sandiganbayan to stop the PCGG and its agents from further interfering with and exercising ownership over the resort and to require the return of its properties and assets covered by said Order. It further alleged that “up to the present time, there is no case filed against movant (Dio Island Resort) before the Sandiganbayan by reason of which, the alleged Sequestration Order is deemed automatically lifted pursuant to Article XVIII, Section 26 of the Constitution.” In the course of the hearing of the Motion, the validity of the Sequestration Order issued by PCGG’s representative, Atty. Jose Tan Ramirez, was directly questioned.

Alerted by such challenge, the PCGG passed a Resolution “to confirm, ratify and adopt as its own all the Writs of Sequestration issued in the name and under the authority of the Commission and signed by its duly authorized representatives in Region VIII, Messrs. Jose Ramirez and Ben Abella,” including the Sequestration Order over Dio Island Resort, in order “remove any doubt as to the validity” of the Writs.

The Sandiganbayan granted Dio Island’s Manifestation and Motion. The former held that the PCGG had not legally sequestered said corporation's assets, having been seized pursuant to an Order issued only by a “duly authorized representative” of the PCGG.

## Issues

1. Were Dio Island Resort and its assets validly sequestered?;

2. Did the Sandiganbayan have jurisdiction over a Motion questioning the validity of a Sequestration Order issued by a duly authorized representative of the PCGG?

## Ruling

1. The assets of Dio Island Resort were not properly sequestered. The Sequestration Order was not issued in accordance with the Rules and Regulations of the PCGG, Section 3 of which clearly outlined the requirements for the issuance of a sequestration order. This provision left no room for interpretation. Under no circumstances could a sequestration or freeze order be validly issued by one who was not a commissioner of the PCGG.

The invalidity of the Sequestration Order was made more apparent by the fact that Atty. Ramirez did not even have any specific authority to act on behalf of the Commission at the time he issued it.

_ 2. Under Section 2 of Executive Order No. 14, lodged within the exclusive and original jurisdiction of the Sandiganbayan were all the PCGG cases, civil or criminal, involving alleged ill-gotten properties of former President Marcos and his relatives, subordinates, cronies, nominees, and so forth. “Moreover, all incidents arising from, incidental to, or related to such cases necessarily fall likewise under the Sandiganbayan’s exclusive and original jurisdiction, subject to review on certiorari exclusively by the Supreme Court.”

Any remaining doubts with respect to the jurisdiction of the Sandiganbayan ought to have been removed by the pronouncement in PCGG v. Pena that “Ta}ny act or order transgressing the parameter of the objectives for which the PCGG was created, if tainted with abuse of discretion, is subject to a remedial action by the Sandiganbayan, the court vested with exclusive and original jurisdiction over cases involving the PCGG."

Obviously, respondent corporation’s Motion properly belonged to the class of cases challenging PCGG’s acts.

## C. Republic of the Philippines v. Sandiganbayan and Provident International Resources Corporation

## Facts

On March 19, 1986, the Presidential Commission on Good Government (PCGG) issued a Writ of Sequestration against all movable and immovable assets of Provident International Resources Corporation and Philippine Casino Operators Corporation (“respondent corporations”). The Writ was signed by one PCGG commissioner only, instead of two as required under Section 3 of the PCGG Rules and Regulations.

On July 29, 1987, petitioner, through the solicitor general, filed before the Sandiganbayan a Complaint against Edward T. Marcelo, Fabian C. Ver, Ferdinand E. Marcos and Imelda R. Marcos. Docketed as Civil Case No. 0021, the Complaint was for reconveyance, reversion, accounting and restitution of alleged ill-gotten wealth; as well as for damages. Respondent corporations were alleged.in the Complaint as among those being held and/or controlled by Marcelo and acquired illegally by defendants. Later, on October 30, 1991, the Complaint was amended to include both corporations as parties-defendants.

## Issues

1, Was a proper judicial action filed against respondent corporations in compliance with and within the period contemplated in Section 26, Article XVIII of the Constitution?

2. Was the Sequestration Order against respondent corporations, issued on March 19, 1986, valid and effective despite having been signed by only one commissioner, instead of two as required by the PCGG Rules and Regulations?

## Ruling

1. Yes, a proper judicial action was filed. As ruled in Republic v. Lobregat,’ it was unnecessary to implead the corporations in the Complaints, which sought to recover defendants’ shares of stock, and which in turn had allegedly been purchased with misappropriated public funds in breach of fiduciary duty or otherwise under illicit or anomalous conditions. The impleading of the corporations was not proper on the strength alone of their having been formed from ill-gotten wealth, absent any other particular wrongdoing on their part. If warranted by the evidence, the defendants may be handed judgments, which would divest them of ownership of their stocks, the acquisition of which was illegal and consequently burdened with a constructive trust. Such judgments would also impose on them the obligation of surrendering those stocks to the government.

In the instant case, respondent corporations were among the properties listed in the original Complaint (Civil Case No. 0021) as having been illegally accumulated by the defendants. They were subsequently impleaded as parties-defendants in the same case, by way of an amended Complaint duly granted by public respondent. Hence, there was faithful compliance with Section 26, Article XVIII of the Constitution.

2. Yes, the Sequestration Order was valid and effective. It was issued on March 19, 1986, prior to the promulgation of the PCGG Rules and Regulations. As a consequence, the PCGG could not be expected to abide by the Rules, which were nonexistent at the time the subject Writ was issued. Basic is the rule that no statute, decree, ordinance, rule or regulation shall be given retroactive effect, unless explicitly decreed. There is no provision in the said Rules that expressly gives them retroactive effect or implies the abrogation of previous Writs not issued in accordance therewith

## D. Republic v. Sandiganbayan and Imelda Cojuangco

## Facts

On May 9, 1986, the Presidential Commission on Good Government (PCGG) promulgated Orders (or Writs) of Sequestration against all properties, assets, records and documents of Prime Holdings, Inc. (PHI); and shares of stock of the Philippine Telecommunications Investment Corporation (PTIC) that had been issued in the name of PHI. Both Orders were signed solely by the late PCGG Commissioner Mary Concepcion Bautista.

On July 16, 1987, petitioner, through the solicitor general, filed before the Sandiganbayan a Complaint: for reconveyance, reversion, accounting, restitution and damages against Spouses Ferdinand and Imelda Marcos, Spouses Imelda (Imee) and Tomas Manotoc, Spouses Irene and Gregorio Ma. Araneta III, Ferdinand R. Marcos Jr., Constante Rubio, Nemesio G. Co, Yeung Chun Kam, Yeung Chun Ho and Yeung Chun Fan. The Complaint mentioned shares of stock in PTIC and PLDT as among the allegedly ill-gotten properties. On April 23, 1990, herein Private Respondents Imelda Cojuangco, the Estate of Ramon Cojuangco and PHI were made additional parties-defendants.

## Issues

1, Were the sequestration Orders validly issued?

2, Was a proper judicial action filed against private respondent corporations, as contemplated under Section 26, Article XVIII of the Constitution?

## Ruling

1, No, the sequestration Orders were not valid, because they had been signed by only one commissioner instead of two, as required by the PCGG Rules. The authority given by two commissioners for the issuance of a sequestration, freeze or hold order should have been evident in the Order itself. Simply stated, the PCGG Rules required that the Writ must bear the signatures of two commissioners, because their signatures were the best evidence by which the corporation or entity, against which the Writ was directed, would be able to visually determine the two commissioners’ approval and the document's validity. The Writs which bore only one signature were obviously transgressions of the PCGG Rules. Note that the former were issued after the latter took effect on April 11, 1986.

2. No, a proper judicial action was not filed in accordance with the pertinent constitutional provision.

First, the original Complaint for the recovery of ill-gotten wealth, which was filed on July 16, 1987, did not implead any of private respondents as parties. Neither were they included in the annexed list of alleged ill-gotten wealth. It was only on April 23, 1990, via an amended Complaint, that Imelda Cojuangco, the estate of Ramon Cojuangco, and Prime Holdings, Inc., were made parties-defendants. By then, three years — well beyond the six months prescribed by the Constitution — had passed since the issuance of the sequestration Orders against PHI and the PTIC shares it owned,

Second, even if PTIC was listed in the Annex to the Complaint, it must be understood that the case referred only to the extent of the shares in PTIC illegally acquired by the original defendants. Considered as another matter was whether PHI itself — an entirely different corporate entity, though a major investor in PTIC — had shareholdings unlawfully or anomalously acquired; that is, whether it had been organized with ill-gotten wealth. Notably, the individual respondents were the registered owners of PHI and, as earlier stated, they had not been included as original defendants in Civil Case No. 0002.

Third, the April 23, 1990 amended Complaint — filed for the specific purpose of impleading PHI, Imelda Cojuangco and the estate of Ramon Cojuangco as defendants — could not be deemed to have dated back to the filing of the original Complaint and to have thereby implied compliance with the constitutional | provision. It had been held that “an amendment which merely supplements and amplifies facts originally alleged in the complaint relates back to the date of the commencement of the action and is not barred by the statute of limitations which expired after the service of the original complaint.” Such rule did not, however, apply to a party who had been impleaded for the first time in an amended Complaint filed beyond the prescriptive period. In the latter case, the statute of limitations ran until the submission of the amendment.

## E. Chavez v. Presidential Commission on Good Governinent

## Facts

In December 1993, the Marcos heirs and the PCGG, through then Chairman Magtanggol Gunigundo, executed two Agreements “settl[ing] all claims and counterclaims which the parties may have against one another x x x.” They also agreed to split $356 million in Swiss deposits on a 75-25 percent sharing (75 percent for the government and 25 percent for the Marcoses). The Marcos heirs subsequently submitted the two Agreements to the Sandiganbayan (in Civil Case No. 141) for its approval.

Petitioner Francisco I. Chavez, as “taxpayer, citizen and former government official who initiated the prosecution of the Marcoses and their cronies x x x,” invoked his constitutional right to information and the correlative duty of the State to disclose to the public all its transactions involving the national interest. Accordingly, he demanded that respondents make public all negotiations and agreements pertaining to the recovery of the Marcoses’ alleged ill-gotten wealth. He also sought to prohibit the PCGG from privately entering into a compromise involving said wealth.

In the course of the proceedings, the heirs of Edgar Jopson, who was “salvaged” during the martial law years, intervened and joined petitioner in praying for the same reliefs.

## Issues

The procedural issues were as follows:

1. Did the petitioner have the personality or legal standing to file the Petition? —

2. Was this Court the proper venue for this original action?

These were the substantive issues:

3. Could the Supreme Court require the PCGG to disclose to the public the details of any agreement with the Marcoses, whether it was perfected or not?

4. Were the 75-25 Compromise Agreement and the Supplemental Agreement between the Marcos heirs and the PCGG valid?

## Ruling

1. Yes, the fact that the petitioner was a citizen satisfied the requirement of personal interest in the assertion of a public right, such as the right of access to public documents and records. Moreover, the intervention of the Jopsons rendered the issue of petitioner’s standing moot and academic.

2. Yes, this Court was the proper venue for this action. If petitioner were merely seeking to enjoin the enforcement of the compromise and/or to compel the PCGG to disclose to the public the terms contained in the Agreements, the Sandiganbayan would have been the proper venue, particularly in Civil Case No. 0141 in which the enforcement of the subject Agreements was pending resolution. However, the Petition involved not only the Agreements that had already been drawn, but likewise any other ongoing or future undertaking towards any settlement on the alleged Marcos loot. Ineluctably, the core issue boiled down to the precise interpretation of the scope of the twin constitutional provisions on “public transactions.” This broad and prospective relief sought by the Petition brought it out of the realm of Civil Case No. 0141.

3, The State has the duty to disclose publicly all “matters of public concern.” In general, documents coming into the hands of public officers in connection with their official functions must be accessible to the public, consistent with the policy of transparency. This principle is aimed at affording the people an opportunity to determine whether those to whom they have entrusted the affairs of the government are honestly, faithfully and competently performing their functions as public servants.

In February 1986, the new government headed by President Corazon C. Aquino was specifically mandated to “[r]ecover ill-gotten properties amassed by the leaders and supporters of the previous regime and [to] protect the interest of the people through orders of sequestration or freezing of assets or accounts.” Thus, President Aquino’s very first executive orders (which partook of the nature of legislative enactments) dealt with the recovery of these alleged ill-gotten properties. Such therefore were matters imbued with public interest.

It was the intent of the framers of the Constitution to include negotiating terms in government transactions within the constitutional provision on the public’s right to information on matters of public concern, subject only to reasonable safeguards for the national interest. It was then incumbent upon the PCGG and its officers, as well as other government representatives, to disclose sufficient public information on any proposed settlement with the ostensible owners and holders of ill-gotten wealth.

4. The General and Supplemental Agreements between the PCGG and the Marcos heirs contained serious legal flaws and are void. First, criminal immunity under Section 5 of EO No. 14, as amended, could not be granted to the Marcoses, who were the principal defendants in the spate of ill-gotten wealth cases pending before the Sandiganbayan. The immunity was applicable mainly to witnesses who would provide information or testify against the respondent, defendant or accused in an ill-gotten wealth case. There was no indication whatsoever that any of the Marcos heirs had indeed provided vital information against any such person as to the manner in which the latter might have unlawfully acquired public property.

Second, under Item No. 2 of the General Agreement, the PCGG was committed to exempt from all forms of taxes the properties to be retained by the Marcos heirs. This was a clear violation of the Constitution. The power to tax and to grant tax exemptions was vested in Congress, not in the PCGG. Section 28 (4), Article VI of the Constitution, specifically provides: “No law granting any tax exemption shall be passed without the concurrence of a majority of all the Members of the Congress.” The PCGG had no power to grant tax exemptions.

Even granting that Congress had enacted a law exempting the Marcoses from paying taxes on their properties, such law would definitely not have passed the test of the equal protection clause under the Bill of Rights. Any special grant of tax exemption in favor only of the Marcos heirs would have constituted class legislation. It would have also violated the constitutional rule that “taxation shall be uniform and equitable.”

Neither could the stipulation be construed to fall within the internal revenue commissioner’s authority to compromise taxes. Such authority may be exercised only when (a) there is reasonable doubt as to the validity of a claim against a taxpayer, and (b) the taxpayer’s financial position demonstrates a clear inability to pay.’ Definitely, neither requisite was present in the case of the Marcoses, because under the Agreement, they were effectively conceding the validity of the claims against their properties, part of which they would be allowed to retain. Neither could the PCGG’s grant of tax exemption fall within the commissioner’s power to abate or cancel a tax liability. This power can be exercised only when (a) a tax appears to be unjustly or excessively assessed, or (b) administration and collection costs do not justify the collection of the tax due.’ In this instance, the cancellation of tax liability was done even before the determination of the amount due. In any event, criminal violations of the Tax Code, for which legal actions had been filed in court or in which fraud was involved, could not be compromised.’

Third, the government bound itself to cause the dismissal of all cases against the Marcos heirs, cases which were pending before the Sandiganbayan and other courts. This stipulation was a direct encroachment on judicial powers, particularly in regard to criminal jurisdiction. Well-settled is the doctrine that once a case has been filed before a court of competent jurisdiction, the matter of its dismissal or pursuance lies within the full discretion and control of the judge. Jurisdiction, once acquired by the trial court, is not lost despite a resolution, even by the justice secretary, to withdraw the information or to dismiss the complaint. Thus, the PCGG, as the government prosecutor of ill-gotten wealth cases, could not guarantee the dismissal of all criminal cases against the Marcoses pending in the courts, for such dismissal was not within its sole power and discretion.

Fourth, the government also waived all claims and counterclaims against the Marcoses, “whether past, present, or future, matured or inchoate.” Again, this all-encompassing stipulation was contrary to law. Under the Civil Code, an action for future fraud may not be waived. The Agreement did not specify the exact scope of future claims against the Marcoses that the government would thereby relinquish. Such vague and broad statement could well be interpreted to include all future illegal acts of any of the Marcos heirs, practically giving them a license to perpetrate fraud against the government without any liability at all.

The stipulation was a palpable violation of the due process and equal protection guarantees of the Constitution, effectively ensconcing the Marcoses beyond the reach of the law. A dangerous precedent for public accountability, it virtually licensed public officials to amass public funds illegally, since they were given an open option to compromise their liability in exchange for only a portion of their alleged ill-gotten wealth.

Fifth, the Agreements did not provide for a definite or determinable period within which the parties should fulfill their respective prestations. It could have taken a lifetime for the Marcoses to submit an inventory of their total assets.

Sixth, the Agreements did not state with specificity the standards for determining which assets should be forfeited by the government and which should be retained by the Marcoses. While the Supplemental Agreement provided that the Marcoses should be entitled to 25 per cent of the $356 million Swiss deposits, such sharing arrangement pertained only to those deposits. No similar splitting scheme was defined with respect to the other properties. Neither was there any basis for the 75-25 percent sharing ratio. Public officers invite their indictment for corruption under the Anti-Graft and Corrupt Practices Act, when they enter into an arrangement that appears to be manifestly and grossly disadvantageous to the government, in violation of the said law.

Finally, the absence of then President Ramos’ approval of the principal Agreement, an express condition therein, rendered the compromise incomplete and unenforceable. Nevertheless, as detailed above, even if such approval were obtained, the Agreements would still not have been valid.
