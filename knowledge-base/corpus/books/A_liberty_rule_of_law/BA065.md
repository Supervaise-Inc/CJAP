# Leadership by Example: The Davide Standard -- Ch. 14: The Power of the Supreme Court to Review Internal Acts of Congress

The Supreme Court is the government arm vested with the ultimate power and duty to interpret the Constitution. In discharging this solemn responsibility, it has passed upon acts and decisions of the executive and the legislative branches of the government, and has annulled some of them on the ground that they had violated specific provisions of our basic law. Indeed, the Court is the guardian of the Constitution and the conscience of society.

Without doubt, the Court has the power and the responsibility “to determine whether or not there has been grave abuse of discretion amounting to lack or excess of jurisdiction on the part of any branch or instrumentality of government.” Thus, “[w]here the action of the legislative branch is seriously alleged to have infringed the Constitution, it becomes not only the right but in fact the duty of the judiciary to settle the dispute.” In taking jurisdiction over petitions questioning acts of the political departments of government, the Court will not review the wisdom, merit or propriety of such actions; it will strike them down only on either of two grounds: (1) unconstitutionality j or (2) grave abuse of discretion.

Question: Does the Supreme Court have jurisdiction to review internal acts of the Senate of the Philippines on an allegation in a petition that it has contravened the Constitution? In Santiago v. Guingona, the Court answered this in the affirmative by reiterating the well-settled doctrine “that jurisdiction over the subject matter of a case is determined by the allegations of the complaint or petition, regardless of whether the plaintiff or petitioner is entitled to the relief asserted.” In light of the allegations in the Petition that the Senate violated a specific provision of the Constitution, the Court ruled that it had jurisdiction over the controversy. In my ponencia in said case, unequivocally wrote: “It is well within the power and jurisdiction of the Court to inquire whether indeed the Senate or its officials committed a violation of the Constitution or gravely abused their discretion in the exercise of their functions and prerogatives.”

For a clearer understanding of the principle, let me summarize the factual background.

On July 27, 1998, by a vote of 20 to 2, the Senate elected Sen. Marcelo B. Fernan (now deceased) as its President. As the losing candidate, Senator Francisco S. Tatad manifested, with the agreement of Sen. Miriam Defensor Santiago — the only other member of the minority who had voted for him — that he was assuming the position of minority leader of the Senate. On July 30, 1998, however, Senate President Fernan, acting on a letter signed by seven Lakas NUCD-UMDP senators, recognized Senator Teofisto T. Guingona Jr. as the minority leader.

Challenging this action of Senate President Fernan, Senators Santiago and Tatad brought before the Supreme Court a Petition for quo warranto, alleging that in choosing Guingona as minority leader, the Senate and its officials violated Section 16 (1), Article of the Constitution.” They contended that this constitutional provision, which required the election of the Senate President “by majority vote of all its members,” carried with it a judicial duty to determine what the concepts of “majority” and “minority” were, and who may elect a minority leader.

They argued that “majority” in the aforesaid constitutional provision referred to that group of senators who (1) voted for the winning Senate President and (2) accepted committee chairmanships. Accordingly, those who voted for the losing nominee and accepted no such chairmanships comprised the “minority,” to whom the right to determine the minority leader belonged. As a result, petitioners asserted, Respondent Guingona could not be the legitimate minority leader, since he had voted for Fernan as Senate President. Furthermore, the members of the Lakas- NUCD-UMDP Party could not choose the minority leader because, having voted for Fernan and accepted committee chairmanships, they did not belong to the minority in the Senate. The Court unanimously voted to deny the Petition. Principally, it ruled that, absent any showing of a violation of the Constitution or of any act constituting grave abuse of discretion, “[c]onstitutional respect and a becoming regard for the sovereign acts of a coequal branch prevent this Court from prying into the internal workings of the Senate.

The Constitution has vested in each house of Congress the power “to determine the rules of its proceedings.” In the exercise of such prerogative, the legislature is supreme. The Supreme Court has no authority to interfere in and unilaterally intrude upon “that exclusive realm, without running afoul of constitutional principles that it is bound to protect and uphold - the very duty that justifies the Court's being.”

My ponencia went on to explain that Senators Fernan and Guingona had acted within the confines of their respective positions. “Where no provision of the Constitution, the laws or even the Rules of the Senate has been clearly shown to have been violated, disregarded or overlooked, grave abuse of discretion cannot be imputed to Senate officials for acts done within their competence and authority.”

While agreeing that the Santiago-Tatad Petition should be dismissed, three members of the Court led by Justice Vicente V. Mendoza differed from me and the majority on the reason for such dismissal. Justice Mendoza argued that the Petition should have been thrown out, not because the Senate and its officials did not commit grave abuse of discretion, but because “[t]he Court has no jurisdiction over the case. The question of who constitutes the minority in the Senate entitled to elect the minority leader of that chamber is political. It respects the internal affairs of a coequal department of the government and is thus addressed solely to that august body.”

The concluding paragraph of Justice Mendoza’s Separate Opinion summarized his position: “This case involves neither an infringement of specific constitutional limitations nor a violation of the rights of a party not a member of Congress. This Court has jurisdiction over this case only in the sense that determining whether the question involved is reserved for Congress is itself an exercise of jurisdiction in the same way that a court which dismisses a case for lack of jurisdiction must in a narrow sense have jurisdiction since it cannot dismiss the case if it were otherwise. The determination of whether the question involved is justiciable or not is in itself a process of constitutional interpretation. This is the great lesson of Marbury v. Madison in which the US Supreme Court, while affirming its power of review, in the end held itself to be without jurisdiction because the Judiciary Act of 1789 granting it jurisdiction over that case was unconstitutional, In other words, a court doing a Marbury v. Madison has no jurisdiction except to declare itself without jurisdiction over the case.”

Basic is the rule, however, that jurisdiction is determined by the allegations of the complaint or petition, and not by the arguments of the respondents or by a finding of the Court, after assuming jurisdiction, that the allegations of the complaint or petition have no merit, It is also basic that once jurisdiction is acquired, it is not lost simply because the Court has found no merit in the petition.

In the present case, the petitioners plainly alleged a violation of the Constitution, particularly Article VI, Section 16 (1). And so the ponencia states that in light of such an allegation, the Court had jurisdiction to determine whether indeed there was such a violation.

To repeat, the 1987 Constitution expressly states that judicial power includes the duty “to determine whether or not there has been a grave abuse of discretion amounting to lack or excess of jurisdiction on the part of any branch or instrumentality of the government.” Such duty impels the Court, in the present case, to determine whether there has been grave abuse of discretion on the Senate’s part. The phrase “any branch or instrumentality of the government” includes, to my mind, the two houses of Congress.

On the other hand, where the allegations of the petition or complaint show neither a prima facie violation of the Constitution or the law nor grave abuse of discretion, but merely demonstrate a violation of the internal rules of Congress, the Court may dismiss the case for lack of jurisdiction. It is in this instance where the Court, in the words of Justice Mendoza, “has no jurisdiction except to declare itself without jurisdiction over the case.”

Voting with the majority to dismiss the Petition, Justice Jose C. Vitug wrote a Separate Opinion, which quote in part:

“Pervasive and limitless, such as it may seem to be, judicial power still succumbs to the paramount doctrine of separation of powers. Congress is the branch of government, composed of the representatives of the people, that lays down the policies of government and provides the direction that the nation must take. The Executive carries out that mandate. Certainly, the Court will not negate that which is done by these co-equal and coordinate branches merely because of a perceived case of grave abuse of discretion on their part, clearly too relative a phrase to be its own sentinel against misuse, even as it will not hesitate to wield the power if that abuse becomes all too clear. The exercise of judicial statesmanship, not judicial tyranny, is what has been envisioned by and institutionalized in the 1987 Constitution.

“There is no hornbook rule by which; grave abuse of discretion may be determined. The provision was evidently couched in general terms to make it malleable to judicial interpretation in the light of any contemporary or emerging milieu. In its normal concept, the term has been said to imply capricious and whimsical exercise of judgment, amounting to lack or excess of jurisdiction, or that the power is exercised in an arbitrary or despotic manner such as by reason of passion or personal hostility. When the question, however, pertains to an affair internal to either of Congress or the Executive, would subscribe to the dictum, somewhat made implicit in my understanding of Arroyo vs. De Venecia, that unless an infringement of any specific Constitutional proscription thereby inheres the Court will not deign substitute its own judgment over that of any of the other two branches of government. Verily, in this situation, it is an impairment or a clear disregard of a specific constitutional precept or provision that can unbolt the steel door for judicial intervention.”

Justice Flerida Ruth P. Romero (now retired), who likewise voted to dismiss the Petition, wrote a Separate Opinion “to render fealty to certain fundamental precepts,” as follows:

“Last year, Arroyo, et al. v. de Venecia, et al.“ presented an opportunity for me to clarify my position further. In that case, Congressman Joker Arroyo filed a petition before the Court complaining that during a session [of] the House of Representatives, he was effectively prevented from raising the question of quorum which to him tainted the validity of Republic Act No. 8240 or the so-called ‘sin-taxes’ law. The Court, speaking again through Justice Mendoza, dismissed Mr. Arroyo's petition, arguing in the main that courts are denied the power to inquire into allegations that, in enacting a law, a House of Congress failed to comply with its own rules, in the absence of [any] showing that there was a violation of a constitutional provision or the rights of private individuals. Concurring with the majority opinion, I discerned a need to explain my position then because of possible misinterpretation. was very emphatic that I did not abandon my position in Tolentino, the facts as presented in Arroyo being radically different from the former. In keeping with my view that judicial review is permissible only to uphold the Constitution, I pointed out that the legislative rules allegedly violated were purely internal and had no direct or reasonable nexus to the requirements and proscriptions of the Constitution in the passage of a bill which would otherwise warrant the Court’s intervention.”

Neither Justice Vitug nor Justice Romero, however, discussed the issue of jurisdiction.

By voting that the Court had jurisdiction over the Petition, the majority asserted the exercise of the Court's constitutional right and duty “to determine whether or not there has been a grave abuse of discretion amounting to lack or excess of jurisdiction on the part of any branch or instrumentality of government.” Thus, even in internal affairs of Congress, the Court may still assume jurisdiction if the petition contains prima facie allegations of violation of the Constitution. Had the majority voted with Mr. Justice Mendoza, the Court would have given the signal that, even if a petition seriously alleges facts showing constitutional violations or grave abuse of discretion, never will it pass upon the internal acts of the legislative on the ground of lack of jurisdiction.

## Summary

Chapter 14 of 'Leadership by Example: The Davide Standard' used CJP's own ponencia in Santiago v. Guingona to explain when the Supreme Court may look into the internal acts of Congress. He began with the Court's role as guardian of the Constitution and conscience of society, and with the limit on that role: it will strike down acts of the political departments only for unconstitutionality or grave abuse of discretion, never for want of wisdom or propriety. The case arose when the Senate elected Marcelo B. Fernan its President on July 27, 1998 by a vote of 20 to 2, Senator Francisco S. Tatad, the losing nominee supported only by Senator Miriam Defensor Santiago, claimed the minority leadership, and Fernan instead recognized Teofisto T. Guingona Jr. on the strength of a letter from seven Lakas-NUCD-UMDP senators. Santiago and Tatad sued in quo warranto, arguing that Article VI, Section 16 (1) required the Court to define 'majority' and 'minority' and that Guingona, having voted for Fernan, could not lead the minority. The Court unanimously denied the petition, holding that absent any showing of a constitutional violation or grave abuse, constitutional respect for a coequal branch forbade prying into the Senate's internal workings, and that Fernan and Guingona had acted within their competence. Three Justices led by Vicente V. Mendoza would have dismissed for lack of jurisdiction, calling the composition of the Senate minority a political question and invoking Marbury v. Madison for the proposition that such a court has no jurisdiction except to declare itself without jurisdiction. CJP answered that jurisdiction is determined by the allegations of the petition, is not lost when the petition proves meritless, and extends to Congress because the Constitution's grave abuse clause covers 'any branch or instrumentality of the government.' He conceded that where a petition shows only a breach of internal rules the Court may indeed dismiss for want of jurisdiction, and he reproduced the Separate Opinions of Justice Jose C. Vitug, who urged judicial statesmanship rather than judicial tyranny and said only a clear disregard of a specific constitutional precept can unbolt the door to intervention, and of Justice Flerida Ruth P. Romero, who reaffirmed her position in Arroyo v. De Venecia. Neither, he noted, addressed jurisdiction -- and had the majority followed Mendoza, the Court would have announced that it will never review internal legislative acts, however grave the allegations.
