# Love God, Serve Man -- Ch. 6: Does the MNLF Have International Legal Personality?

(Editor's note: This was a legal opinion rendered by Atty. Artemio V. Panganiban at the request of former Ambassador to Germany and then Manila Bulletin columnist Melchor P. Aquino. The opinion was printed in full in the Manila Bulletin [January 14, 1987). Ambassador Aquino's comments which came out in the same issue of the Bulletin are likewise reproduced here.)

## Brief Factual Background

A few days after Minister Aquilino Pimentel, head of the Philippine panel negotiating with the MNLF, announced that "the government prefers that all talks be held in the Philippines, not in the Middle East" (Manila Bulletin, Dec. 25, 1986), he unceremoniously left for Jeddah, Saudi Arabia, on Dec. 30, 1986, precisely to meet there with his MNLF counterparts. He was accompanied by panel members Agapito "Butz" Aquino and Norberto Gonzales, and Deputy Minister of Local Government Saidamen Pangarungan. This meeting in Jeddah "is in accordance with a ceasefire agreement signed in a Jolo, Sulu, convent a few months ago." (Bulletin, Dec. 31, 1986).

On Jan. 5, 1987, the newspapers reported that an agreement between the government and the MNLF was signed on Jan. 3, 1987 in Jeddah, "under the auspices of the 46-member Organization of Islamic Conference," whereby "the Muslim group dropped its demand for full independence and agreed to negotiate autonomy," which according to MNLF chairman Nur Misuari would mean a 'state within a state." Both sides agreed to continue discussion on whether to grant full autonomy to five groups of islands in the Philippines, namely, Mindanao, Sulu, Basilan, Tawi-Tawi and Palawan.

## Query

Our legal opinion was sought by a Bulletin columnist, Ambassador Melchor Aquino, on this question: "What are the effects in international law of the agreement by the Philippine government to meet with representatives of the MNLF in a third country (Saudi Arabia) under the auspices of an association of 46 governments/states of which the Philippines is not a member (Organization of the Islamic Conference)?"

## Answer

In answer to an earlier query posed by Ambassador Aquino, (see Bulletin, Dec. 17, 1986) we opined that, on the basis of generally accepted principles of international law, the MNLF (and the NDF, for that matter) did not have a status of "belligerency." This opinion is subject to the caveat that "since there is no purely legal right to be recognized, the question as to whether belligerency may be accorded recognition is a matter of policy on the part of the recognizing state or government." 1 In other words, such recognition may be given at the discretion of a sovereign third state, and being political in character, recognition can hardly be questioned in an international legal tribunal.

It is in this light that the query assumes relevance. Otherwise stated, by its acquiescence to confer with the MNLF in Saudi Arabia under the auspices of the Organization of the Islamic Conference, is the Philippine government giving other states some grounds to accord recognition to the MNLF as a belligerent community or as some other "subject" of international law?

To be sure, this is not the first time that the Philippines as a sovereign state has agreed to negotiate with the MNLF in a foreign country "under the auspices," if not sponsorship, of the OIC. The Tripoli Agreement of 1977 was concluded in Libya by the Marcos government under such circumstances. In other words, a refusal by the present government to negotiate in Jeddah under the auspices of OIC would not have prevented third states from using these excuses as political justifications for recognition. However, by its agreement to meet in a third country, the present government is estopped from denouncing such acts of the previous regime.

It is difficult to think that Great Britain would agree to negotiate with the Irish Republican Army in Italy or Spain under the auspices of an association of Catholic states, as it is equally difficult to imagine India consenting to meet with Sikh separatists in Nepal or another third state, under the sponsorship of an association of states of which it is not a member. Be that as it may, the Philippines - both under the old and new regime - has made this thought rather academic.

Traditionally, "only sovereign states can be regarded as the subjects of international law." Hence, in the days of Grotius who is generally regarded as the father of international law, only states had "international personality" - that is, only states had rights and duties under international law." In the course of time, this concept has changed and other entities have been vested with or have come to possess international personality even if "incomplete, imperfect, qualified or quasi."3 The International Court of Justice recognized this in an advisory opinion in Reparations for Injuries Suffered in the Service of the United States. It said that, "(T)hroughout its history, the development of international law has been influenced by the requirements of international life, and the progressive increase in the collective activities of States has already given rise to instances of action upon the international plane by certain entities which are not states."

Currently, international law is fast evolving to meet fast-changing events. International law is not imposed by a political superior upon its subjects. It is derived not from any particular legislation, but its source is largely the collective acts or agreements of states.

During the last 50 years, more than 100 new states have emerged and have challenged traditional international law doctrines which run counter to their interest. New principles have thus evolved. Whereas before, intervention in purely domestic affairs was absolutely illegal, now customary international law has allowed exceptions. For instance, "when a State renders itself guilty of cruelties or persecutions of its own materials to the extent of denying them fundamental human rights, intervention in the interest of humanity is permissible. In 1971, India used large-scale military forces in Pakistan, on the allegation that said state was violating minimal standards of human rights in East Bengal by killing and imprisoning a large number of the population, causing mass flights of refugees to India."

In summary, we submit that the agreement by the government to meet and thereafter to sign an accord with the MNLF in Saudi Arabia under the auspices of the OIC did not substantially alter our earlier opinion that, under traditional international law, a status of belligerency has not been accorded to the Muslim insurgents.

However, in the light of fast developments in international law, we frown upon these acts of acquiescing to meet in a place outside the territory of the Philippines, especially under the auspices, if not sponsorship, of the Organization of the Islamic Conference. They could provide excuses, if not justifications, to vest "quasi" international personality in the MNLF, not necessarily belligerency status, but enough to grant it international rights and warrant intervention by the members of the Islamic Conference in what otherwise would have been clearly a domestic affair. By their sheer number - an awesome 46 states - the Islamic Conference members could indeed by agreement spawn new principles of international law and thus justify their recognition of the MNLF and eventual direct intervention in the problem. Let it be remembered that recognition is a political act not subject to judicial review by international legal tribunals.

We are not privy to the reasons behind our government's consenting to meet in Jeddah. And the fact that the MNLF has agreed to abandon its demand for independence and to negotiate merely for "full autonomy" or for a "state within a state" does not minimize, much less excuse, the role of third states (Saudi Arabia) and organizations (OIC) in the problem. On the contrary, it gives them a more permanent, if not more important, role in finding a solution to our Muslim insurgency problem in the future.

This is not to say that we blame the present government for its current quest for peace. For indeed, if it succeeds in its attempt to settle this very difficult and delicate problem, then all this brinkmanship would have been worth the exercise. If it fails, then it will have the satisfaction that it exhausted all avenues to peace, even to the extent of endangering its international law posture. Indeed, peace must be pursued regardless of legal and political niceties that, anyway, its opponents would invoke whether they are used or not.

## Summary

Chapter 6 of 'Love God, Serve Man' reproduces the legal opinion Atty. Artemio V. Panganiban rendered at the request of former Ambassador to Germany and Manila Bulletin columnist Melchor P. Aquino, printed in full in the Bulletin on January 14, 1987. The facts he sets out are that Minister Aquilino Pimentel, head of the Philippine panel negotiating with the MNLF, had announced that the government preferred all talks to be held in the Philippines and not the Middle East, yet left unceremoniously for Jeddah on December 30, 1986 with panel members Agapito 'Butz' Aquino and Norberto Gonzales and Deputy Minister Saidamen Pangarungan, pursuant to a ceasefire signed in a Jolo convent; on January 3, 1987 an agreement was signed under the auspices of the 46-member Organization of the Islamic Conference, the MNLF dropping its demand for full independence and agreeing to negotiate autonomy that chairman Nur Misuari said would mean a 'state within a state' for Mindanao, Sulu, Basilan, Tawi-Tawi and Palawan. Asked what the international-law effects of meeting in a third country under such auspices were, Panganiban restated his earlier opinion that neither the MNLF nor the NDF had a status of belligerency, subject to the caveat that there is no purely legal right to be recognized, so recognition is discretionary, political, and hardly reviewable in an international tribunal. He observed that the practice was not new -- the Tripoli Agreement of 1977 was concluded in Libya by the Marcos government -- so that refusal would have prevented nothing and the present government is estopped from denouncing the prior regime, though he doubted Britain would meet the IRA, or India Sikh separatists, on comparable terms. Tracing the doctrine from Grotius, when only states had international personality, through the ICJ's Reparations advisory opinion recognizing action by entities that are not states, he stressed that international law derives from the collective acts of states and has evolved -- more than 100 new states in fifty years, and exceptions now allowing humanitarian intervention, as with India in East Bengal in 1971. He concluded that belligerency had not been accorded, but frowned upon acquiescing to meet outside Philippine territory under OIC auspices, warning that 46 states could by agreement spawn new principles vesting 'quasi' personality in the MNLF and warranting intervention in a domestic affair. Still, he refused to blame the government, holding that peace must be pursued regardless of legal and political niceties.

## Notable Anecdotes

### Anecdote 1

The unceremonious departure: he sets the scene with Pimentel announcing that the government prefers all talks be held in the Philippines and not the Middle East, then leaving for Jeddah days later on December 30, 1986.

### Anecdote 2

The Jolo convent: the Jeddah meeting is traced to a ceasefire agreement signed a few months earlier in a convent in Jolo, Sulu.

### Anecdote 3

The IRA and Sikh hypotheticals: he tests the propriety of the venue by imagining Great Britain negotiating with the Irish Republican Army in Italy or Spain under an association of Catholic states, and India meeting Sikh separatists in Nepal under a body it does not belong to.

### Anecdote 4

The East Bengal precedent: he cites India's 1971 use of large-scale military force in Pakistan, justified by allegations of killings, imprisonment and mass refugee flights, as proof that the absolute bar on intervention in domestic affairs has given way.
