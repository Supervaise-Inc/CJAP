# Chapter 20: Romualdez v. Sandiganbayan: Constitutionality of the Anti-Graft Law

In Estrada v. Sandiganbayan, former President Joseph E. Estrada — through his lawyers — challenged the constitutionality of the Anti-Plunder Law on the ground that, “on its face,” the law was allegedly “vague” and “overbroad.” While the Court denied the Petition and declared the law constitutional, its Decision did not meet head on the Estrada argument that the whole Anti-Plunder Law should be set aside on the “void for vagueness” ground.

The ponencia did cite, however, the Separate Concurring Opinion of Justice Vicente V. Mendoza, who had squarely debunked this novel argument. He opined that the overbreadth and vagueness doctrines had special application only to free speech cases and could not be used to invalidate criminal laws.

The ponencia written by Justice Bellosillo and the Separate Opinion of Justice Mendoza were concurred in by Chief Justice Davide; and Justices Melo, Puno, Vitug and Quisumbing. I wrote a Separate Concurring Opinion. Justices Kapunan, Pardo, Santiago and Gutierrez dissented. Justice Carpio took no part because he had been one of the complainants against Estrada while the case was being investigated by the Office of the Ombudsman.

Even if the Estrada Petition was dismissed by the Court through a 10-4-1 vote, some constitutionalists still opine that because the ponencia did not directly take up the on-its-face challenge hurled by Estrada, but merely cited the Mendoza Opinion, the issue was still open to debate.

The opportunity to close this debate and to rule on whether the void-for-vagueness doctrine might be applied to penal laws presented itself in Romualdez v. Sandiganbayan.

## The Facts

Alfredo T. Romualdez, a brother-in-law of former President Ferdinand E. Marcos was charged by the Presidential Commission on Good Government (PCGG) on July 12, 1989, with violation of Section 5 of Republic Act No. 3019, the Anti-Graft Law.

On December 27, 1996, he filed his first “MOTION TO DISMISS AND TO DEFER ARRAIGNMENT,” claiming that no valid preliminary investigation had been conducted. On January 9, 1997, the Sandiganbayan (First Division) issued an Order directing him to file a Motion for Reinvestigation with the Office of the Special Prosecutor.

Romualdez questioned the Order before the Supreme Court via a Petition for Certiorari and Prohibition. On January 21, 1998, the Court dismissed the Petition for failure to show that the Sandiganbayan had committed grave abuse of discretion in issuing the assailed Order.

Romualdez then filed a Motion to Quash with the Office of the Special Prosecutor, which subsequently recommended the dismissal of the case. However, Ombudsman Aniano A. Desierto disagreed and directed the prosecutors to let Romualdez present his evidence in Court.

Consequently, Romualdez filed on October 8, 1999 his second “MOTION TO QUASH AND TO DEFER ARRAIGNMENT,” which the Sandiganbayan denied for lack of merit.

On June 19, 2001, he filed a “MOTION FOR LEAVE TO FILE MOTION TO DISMISS.” This time, the Sandiganbayan admitted the Motion as well as the attached (third) Motion to Dismiss.

## The Ruling of the Sandiganbayan

In disposing of the (third) Motion to Dismiss filed by Romualdez, the Sandiganbayan explained that all the grounds he had invoked, except the third one, had already been raised by him and passed upon in its previous Resolutions.’ In resolving the third ground, it pointed out that Section 17 of the 1973 Constitution had become effective only in 1981 when the basic law was amended. Since his alleged crime had been committed in or about 1975, the amended provision was inapplicable to him.°;

In denying the Motion for Reconsideration, the Sandiganbayan further ruled that his right to a preliminary investigation had not been violated, because he had already been granted a reinvestigation. It also held that his right to be informed of the nature and cause of the accusation had not been trampled upon, inasmuch as the Information had set forth the essential elements of the offense charged.

## Issues

The issues were as follows: (1) whether Section 5 of Republic Act 3019 was unconstitutional: (2) whether the Information was vague; (3) whether there was a valid preliminary investigation; (4) whether the criminal action or liability was extinguished by prescription; and (5) whether petitioner was immune from criminal prosecution under then Section 17 of Article VII of the 1973 Constitution.

## The Court’s Ruling

## Repetitive Motions Prohibited

Petitioner challenged the constitutionality of Section 5 of RA 3019 for the first time in the Sandiganbayan through a Supplemental Motion to Dismiss, which was attached to his December 7, 2001 Motion for Reconsideration of the Order that had denied his original Motion to Dismiss. In effect, the said Supplemental Motion was his third motion to quash.’ The instant Petition for Certiorari challenged the denial of his original, not his Supplemental, Motion to Dismiss. By filing a Motion to Dismiss, Romualdez submitted in effect a prohibited second motion to quash.

Besides, there is no substantial distinction between a motion to quash and a motion to dismiss — both pray for an identical relief, which is the dismissal of the case, The Rules abhor repetitive motions. Furthermore, a party is not permitted to raise issues, whether similar or differ ent, by installment.

If only for the foregoing procedural lapses, the Petition deserved to be dismissed outright. Given the importance of the case in curtailing graft and corruption, the Court nevertheless addressed the other issues on their merit.

## Constitutionality of Section 5 of Republic Act 3019

Republic Act 3019, a penal statute, on the ground that the act constituting the offense was allegedly vague or impermissibly broad.

The Court stressed at the outset that the over-breadth and the vagueness doctrines had special application only to free-speech cases. Indeed, these doctrines are not appropriate for testing the validity of penal statutes. “To this date, the Court has not declared any penal law unconstitutional on the ground of ambiguilty.”

As conduct — not speech — was its object, the challenged provision should be examined only “as applied” to the defendant (Romualdez) and should not be declared unconstitutional for overbreadth or vagueness.

The questioned provision reads as follows:

“Section 5. Prohibition on certain relatives — It shall be unlawful for the spouse or for any relative, by consanguinity or affinity, within the third civil degree, of the President of the Philippines, the Vice-President of the Philippines, the President of the Senate, or the Speaker of the House of Representatives, to intervene, directly or indirectly, in any business, transaction, contract or application with the Government: Provided, That this section shall not apply to any person who, prior to the assumption of office of any of the above officials to whom he is related, has been already dealing with the Government along the same line of business, nor to any transaction, con- — tract or application already existing or pending at the time of such assumption of public office, nor to any application filed by him the approval of which is not discretionary on the part of the official or officials concerned but depends upon compliance with requisites provided by law, or rules or regulations issued pursuant to law, nor to any act lawfully performed in an official capacity or in the exercise of a profession.”

Romualdez argued that the phrase “to intervene directly or indirectly, in any business, transaction, contract or application with the Government” was vague and violated his right to be informed of the cause and nature of the accusation against him."

The Court disagreed.

It held that every statute was to be presumed valid. On the party challenging its validity weighed heavily the onerous task of rebutting this presumption. Romualdez miserably failed to overcome such presumption.

Dans v. People decreed that there was nothing vague about a penal law that adequately answered the basic query “What is the violation?” Anything beyond — the hows and the whys — were evidentiary matters that the law itself could not possibly disclose, in view of the uniqueness of every case."

The question “What is the violation?” was sufficiently answered by Section 5 of RA 3019, as follows:

“1. ‘The offender is a spouse or any relative by consanguinity or affinity within the third civil degree of the President of the Philippines, the Vice-President of the Philippines, the President of the Senate, or the Speaker of the House of Representatives; and

“2. The offender intervened directly or indirectly in any business, transaction, contract or application with the government.”

Romualdez further complained that the provision had not specified what acts were punishable under the term intervene and thus transgressed his right to be presumed innocent. The Court agreed with the Office of the Solicitor General that the word could easily be understood through simple statutory construction. Verily, the absence of a statutory definition of a term used in a statute will not render a law void for vagueness, if the meaning can be determined through the judicial tion of construction. Elementary is the principle t words should be construed in their ordinary and meaning.

The term intervene, in its ordinary acceptation, means “to come between.” Criminally liable is anyone covered in the enumeration of Section 5 of RA 3019 — any person who intervenes in any manner in any bust ness, transaction, contract or application with the ernment. The courts may pass upon the details of how such acts of intervention can be performed once trial is concluded. Thus, the alleged vagueness of intervene in this case was not a ground to quash the Information prior to the commencement of the trial.

## Allegedly Vague Information

Petitioner further contended that the Information itself was also unconstitutionally vague, because it did not specify the acts of intervention that he had supposedly performed.” Again, the Court disagreed.

When allegations in the information are vague or indefinite, the remedy of the accused is not a motion to quash, but a motion for a bill of particulars. The Rules merely require the information to describe the offense with sufficient particularity as to apprise the accused of what they are being charged with and to enable the court to pronounce judgment.

A cursory reading of the Information in the instant case showed that the elements of a violation of Section 5 of RA 3019 had been stated sufficiently. Likewise, the allegations described the offense committed by Romualdez with such particularity as to enable him to prepare an intelligent defense.

## Preliminary Investigation

Romualdez cited Cojuangco v. PCGG to support the quashal of the Information against him. The Court clarified that the PCGG, having initiated the present Complaint against him, could not properly conduct the preliminary investigation. Nonetheless, the Sandiganbayan had already suspended the trial and afforded him a reinvestigation by the Ombudsman. The procedure outlined in Cojuangco had thus been followed.

The Sandiganbayan’s actions were in accord also with Raro v. Sandiganbayan, according to which the failure to conduct a valid preliminary investigation would not warrant the quashal of an information. If the information had already been filed, the proper procedure was for the Sandiganbayan to hold the trial in abeyance while the preliminary investigation was being conducted or completed.

## Prescription

The issue of prescription should be disregarded 4 this stage, since Romualdez failed to challenge the Sandiganbayan ruling debunking his Motion to Quash within the 60-day period for the filing of a petition for certiorari.

Act No. 3326, as amended,” governs the prescription of offenses penalized by special laws. Its pertinent provision reads:

“Sec. 2. Prescription shall begin to run from the day of the commission of the violation of the law, and if the same not be known at the time, from the discovery thereof and the institution of judicial proceedings for its investigation and punishment.

“The prescription shall be interrupted when proceedings are instituted against the guilty person, and shall begin to run again if the proceedings are dismissed for reasons not constituting jeopardy.”

Consistent with the above provision, this Court had previously reckoned the prescriptive period of cases involving RA 3019 (committed prior to the February 1986 EDSA Revolution) from the discovery of the violation.”

Prior to February 1986, no person was expected to have seriously dared question the legality of the sale or would even have thought of investigating the petitioner's alleged involvement in the transaction. It was only after the creation of PCGG and its exhaustive investigations that the alleged crime was discovered. This led to the initiation on November 29, 1988 of a Complaint against former President Marcos and petitioner for violation of the Anti-Graft and Corrupt Practices Act. Consequently, the filing of the Information on July 12, 1989, was well within the prescriptive period of ten years from the discovery of the offense.

## Immunity from Prosecution

Relying on Section 17 of Article VII of the 1973 Constitution, as amended, Romualdez further argued that he enjoyed derivative immunity, because he had allegedly served as a high-ranking naval officer — specifically, as naval aide-de-camp — of former President Marcos.

The Court ruled that the provision was not applicable to Romualdez because the immunity amendment became effective only in 1981, while the alleged crime happened in 1975.

In Estrada v. Desierto, this Court exhaustively traced the origin of executive immunity in order to determine the extent of its applicability and held that such immunity applied only during the incumbency of a President. It could not be used to shield a non-sitting President from prosecution for alleged criminal acts done while in office. The reasoning of the petitioner was therefore misplaced, since he was deriving his immunity from one who was no longer sitting as President.

In sum, Romualdez utterly failed to show that the Sandiganbayan had gravely abused its discretion in issuing the assailed Resolutions. Thus, the Court DISMISSED his Petition and AFFIRMED the questioned Resolutions of the Sandiganbayan.

## The Separate Opinion of Justice Tinga

Justice Dante O. Tinga concurred “in the result” of the ponencia that the Romualdez Petition should be dismissed, and that Section 5 of the Anti-Plunder Law was constitutional. However, he raised serious reservations on the ponencia’s holding that the void-for-vagueness doctrine had special application only to free speech cases,” and that penal laws could not be stricken down on the ground of ambiguity.”

According to him, the pronouncement in Estrada that penal statutes could not be challenged on the ground of vagueness did not form part of the ratio decidendi, The ratio, in the words of Justice Bellosillo, was that “fall it is written, the [Anti-]Plunder Law contains ascertainable standards and well-defined parameters which would enable the accused to determine the nature of his violation.” Thus, Section 5 could not be considered unconstitutional. The discussion on the vagueness aspect was not decisive of the main issue and was, therefore, clear obiter dictum. It would be erroneous to resolve the Petition on the basis of that orbiter in Estrada.

Justice Tinga emphasized that the Bill of Rights occupied a position of primacy in the fundamental law.” Sacrosanct in this jurisdiction, therefore, was the principle that no person should be deprived of life, liberty or property without due process of law.”

The Separate Opinion also pointed out that, when premised on the argument that the law was vague, a challenge to a penal statute was a proper invocation of the due process clause. A statute that was so lacking in comprehensible standards that men of common intelligence must necessarily have to guess at its meaning and differ as to its application violated the due process clause, for failure to accord persons fair notice of the conduct to avoid.

Justice Tinga further opined that the Bill of Rights likewise guaranteed that no person should be held to answer for a criminal offense without due process of law, and that the accused enjoyed the right to be informed of the nature and cause of the accusation against them.” If a particular mode of constitutional challenge — such as one predicated on the void-for-vagueness doctrine — was available to an ordinary person deprived of property or a means of expression, then more so should it be accessible to one who was in jeopardy of being deprived of liberty or life."

While contesting the Court’s ruling in regard to the void for vagueness doctrine, Justice ‘Tinga nonetheless concurred in the other parts of the Decision. Justices Puno, Santiago and Gutierrez concurred only “in the result.” Since Justices Santiago and Gutierrez dissented in Estrada, I surmise that their “in the result’ vote showed a reservation also in regard to the void-for-vagueness discussion.
