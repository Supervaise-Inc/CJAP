# Transparency, Unanimity & Diversity -- Ch. 18: Secretary of Justice v. Lantion and Jimenez: Due Process in Extradition Cases

On November 13, 1994, the Philippines and the United States signed an Extradition Treaty, which the Philippine Senate later ratified. Pursuant to this Agreement, the US-via Note Verbale No. 0522 requested on June 18, 1999, the extradition of Mark Jimenez. He had been charged in the United States with violation of the US Code (USC), specifically “(1) conspiracy to commit an offense or to defraud the US; (2) attempt to evade or defeat tax; (3) fraud by wire, radio or television; (4) false statements or entries and (5) election contributions in [the] name of another.”

On the same day (June 18, 1999), the secretary of justice created a panel of government lawyers to evaluate the request. It found that “the official English translation of some documents in Spanish were not attached to the request and that there [were] some other matters that needed to be addressed.” Hence, it asked the US government to correct these inadequacies.

In the meantime, the intended extraditee, Mark Jimenez, through Counsel Estelito P. Mendoza in a letter dated July 1, 1999, asked the secretary of justice for copies of the official request for his extradition, as well as other documents in connection therewith, and to be given ample time to comment on them. The request was however denied.

Thus, on August 6, 1999, Jimenez filed a suit with the Regional Trial Court of Manila to compel the secretaries of justice and of foreign affairs and the director of the National Bureau of Investigation to give him copies of the extradition documents and an opportunity to comment on them. After a hearing, then Manila RTC Judge Ralph C. Lantion (now a commissioner of the Commission on Elections), to whom the case had been raffled, ordered the three government officials to “maintain the status quo”; that is, to refrain from conducting further proceedings on the extradition request.

Disagreeing with the trial court, the secretary of justice brought the matter to the Supreme Court with this basic question: Was Mark Jimenez entitled to be given copies of the extradition documents and to comment on them, before the executive department could continue evaluating them?

Before answering the question, I should explain that there are two phases in an extradition proceeding: first, a preliminary or an evaluation stage, during which the executive authority of the requested state ascertains whether the extradition request is supported by the documents and the information required under the Extradition Treaty; and second, the extradition hearing, during which the executive authority, after determining that the extradition request is properly supported by the required documents, files the extradition charge before a court of justice, which in turn determines whether the wanted person should be extradited. The first is essentially an executive determination, while the second is a judicial process.

More precisely then, the question in Secretary of Justice v. Lantion and Jimenez! was as follows: During the evaluation stage undertaken by the executive department, was Mark Jimenez entitled to be given copies of the extradition documents sent by the US government to our secretary of foreign affairs? Otherwise stated, the issue was whether an extraditee was entitled to the due process rights of notice and hearing during the preliminary or evaluation phase of an extradition proceeding.

- By a vote of 9-6, the Supreme Court, in a 40-page Decision penned by Justice Jose A.R. Melo,? answered the question in the affirmative: the extraditee was entitled to be heard, even during the initial or preliminary stage, for the following reasons:

1. The initial evaluation process “may result in the deprivation of liberty of the prospective extraditee. This deprivation can be effected at two stages: First, the provisional arrest of the prospective extraditee

Logically, although the Extradition Law is silent on this respect, the provisions only mean that once a request is forwarded to the requested state, the prospective extraditee may be continuously detained, or if not, subsequently rearrested (Paragraph 5, Article 9, RP- US Extradition Treaty), for he will only be discharged if no request is submitted. Practically, the purpose of this detention is to prevent his possible flight from the requested state. Second, the temporary arrest of the prospective extraditee during the pendency of the extradition petition in court (Section 6, Presidential Decree No. 1069).”

2. The “evaluation procedure is akin to a preliminary investigation since both procedures may have the same result — the arrest and imprisonment of the respondent or the person charged. Similar to the evaluation stage of extradition proceedings, a preliminary investigation, which may result in the filing of an information against the respondent, can possibly lead to his arrest, and to the deprivation of his liberty.”

3. The “basic rights of notice and hearing pervade not only in criminal and civil proceedings, but in administrative proceedings as well. Non-observance of these rights will invalidate the proceedings. Individuals are entitled to be notified of any pending case affecting their interests, and upon notice, they may claim the right to appear therein and present their side and to refute the position of the opposing parties.”

Justice Melo elaborated this point thus: “In a preliminary investigation which is an administrative investigatory proceeding, Section 3, Rule 112 of the Rules of Court guarantees the respondent's basic due process rights, granting him the right to be furnished a copy of the complaint, the affidavits, and other supporting documents, and the right to submit counter-affidavits and other supporting documents within ten days from receipt thereof. Moreover, the respondent shall have the right to examine all other evidence submitted by the complainant.”

4, Jimenez was entitled to the constitutional right to information? on matters of public concern, especially because the extradition documents directly and personally affected him and his rights.

5. Neither the RP-US Extradition Treaty nor the Extradition Law (PD 1069) precluded the “twin due process rights of notice and hearing.” Similarly, American jurisprudence posed no such proscription. “In fact, in interstate extradition proceedings x x x, the prospective extraditee may even request x x x copies of the extradition documents from the governor of the asylum state, and if he does, his rights to be supplied the same becomes a demandable right,” the ponente emphasized.

6. The granting of Jimenez’s request would not violate the RP-US Extradition Treaty or any norm of international law. Quite the contrary, such grant was “in keeping with the principles of democracy on which our Constitution is premised.”

Justice Melo ended his ponencia with a brief gene call for freedom. In his words:

“Verily, as one traverses treacherous waters of conflicting and opposing currents of liberty and government authority, he must ever hold the oar of freedom in the stronger arm, lest an errant and wayward course be laid.”

Justice Puno and I separately wrote Dissenting Opinions. In my Dissent, I explained:

1. In the context of the peculiar factual milieu of Jimenez, there was no danger that he would be deprived of his “life, liberty or property.” Only when a person is in danger of losing it does the Constitution guarantee due process.*

As will be presently explained, this due process clause had no relevance to his case, because his “life, liberty or property” was not in jeopardy during the preliminary stage of his extradition proceedings.

On the other hand, Justice Melo’s ponencia held that Jimenez, even during the preliminary stage, could be provisionally arrested pursuant to Article 9 of the RP-US Extradition Treaty, which I quote below: “In case of urgency, a Contracting Party may request the provisional arrest of the person sought pending presentation of the request for extradition. A request for provisional arrest may be transmitted through the diplomatic channel or directly between the Philippine Department of Justice and the United States Department of Justice.”

x x x

Answering this point, I contended that the foregoing Article of the Treaty “is not automatically operative at all times, and its enforcement does not depend solely on the discretion of the requested state. From the wordings of the provision itself, there are at least three requisites: (1) there must be an urgency, and (2) there is a corresponding request (3) which must be made prior to the presentation of the request for extradition.”

I added that “[i]n the instant case, there appears to be no urgency characterizing the nature of the extradition of private respondent. Petitioner does not claim any such urgency. There is no request from the United States for the provisional arrest of Mark Jimenez either. And the secretary of justice stated during the Oral Argument that he had no intention of applying for the provisional arrest of private respondent.> Finally, the formal request for extradition had already been made; therefore, provisional arrest was not likely, as it should really come before the extradition request.°” In sum, Jimenez was not in danger at all of being arrested or detained during the evaluation stage of the extradition proceedings. 5 TSN, p. 76. ® See also TSN, p. 30.

2. The evaluation stage was not adjudicative in nature. It was essentially executive and ministerial in character. It “simply involves the ascertainment by the foreign affairs secretary of whether the extradition request is accompanied by the documents stated in paragraphs 2 and 3, Article 7 of the Treaty, relating to the identity and the probable location of the fugitive; the facts of the offense and the procedural history of the case; provisions of the law describing the essential elements of the offense charged and the punishment therefor; its prescriptive period; such evidence as would provide probable cause for the arrest and the committal for trial of the fugitive; and copies of the warrant or order of arrest and the charging document. The foreign affairs secretary also sees to it that these accompanying documents have been certified by the principal diplomatic or consular officer of the Philippines in the United States, and that they are in the English language or have English translations. Pursuant to Article 3 of the Treaty, he also determines whether the request is politically motivated, and whether the offense charged is a military offense not punishable under non-military penal legislation.”” In fine, I stressed:

“Upon a finding of the secretary of foreign affairs that the extradition request and its supporting documents are sufficient and complete in form and substance, he shall deliver the same to the justice secretary, who shall immediately designate and authorize an attorney in his office to take charge of the case. The lawyer designated shall then file a written petition with the proper regional trial court, with a prayer that the court take the extradition request under consideration.*”

x x x

“It must be borne in mind that during the preliminary stage, the foreign affairs secretary’s determination of whether the offense charged is extraditable or politically motivated is merely preliminary. The same issue will be resolved by the trial court.’ Moreover, it is also the power and the duty of the court, not the executive authority, to determine whether there is sufficient evidence to establish probable cause that the extraditee committed the crimes charged." The sufficiency of the evidence of criminality is to be determined based on the laws of the requested state.’ Private Respondent Jimenez will, therefore, definitely have his full opportunity before the court, in case an extradition petition will indeed be filed, to be heard on all issues including the sufficiency of the documents supporting the extradition request.”

From the foregoing process, it is clear there was really nothing that the prospective extraditee could genuinely comment on, since his rights were not yet affected.

3. The remedy which Jimenez should have resorted to, in order to alleviate the alleged “incalculable prejudice” that had been brought upon him by the extradition request, was to fight the charges directly in the US Courts. “The concrete charges that he has allegedly committed certain offenses already exist. These charges have been filed in the United States and are part of public and official records there. Assuming the existence of moral injury, the only means by which he can restore his good reputation is to prove before the proper judicial authorities in the US that the charges against him are unfounded. Such restoration cannot be accomplished by simply contending that the documents supporting the request for his extradition are insufficient.”

On the other hand, Justice Puno began his scholarly Dissent with a “brief review of the history of extradition law,” traversing various schools of thought in international law, from the early practices of the Egyptians and the Chinese, to Grotius and Vattel, thence to Kelsen, later to Mc Dougall and to our own Florentino Feliciano — all these to emphasize that the executive department should be given great latitude in dealing with extradition as an instrument of foreign policy. He proposed a “balancing approach” between international obligations and our Constitution and concluded that Jimenez had not proven his entitlement to due process. Indeed, “the degree of denial of private respondent’s rights to due process and to information is too slight to warrant the interposition of judicial power.” He argued that an extradition proceeding could not be likened to a criminal case. In his words: “x x x, As admitted in the ponencia itself, an extradition proceeding is sui generis. It is, thus, futile to determine what it is. What is certain is that it is not a criminal proceeding where there is an accused who can claim the entire array of rights guaranteed by the Bill of Rights. Let it be stressed that in an extradition proceeding, there is no accused and the guilt or innocence of the extraditee will not be passed upon by our executive officials nor by the extradition judge. Hence, constitutional rights that are only relevant to determine the guilt or innocence of an accused cannot be invoked by an extraditee. Indeed, an extradition proceeding is summary in nature which is untrue of criminal proceedings.’* Even the rules of evidence are different in an extradition proceeding. Admission of evidence is less stringent, again because the guilt of the extraditee is not under litigation.“It is not only the quality but even the quantum of evidence in [an] extradition proceeding [that] is different. In a criminal case, an accused can only be convicted by proof beyond reasonable doubt.'5 In an extradition proceeding, an extraditee can be ordered extradited “upon showing of the existence of a prima facie case.” If more need be said, the nature of an extradition decision is different from a judicial decision whose finality cannot be changed by executive fiat. Our courts’ may hold an individual extraditable but the ultimate decision to extradite the individual lies in the hands of the Executive. Section 3, Article 3 of the RP-US Extradition Treaty specifically provides that “extradition shall not be granted if the executive authority of the Requested State determines that the request was politically motivated, or that the offense is a military offense which is not punishable under non-military penal legislation.” In the United States, the Secretary of State exercises this ultimate power and is conceded considerable discretion. He balances the equities of the case and the demands of the nation’s foreign relations." In sum, he is not straitjacketed by strict legal considerations like an ordinary court.”

Justice Puno stressed that “the ponencia failed to accord due importance to the international law aspect of an extradition treaty as it unduly stressed its constitutional law dimension. This goes against the familiar learning that in balancing the clashing interests involved in extradition treaty, national interest is more equal than the others. While lately, humanitarian considerations are being factored in the equation, still the concept of extradition as a national act is the guiding idea. Requesting and granting extradition remains a power and _ prerogative of the national government of a State. The process still involves’ relations between international personalities.!? Needless to state, a more deferential treatment should be given to national interest than to individual interest.”

## Postscript: Jimenez Decision Reversed

Ina Resolution promulgated on October 17, 2000? (one week after my printing deadline), the Court, by a vote also of 9-6, reversed its January 18, 2000 Decision. This unusual reversal was brought about by the change in the votes of Justices Quisumbing, Purisima and De Leon. In reversing itself, the Court invoked essentially the same arguments earlier set forth in the two erstwhile Dissenting Opinions of Justice Puno and me. It ruled that Respondent Mark Jimenez was not entitled to notice and hearing during the preliminary stage of the extradition proceedings for the following reasons: ie

1. There is no provision in the RP-US Extradition Treaty and in PD 1069 that gives an extraditee such rights.

## Summary

Chapter 18 of 'Transparency, Unanimity & Diversity' recounts Secretary of Justice v. Lantion and Jimenez, the extradition case in which the Supreme Court first held, by a vote of 9-6 in a forty-page Decision penned by Justice Jose A.R. Melo, that Mark Jimenez was entitled to copies of the extradition documents and an opportunity to comment even during the executive department's preliminary evaluation of the United States' request. CJP set out the chronology -- the November 13, 1994 RP-US Extradition Treaty, the US request of June 18, 1999 by Note Verbale No. 0522, the evaluation panel created that same day, Jimenez's denied request through counsel Estelito P. Mendoza, and the status quo order of Manila RTC Judge Ralph C. Lantion -- and then distinguished the two phases of an extradition proceeding: an evaluation stage that is essentially executive, and an extradition hearing that is judicial. He summarized the Melo majority's six reasons, including that the evaluation could lead to provisional or temporary arrest, that it was akin to a preliminary investigation, that notice and hearing pervade administrative proceedings, and that the constitutional right to information applied. He then reproduced his own Dissent: in the peculiar factual milieu of the case there was no danger that Jimenez would lose life, liberty or property, because Article 9 of the Treaty required urgency, a corresponding request, and priority to the extradition request, none of which was present, and the justice secretary had disclaimed any intent to seek provisional arrest; the evaluation stage was ministerial, confined to checking the Treaty's documentary requirements, so there was nothing the extraditee could genuinely comment on; and his remedy for reputational injury lay in fighting the charges in the US courts. He also summarized Justice Puno's scholarly dissent, which traced extradition law from antiquity to modern publicists, proposed a balancing approach, and held extradition sui generis rather than criminal. A postscript records that on October 17, 2000, one week after his printing deadline, the Court reversed itself by the same 9-6 margin, adopting essentially the two dissents' arguments after three justices changed their votes.
