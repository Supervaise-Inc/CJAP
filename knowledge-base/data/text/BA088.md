# Leveling the Playing Field -- Ch. 17: People v. Genosa: The Battered Woman Syndrome
Date:       2004-12-01
Publisher:  Not stated in the file (Google Books lists the publisher as 'Artemio V. Panganiban, 2004')
Source:     Leveling the Playing Field, Not stated in the file (Google Books lists the publisher as 'Artemio V. Panganiban, 2004') — ch. 17
By: Artemio V. Panganiban

In this day and age of militancy and advocacy for the protection of women’s rights, People v. Genosa takes center stage, because it discusses for the first time the viability of the “battered woman syndrome” as a defense in a prosecution for parricide. To be able to write the Decision in this case, I had to immerse myself in an in-depth medical and scientific study of this phenomenon, and how it was applied in relevant foreign jurisprudence.

To set the case in its proper perspective, let me relate the background.

## The Facts

Marivic Genosa was a victim of repeated physical and verbal abuse perpetrated by her husband for over eight of their twelve years of marital union. On the evening of November 15, 1995, another such violent incident occurred despite the fact that she was eight months pregnant. This time the incident culminated 1 the death of her abusive husband, Ben Genosa.

Admitting she had killed her husband, Marivic anchored her prayer for acquittal on a novel theory — the “battered woman syndrome” (BWS), which allegedly constituted self-defense.

With the assistance of her counsel, she pleaded not guilty during her arraignment on March 3, 1997. In due course, she was tried for and convicted of parricide.

While there were collateral questions raised, the following were the essential legal issues: (1) whether Marivic acted in self-defense and in defense of her fetus; and (2) whether treachery attended the killing of Ben.

Initially, the Court endeavored to understand and explain the theory of the battered woman syndrome While new in Philippine jurisprudence, the concept was recognized in foreign jurisdictions as a form of self-defense or at least incomplete self-defense.

## The Battered Woman Syndrome

A battered woman has been defined as one “who is repeatedly subjected to any forceful physical or psychological behavior by a man in order to coerce her to do something he wants her to do without concern for her rights. Battered women include wives of women in any form of intimate relationship with men. Furthermore, in order to be classified as a battered woman, the couple must go through the battering cycle at least twice. Any woman may find herself in an abusive relationship with a man once. If it occurs a second time, and she remains in the situation, she is defined as a battered woman.”

Battered women exhibit common personality traits, such as low self-esteem; traditional beliefs about the home, the family and the female sex role; emotional dependence upon the dominant male; a tendency to accept responsibility for the batterer’s actions; and false hopes that the relationship would improve.

More graphically, the battered woman syndrome is characterized by the so-called “cycle of violence,” which has three phases: (1) the tension-building phase (2) the acute battering incident; and (3) the tranquil, loving (or at least nonviolent) phase.

During the tension-building phase, minor battering occurs — it could be verbal or slight physical abuse or some other form of hostile behavior. The woman usually tries to pacify the batterer through a show of kind, nurturing behavior; or by simply staying out of his way. What actually happens is that she allows herself to be abused in ways that, to her, are comparatively in All she wants is to prevent the escalation of the violence exhibited by the batterer. This wish, however, proves to be double-edged, because her “placatory” and passive behavior legitimizes his belief that he has the right to abuse her in the first place.

Each partner senses the imminent loss of control and the growing tension and despair. Exhausted from the persistent stress, the battered woman soon withdraws emotionally. But the more she becomes emotionally unavailable, the more the batterer becomes angry, oppressive and abusive. Often, at some unpredictable point the violence “spirals out of control” and leads to an acute battering incident.

The acute battering incident is characterized by brutality, destructiveness, and sometimes death. The battered woman deems this incident unpredictable, yet also inevitable. During this phase, she has no control; only the batterer may put an end to the violence. Its nature can be as unpredictable as the time of its explosion, and so are his reasons for ending it. The battered woman usually realizes that she cannot reason with him, and that resistance would only exacerbate her condition.

At this stage she has a sense of detachment from the attack and the terrible pain, although she may later clearly remember every detail. Her apparent passivity in the face of acute violence may be rationalized thus: the batterer is almost always much stronger physically, and she knows from her past painful experience that it is futile to fight back. Acute battering incidents are often very savage and out of control.

The final phase of the cycle of violence begins when the acute battering incident ends. During this tranquil period, a profound relief is experienced by the couple. On the one hand, the batterer may show a tender and nurturing behavior towards his partner. He knows that he has been viciously cruel and tries to make up for it, begging for her forgiveness and promising never to beat her again. On the other hand, the battered woman also (tries to convince herself that the battery will never happen again; that her partner will change for the better; and that this “good, gentle and caring man” is the real person whom she loves.

A battered woman usually believes that she is sole anchor of the emotional stability of the batter Sensing his isolation and despair, she feels response for his well-being, The truth, though, is that the changes of his reforming — or seeking or receiving professional help — are very slim, especially if she remains with him Yet, it is in this phase of remorseful reconciliation that she is most thoroughly tormented psychologically.

## Illusion of Interdependency

The illusion of absolute interdependency is well-entrenched in a battered woman’s psyche. She and her batterer are emotionally dependent on each other — she for his nurturant behavior, he for her forgiveness. Underneath this miserable cycle of “tension, violence and forgiveness,” each partner may believe that it is better to die than to be separated. Neither one may really feel independent, capable of functioning without the other.

Because of the recurring cycles of violence experienced by the abused woman, her state of mind metamorphoses.

To understand the syndrome properly, one’s view point should not be drawn from that of an ordinary reasonable person. What goes on in the mind of a person who has been subjected to repeated, severe beatings may not be consistent with — nay, comprehensible to — those who have not been through a similar experience. Expert opinion is essential to clarify and refute common myths and misconceptions about battered women.

Lenore Walker, an American clinical psychologist, has conducted research studies on domestic violence that have had a significant impact in the United States and the United Kingdom on the treatment and the prosecution of cases in which a battered woman has been charged with the killing of her violent partner. Dr. Walker explains that the cyclical nature of the violence inflicted upon the battered woman immobilizes the latter’s “ability to act decisively in her own interests, making her feel trapped in the relationship with no means of escape.” In her years of research, Dr. Walker has found that “the abuse often escalates at the point of separation and battered women are in greater danger of dying then.”

“The psychologist further explains that “the truth or facts of a situation turn out to be less important than the individual’s set of beliefs or perceptions concerning the situation. Battered women don’t attempt to leave the battering situation, even when it may seem to outsiders that escape is possible, because they cannot predict their own safety; they believe that nothing they or anyone else does will alter their terrible circumstances.”

Thus, just as the battered woman believes that she is somehow responsible for the violent behavior of her partner, she also believes that he is capable of killing her, and that there is no escape. She feels unsafe, suffers from pervasive anxiety, and usually fails to leave the relationship. Unless a shelter is available, she stays with her husband not only because she typically lacks a means of self-support, but also because she fears that if she leaves she would be found and hurt even more.

According to Dr. Natividad Dayan, an expert witness for the defense, there are several reasons why a battered woman does not readily leave an abusive part — poverty, self-blame and guilt arising from the latter’s belief that she provoked the violence, that she has obligation to keep the family intact at all cost for the sake of their children, and that she is the only hope for her spouse to change.

Another expert witness, Dr. Alfredo Pajarillo, explained that “overwhelming brutality, trauma” could result in posttraumatic stress disorder, a form of “anxiety neurosis or neurologic anxietism.” After being repeatedly and severely abused, battered persons “may believe that they are essentially helpless, lacking power to change their situation. x x x [A]cute battering incidents can have the effect of stimulating the development of coping responses to the trauma at the expense of the victim's ability to muster an active response to try to escape further trauma. Furthermore, x x x the victim ceases to believe that anything she can do will have a predictable positive effect.”

## The Court’s Ruling

The Court, however, ruled that in the Genosa case, the defense had fallen short of proving all three phases of the “cycle of violence” supposedly characterizing the relationship of Ben and Marivic Genosa. There can be no doubt that there were acute battering incidents. In relating to the court a quo how the fatal incident that led to the death of Ben had started, Marivic perfectly described the tension-building phase of the cycle. She was able to explain in adequate detail the typical characteristics of that stage. That single incident, though, did not prove the existence of the syndrome. In other words, he failed to establish that in at least another battering episode in the past, she had gone through a similar pattern. It must be noted that for there to be a cycle of violence, the couple must have gone through it at least twice.

The Court appreciated the ratiocinations given the expert witnesses for the defense. Indeed, they were q able to explain fully, albeit merely theoretically and scientifically, how the personality of the battered woman usually evolved or deteriorated as a result of repeated and severe beatings inflicted upon her by her partner or spouse. They corroborated each other's testimonies which were culled from their numerous studies of hundreds of actual cases. However, they failed to present in com the factual experiences and thoughts that Marivic had related them — if at all — on the basis of which they concluded that she had BWS.

## BWS as Self-Defense

In any event, the existence of the syndrome in a relationship does not in itself establish the legal right of the woman to kill her abusive partner. Evidence must still be considered in the context of self-defense.

Settled in our jurisprudence is the rule that the one who resorts to self-defense must face a real threat to one’s life; and the peril sought to be avoided must be imminent and actual, not merely imaginary.

## Absence of Unlawful Aggression

Unlawful aggression is the most essential element of self-defense. It presupposes actual, sudden and unexpected attack — or an imminent danger thereof — on the life or safety of a person.” According to the testimony of Marivic herself, however, there was a sufficient time interval between the unlawful aggression of Ben and her fatal counterattack. She had already been able to withdraw from his violent behavior and escape to their children’s bedroom. During that time, he apparently ceased his attack and went to bed. The reality or even the imminence of the danger he posed had ended altogether. He was no longer in a position that presented an actual threat to her life or safety. Absent any unlawful aggression, there can be no self-defense — complete or incomplete.

The Court cautioned, though, that had Ben still been awaiting Marivic when she came out of their children’s bedroom — and based on past violent incidents, there was a great probability that he would still have pursued her and inflicted graver harm — then the imminence of the real threat to her life would not have ceased yet. Where the brutalized person is already suffering from BWS, further evidence of actual physical assault at the time of the killing its not require,

Incidents of domestic battery usually have a predictable pattern. To require the battered person to await an obvious, deadly attack before she can defend her “would amount to sentencing her to ‘murder by installments.” Still, impending danger (based on her conduct in previous battering episodes) prior to the batterer's use of deadly force must be shown. Threatening behavior or communication can satisfy the required imminence of danger.*’ Considering such circumstances and the existence of BWS, self-defense may be appreciated.

## Mitigating Circumstances

But all was not lost, the Court assured. Severe beatings repeatedly inflicted on Marivic over a period of time constituted a form of cumulative provocation that broke down her psychological resistance and self-control. This “psychological paralysis” she suffered diminished her will power, thereby entitling her to the mitigating factor under paragraphs 9 and 10 of Article 13 of the Revised Penal Code.

In her Psychological Evaluation Report of Marivic Dr. Dayan explained:

“x x x. The repeated battering Marivic experienced with her husband constitutes a form of [cumulative] provocation which broke down her psychological resistance and natural self-control. It is very clear that she developed heightened sensitivity to the sight of impending danger her husband posed continuously. Marivic truly experienced at the hands of her abuser husband a state of psychological paralysis which can only be ended by an act of violence on her part.”

Dr. Pajarillo corroborates the findings of Dr. Dayan. He explained that the effect of “repetitious pain taking, repetitious battering, [and] repetitious maltreatment” as well as the severity and the prolonged administration of the battering was posttraumatic stress disorder.

In sum, the cyclical nature and the severity of the violence inflicted upon Marivic resulted in a “cumulative provocation which broke down her psychological resistance and natural self-control,” her “psychological paralysis,” and “difficulty in concentrating or impairment of memory.”

Based on the explanations of the expert witnesses, such manifestations were analogous to an illness that diminished her exercise of her will power without, however, depriving her of copiousness of her acts. There was, thus, a resulting diminution of her freedom of action, intelligence or intent Pursuant to paragraphs 9 and 10 of Article 13 of the Revised Penal Code, this circumstance should be taken in her favor and considered as a mitigating factor.

Furthermore, Marivic should also be credited with the & tenuating circumstance of having acted upon an impulse so powerful as to have naturally produced passion and obfuscation.

An acute battering incident, wherein Ben Genosa was the unlawful aggressor, preceded his being killed by Marivic. He had also threatened to kill her, while dragging her by the neck towards a cabinet in which he had kept a gun. It should also be recalled that she was eight months pregnant at the time. The attempt on her life was likewise on that of her fetus.

Ben’s abusive and violent acts, an aggression that was directed at both her life and that of her unborn child, naturally produced passion and obfuscation that must have overcome her reason. Even though she was able to retreat to a separate room, her emotional and mental state continued. According to her, she felt her blood pressure rise; she was filled with feelings of self-pity and of fear that she and her baby were about to die. In a fit of indignation, she pried open the cabinet drawer where Ben had kept a gun, then she took the weapon and used it to shoot him.

The confluence of these events brought us to the conclusion that there was no considerable period of time within which Marivic could have recovered her normal equanimity. Helpful was Dr. Pajarillo’s testimony* that with “neurotic anxiety” — a psychological effect of “overwhelming brutality [or] trauma” — the victim re-lived the beating of trauma as if it were real, although she was not actually being beaten at the time. She could not control “re-experiencing the whole thing, the most vicious, and the trauma that she suffered.” She thinks “of nothing but suffering.”

Such reliving, which is beyond the control of a person under similar circumstances, must have been what she experienced during the brief time interval and prevented her from recovering her normal equanimity. Accordingly, she should further be credited with the mitigating circumstance of passion and obfuscation.

## Treachery

Ruling that treachery was present in this case, the trial court imposed on Marivic the penalty of death. -inferred this qualifying circumstance merely from the fact that the lifeless body of Ben had been found lying in bed with an “open, depressed, circular” fracture located at the back of his head. As to exactly how and when he had been fatally attacked, however, the prosecution failed to establish clearly.

There is no showing of the relative position of the two at the time of the shooting. Besides, equably axiomatic is the rule that when a killing is preceded by argument of a quarrel, treachery cannot be appreciated as a qualifying circumstance, because the deceased may be said to have been forewarned and to have an ral pated aggression from the assailant.

Neither was there any showing that Marivic intentionally chose a specific means of successfully attacking her husband without any risk to herself from any retaliatory act that he might make. To the contrary, it appears that the thought of using the gun occurred to her only about the same moment when she decided to kill her batterer-spouse. In the absence of any convincing proof that she consciously and deliberately employed the method by which she committed the crime in order to ensure its execution, this Court resolved the doubt in her favor.

Considering the presence of these two mitigating circumstances arising from BWS, her death sentence was vacated and reduced to “six years and one day of prision mayor as minimum; to 14 years, 8 months and 1 day of reclusion temporal as maximum. Moreover, she was qualified for release from custody on parole, because she had already served the minimum period of her penalty while under detention during the pendency of her case.

## Epilogue

May I now quote in full the Epilogue I wrote to emphasize the work that had to be done to be able to decide the case prudently.

“Being a novel concept in our jurisprudence, the battered woman syndrome was neither easy nor simple to analyze and recognize vis-à-vis the given set of facts in the present case. The Court agonized on how to apply the theory as a modern-day reality. It took great effort beyond the normal manner in which decisions [were] made — on the basis of existing law and jurisprudence applicable to the proven facts. To give a just and proper resolution of the case, it endeavored to take a good look at studies conducted here and abroad in order to understand the intricacies of the syndrome and the distinct personality of the chronically abused person. Certainly, the Court has learned much. And definitely, the solicitor general and appellant's counsel, Atty. Katrina Legarda, have helped it in such learning process.

“While our hearts empathize with re-currently battered persons, we can only work within the limits of law, jurisprudence and given facts. We cannot make or invent them. Neither can we amend the Revised Penal Code. Only Congress, in its wisdom, may do so.

“The Court, however, is not discounting the possibility of self-defense arising from the battered woman syndrome. We now sum up our main points. First, each of the phases of the cycle of violence must be proven to have characterized at least two battering episodes between the appellant and her intimate partner. Second, the final acute battering episode preceding the killing of the batterer must have produced in the battered person’s mind an actual fear of an imminent harm from her batterer and an honest belief that she needed to use force in order to save her life. Third, at the time of the killing, the batterer must have posed probable — not necessarily immediate and actual — grave harm to the accused, based on the history of violence perpetrated by the former against the latter. Taken altogether, these circumstances could satisfy the requisites of self-defense. Under the existing facts of the present case, however, not all of these elements were duly established.”

## The Dissenting Opinion of Justice Santiago

According to Mme. Justice Consuelo Ynares-Santiago, the defense was able to establish the occurrence on more than one occasion of the “tension-building phase” of the cycle. To require Marivic to prove the state of mind of the deceased, as seems to be required in the ponencia, would mean that no person would ever be able to prove self-defense in a battered woman case. She could not have possibly proved whether he had felt provoked into battering her by any act or omission on her part. She could not have possibly proved that she had felt herself to be the sole support of his emotional stability and well-being. Nevertheless, she must have felt trapped and helpless in the relationship; in the end, she resorted to killing him, as no one could or did help her, whether out of fear or insensitivity, during the violent marriage she had endured.

The stage of “acute battering incident” was well demonstrated by the severe beatings suffered by Marivic in the hands of Ben, as well as his threats to kill her using a bolo or a cutter. The physical abuse occurred at least 3 times a week in the 11 miserable years of their marriage, 6 incidents of which were documented in her 1990 1995 medical records. The tranquil period she underwent was shown by the repeated “kiss-and-make-up episodes of their relationship. On more than five occasions, she ran to her parents’ house after violent fights with her husband, only to forgive him every time he would fetch her and promise to change.

All these recurring phases of the cycle of violence repentance and forgiveness developed a trauma in her mind, making her believe that a forthcoming attack from Ben would cause her death.

There is no doubt, therefore, that Marivic was afflicted with the battered woman syndrome, and that was an apprehension of death and the instinct to defend her life and that of her unborn child that drove her to kill her husband.

Traditionally, in order that self-defense may be appreciated, the unlawful aggression or attack must be imminent and actually in existence. This interpretation must, however, be reevaluated vis-à-vis the recognized inherent characteristic of the psyche of one who is afflicted with battered woman syndrome. Such a person lives in constant fear for her life and thus responds in self-defense.

Once BWS and an impending danger based on the conduct of the deceased in previous battering episodes are established, the actual occurrence of an assault is no longer a condition sine qua non before self-defense may be upheld. Threatening behavior or communication can satisfy the required imminence of danger. As stated in the ponencia, to require the battered woman to await an obviously deadly attack before she can defend her life would amount to sentencing her to murder by installment.

In the case at bar, the cycle of violence perpetrated by Ben, culminating in the physical assaults on Marivic and the attempt to shoot her when she was eight months pregnant, took the place of unlawful aggression. She was thus entitled to a complete self-defense, even if he had employed no actual violence against her at the time of the killing She had every reason to believe that he would kill her that night, not only because he had verbally threatened to kill her while attempting to get a gun from the drawer. More importantly, he had previously threatened to cut her throat with a cutter that he kept in his wallet and later actually wounded her on the wrist with a bolo.

Verily, the requirement of a threatening behavioral pattern exhibited by the batterer in previous violent episodes was sufficiently satisfied. Such episodes, juxtaposed to Marivic’s affliction with BWS, justified the killing of the deceased. A state of fear was bred or created in her mind by the danger posed by his threats with the use of bladed weapons. Under such circumstances, the 1 response of a battered woman would be to defend her: self, even at the cost of taking the life of the batterer.

Justice Santiago opined that the ponencia's’ acknowledgement of the battered woman syndrome as a valid form of self-defense was a noble recognition of the plight of — and a triumph for — battered women trapped in a culture of silence, shame and fear. She further said, however, that this acknowledgment would be an empty victory if the Court were to close its eyes deliberately to the antecedents of this case.

## The Outcome

In sum, Justice Santiago — joined by Chief Justice Davide as well as Justices Gutierrez and Martinez — vote to acquit Marivic Genosa.

On the other hand, those who concurred in my Decision were Justices Puno, Vitug, Quisumbing, Carpio Corona, Morales, Callejo, Azcuna and Tinga — thus resulting in a decisive 10-4 vote. Marivic seemed to agree with the majority, because she did not file any motion for reconsideration and accepted the Decision per se.

## Postscript

After this Decision became final, a new law on the battered woman syndrome was passed by Congress and became effective on March 27, 2004.

Republic Act No. 9262 or the “Anti-Violence Against Women and Their Children Act of 2004” exculpate victim-survivors of the battered woman syndrome” from any criminal and civil liability, notwithstanding the absence of any of the elements of the justifying circumstances of self-defense under the Revised Penal Code.

Other acts of violence against women and their children are also penalized under this law. These include causing or threatening to cause them harm; attempting to compel or compelling them to engage in conduct from which they have the right under the law to desist; stalking; and engaging in any form of harassment or violence against them. ‘These acts are considered public offenses hence, they may be prosecuted upon the filing of a complaint by any citizen having personal knowledge of the circumstance involving the commission of such acts.

The law entitles victims to apply for a protection order for the purpose of preventing further acts of violence against them; safeguarding them from further harm; minimizing any disruption in their daily lives; and facilitating their opportunity and ability to regain control over their lives.

How this new law would affect the foregoing Decision on Genosa remains to be seen.
