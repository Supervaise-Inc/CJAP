# Leveling the Playing Field -- Ch. 11: Judicial Excellence in the Bio-Age

Two months ago, my wife, my children (and their spouses), my grandchildren and I spent our Christmas holidays in Orlando, Florida. Let me tell you that all of us were impressed by Mickey Mouse and Donald Duck. They had perfected the art of entertainment, such that even old folks, like my wife and me, enjoyed the Disney Magic Kingdom as much as our young grandchildren did. Indeed, we savored the American extravaganza to the max!

But what specially delighted me was our visit to the nearby Kennedy Space Center in Cape Canaveral. Our grand tour of the premises was spiced with a historical account of how, during the early 60s, the space race had eerily been advanced one gigantic step after another by the Cold War rivalry between the United States and the then Soviet Union.

We were ruefully reminded that, to the chagrin of US President John F. Kennedy, the first astronaut to orbit the earth was a Russian — Yuri Gagarin. Consequently, President Kennedy publicly vowed that America would overtake the USSR in transporting the first humans to the moon. The Kennedy promise was fulfilled on July 20, 1969, when Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin became the first men to walk on the surface of the earth’s only satellite.

Although space exploration was triggered and hastened by the flexing of military muscles, humanity’s ambition to reach the vast universe beyond the earth was not dampened by the dismantling of the Soviet Union in 1089 and the end of the Cold War.

## International Cooperation In the Conquest of Space

In fact, the decade of the 1990s was remarkable for fostering cooperation, no longer competition, between Russia and the USA. Both nations jointly sponsored and developed the International Space Station (ISS), from which humans would eventually explore the solar system and the reaches of outer space.

But what is even more remarkable is that the International Space Station is being built, not just by the two erstwhile military rivals; they have been joined by 14 other countries (for a total of 16): Brazil, Belgium, Canada, Denmark, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, the Netherlands, Norway, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland and the United Kingdom.

The transition in the space program — from competition to cooperation and from bilateral efforts of military titans to the multilateral conscription of 16 nations — bespeaks the collaboration currently going on around the world in all fields of human endeavor.

Truly, the space program is typical of the globalization trend in every major human activity. The advent 0 the Information Age — triggered by the personal computer, the Internet and the cell phone — has transformed the world into one global village, where traditional legal borders no longer deter human progress. The digitization of information, more than the retail of goods and services, has become the new means for distributing wealth and power.

This phenomenon of globalization was simply & plained by a well-read Filipino columnist, Federico ~ Pascual Jr.,' in the light of this question-and-answer banter:

“Question: What is the truest definition of globalization?

“Answer: Princess Diana’s death. “Question: How come?

“Answer: An English princess with an Egyptian boyfriend crashes in a French tunnel [aboard] a German car, driven by a Belgian who was drunk on Scottish whiskey, followed closely by Italian paparazzi on Japanese motorcycles, [and] treated by an American doctor using Brazilian medicine. [This story was] sent to me by a Paraguayan using [American technology] on Taiwanese-made chips and a Koreanmade monitor, assembled by Bangladeshi workers in a Singapore plant, transported in lorries driven by Indians, unloaded by Sicilian longshoremen, [and] sold by Jews.”

Indeed, in our day and age, we know that the advances in trade, banking transactions, telecommunications, transportation and migration of people have begun to blur national legal boundaries. Even more recently, the advances in science, the cross-pollination of technology, and the realities of our ever-shrinking world have gradually assaulted and, in fact, diluted the territoriality doctrine in international law. A new paradigm is emerging, demanding the universalization of laws and of the judicial rulings interpreting them.’

To be sure, the people of today find themselves in unusual and unprecedented predicaments deeply entrenched In the overwhelming complexities of the bio-age.

## Judicial Pronouncements on the Intricacies of the Bio-Age

In this lecture, let me cite some bio-cases that have been adjudicated by a number of courts in various countries. As you will note, most of these cases come from the United States. Because of scientific advances in that country, American courts had to grapple early on with A involving the new biosciences and biotechnologies. Questions similar to those raised in such litigations will surely reach the judicial authorities of other nations, because legal problems that the new sciences engender are themselves universal.

What I am saying is that disputes spawned by genetic engineering — whether of humans, animals of play — will occur worldwide. Ideally then, the common problems brought about by the new sciences should 0 -i solved similarly by various national tribunals in order to avoid the evils of forum shopping. Indeed, conflicts of jurisdiction and judgment could be avoided by globalizing justice.

Controversies involving the biosciences and. technologies range from the ability to invent and influence life forms at the molecular level; to the tect deployment of reversible and permanent changes to eff plants, animal life, and the structure and function of human biology.’ To repeat, the consequences and implications of these decisions transcend territorial jurisdictions.

An excellent way of organizing these bioscience cases is by referring to the theme “Hatching, Matching, Snatching and Dispatching” evolved in 2002 during the Seventh Annual Conference of the Australian Institute of Health, Law and Ethics (AIHLE) held at the University of Newcastle.

## “Hatching” Cases

I have no doubt that the old-fashioned way of making babies will never go out of style, but there are now “new” ways of producing them. From such a novel endure was born the term hatching. It refers to cases icing to embryo ownership and disposition, including sion of fertility, abortion, reproductive rights, papery and heirship. During the last few years, people have begun to ask who should have access to the different forms of reproductive technology and — the most controversial question — whether we should clone humans. Interestingly, in the AIHLE conference, the medicolegal debate asked whether it would be ethical to clone a lawyer"

With the advent of cryogenic technology, questions relating to conception and childbearing come to the fore. Specifically, the issues of ownership and disposition of embryos and sperms have been elevated to courts. Kass v. Kass resolved the disposition of five frozen and stored pre-embryos or pre-zygotes — fertilized previously, during the marriage, to assist a couple in having a child.

When divorced, the woman wanted the pre-zygotes implanted in her womb, claiming that this procedure was her only chance for genetic motherhood. On the other hand, the father objected to the burdens 0} unwanted fatherhood. He argued that the couple had agreed at the time they embarked on the effort that, in case of divorce, the pre-zygotes would be donated to an in vitro fertilization (IVF) program for approved research purposes. To document their dispositional intent, an agreement indicating it was signed by the parties prior to the cryopreservation of the pre-zygotes.

The Court of Appeals of New York ruled that the informed consent signed by the parties had unequivocally manifested their mutual intention that the pre-zygotes be donated to the IVF program for the purpose of research. Thus, only by a joint decision of the couple would these be used for implantation.

In J.B. v. M.B., the facts are substantially the same as in Kass v. Kass. In the former, however, it was the husband, M.B., who wanted the zygotes implanted in another woman. J.B., his wife, opposed his position, arising that it would violate her right to control her reproduction.

The Superior Court of New Jersey Appellate Division found that in the contracts signed by the parties, (the appropriate disposition of the embryos under the circumstances of the case had not been clearly determined. With no controlling contract, the court then considered the public policy implications of the litigants’ claims. In other words, it ruled that the right of the husband to procreate would not be lost if he were to be denied an opportunity to use or donate the pre-embryos. He was already a father and was capacitated to sire additional children, whether through natural procreation or through artificial in vitro fertilization.

In contrast, the wife’s right not to procreate might be lost through the donation of the pre-embryos or the implantation in another womb. Implantation, if successful, would result in the birth of her biological child and could have lifelong emotional and psychological repercussions. Thus, the Court concluded that a contract was against public policy if it would force one to become a parent at some future date, when one would no longer have wanted to become one. On the other hand, it he that a contract that provided for the disposal of en bryos, but that did not raise the same reproductive right issues, would be proper.

Please note that in the first case (Kass v. Kass), the dispositional intent of the parties was deemed clear at was thus upheld; whereas in the second (J.B. v. M.B.), it was not. Thus, the Court used public policy to decide the controversy.

The question of a child’s heirship has always been a major source of court litigation. In this age of biosciences, the question has become more complex, as in Woodward ex rel. Estate of Woodward v. Commissioner of Social Security. This case raised the issue of whether children conceived with the use of the sperms of a dead father were his heirs.

The Supreme Judicial Court of Massachusetts has ruled on the question of whether posthumously conceived genetic children may enjoy inheritance rights under the intestacy statute. The case involved three powerful State interests: the best interests of children, the or-(derly administration of estates, and the reproductive rights of the genetic parent. The task was to balance and harmonize these interests to effect the legislature’s overall purposes.

After the donor-parent’s death, the burden shifted to the surviving parent (or the posthumously conceived child’s other legal representative) to prove the deceased genetic parent’s affirmative consent to two requirements for posthumous parentage: (1) posthumous reproduction and (2) support of any resulting child. Merely showing the genetic linkage to the deceased was not enough; proof of consent was required. The plaintiff would have to prove to the satisfaction of the court that the deemed both wanted to have children and intended to support them.

## “Matching Cases”

“Matching” confronts the question of the people’s es to goods and services — especially consumer products, drugs and treatments brought about by scientific innovations. More specifically, matching refers to emergence and the regulation of genetically modified organisms (GMOs).

In Europe, GMOs have been regulated during the last ten years. Recognizing the consumer's right to information and labeling as a tool to make an informed choice, the European Union (EU) has in fact mandated specific labeling requirements to indicate the presence of GMC in a product.”

Monsanto v. Tilly involves a damage suit filed by the biotechnology company Monsanto against several activists (Tilly et al.), who had pulled its crops from the ground to protect non-GMO ones that were in the vicinity and might thus be cross-pollinated.

The Court ruled that an individual had no right to destroy the property of another in the guise of protecting others from danger. The only circumstance that wor justify such a trespass would be an immediate danger that amounted to an emergency. But even in those emergency situations, trespass by an individual would not be justified where there was a public body that would be responsible for the protection of the relevant public interest. The Court added that those who suffered infringement of their lawful rights were entitled to the protection of the law. If those who committed such infringement deliberately did so to attract publicity for their own cause, then they must bear the consequences of their actions.

There are also demands for unusual treatments and "miracle” drugs that have been brought about by advances in science and technology.

In Schiff v. Prados, the interesting question raised was whether a physician could be required to disclose illegal alternative treatments to patients. The case involved the death of a child, Crystin, from a medically untreatable brain tumor; and the parents’ quest for hope in) the form of an unproven and illegal treatment — antineoplastons — being offered by a physician in Houston, Dr. Stanislaw Burzynski.

In his Complaint relating to the death of Crystin, the plaintiff alleged that Dr. Prados had not obtained their informed consent to treat her, because he had failed to advise them of the antineoplaston treatment offered by Dr. Burzynski. The plaintiff argued that had he and his wife been informed of the alternative treatment, they would have refused the normal radiation and chemo therapy that their child had undergone.

The Court simply held that as a matter of law, the treatment was not available in California, and so the defer: dant could not be held liable for failing to recommend it.

Similarly, in People v. Privitera, the plaintiffs: demanding their right to access a drug — laetrile — intended for the alleviation or cure of cancer. The co ruled that the ban on access to the drug protected legitimate state interest in the health and safety of its citizens Neither did the ban encroach on the plaintiffs’ constitutional right to privacy..

Matching also saw the dawn of deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) testing in court cases. The Supreme Court in my country has recently recognized the scientific soundness of DNA testing as a conclusive means of identifying suspects in criminal cases or of establishing filiation in civil cases. But elsewhere in the world, DNA testing has already mutated into questions in the area of post-conviction appeals,” thus affecting rules on the statute of limitations for filing appeals and charges.

DNA testing is just the preliminary step to the more advanced phases of the emerging bio-age. Yet, in the United States alone, Time magazine has reported”! that nearly 80 convicts by final judgments were later ex-operated, because subsequent DNA evidence had proven that they were completely innocent of the crimes attributed to them.

## "Snatching” Cases

“Snatching” refers to controversies that involve researcher done on individuals; and that have inevitable implications on body parts, tissues, genetic testing and incest to genetic information.

Moore v. Regents of University of California (the spleen face) was about a doctor who was treating his patient for hairy-cell leukemia, but was at the same time conducting research using information obtained there from. The defendant-doctor extracted an extensive amount of blood, blood serum, skin, bone marrow aspirate, and sperm from the plaintiff-patient; and later recommended the removal of the latter's spleen. The organ was used in the research being undertaken by the defendant, who was later able to establish a cell line from the plaintiff’s 1 lymphocytes. The patient subsequently sued the doctor, his researcher and everyone else involved in the research.

The Supreme Court of California began its Dee sion with three well-established principles. First/, in the exercise of control over one’s own body, an adult of sound mind has the right to determine whether or not to submit to lawful medical treatment. Second, to be effective, a patient’s consent to treatment must be informed. Third, in soliciting the patient’s consent, a physician has the fiduciary duty to disclose all information that is material to such consent.

These principles led to the following conclusions: (1) a physician must disclose personal interests, whether research or economic, that are unrelated to the patient’s health and may affect the physician’s professional judgment; and (2) a physician’s failure to disclose such interests may give rise to a cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty or for performing medical procedures without the patient’s informed consent.

The Court stressed that no law prohibited physicians from conducting research in the same area in which they practiced. Progress in medicine often depends upon physicians — such as those practicing in the university hospital where Moore received treatment — who conduct research while caring for their patients. Yet, a physician who treats a patient in whom he also has a research interest has potentially conflicting loyalties, because medical decisions would be made by disproportionally weighing the research benefits against the health risks to the patient.

In another case, the Court of Appeals of Maryland easily ruled against a research done on individuals -this time on healthy children who were made the subjects of a study concerning lead paint abatement procedures. The appellate court held that the consent form had not directly informed the parents that some of the children might ingest lead dust particles, and that one of the rea for testing the children’s blood was to evaluate how effective the various countermeasures would be.

The Court further ruled that human subjects we entitled to all material information. Whether subjective or objectively assessed, the children or their surrogates should have been additionally informed that as a rest of the experiment, it was possible that some lead might accumulate in their blood. Thus, the consent given by the parents was not valid, because they had not been furnished full material information.

In the area of genetic testing, the Supreme Court of Florida recognized, in Pate v. Threlkel, the duty of a physician to warn patients of the transmissibility of a genetic disease to a child. In relation to this Decision, US courts have laid down, in a string of cases, rules protecting the lives of individuals in this age of biological and technological advances.

## “Dispatching” Cases

Finally, “dispatching” cases discussed hereunder involve ownership and enjoyment of technological and scientific innovations. In other words, before a product can be dispatched and the fruit of one’s labor fully enjoyed, the basic question of the extent of one’s right must be resolved. Thus, courts all over the world are swamped today with hives relating to patents, trademarks and intellectual property rights.

In Diamond v. Chakrabarty, the US Supreme Court ruled that a “human-made, genetically engineered bacterium” capable of breaking down multiple components of crude oil may be patented. The usefulness of this genetically modified organism — a bioscience phenomenon — could come in the form of protection of the environment from oil-tanker spillages in the ocean.

Voting 5-4, the highest US Court explained that the bacterium was patentable. The fact that it was 4 did not matter. The relevant distinction was not between animate and inanimate objects, but between made inventions and products of nature. Provided; invention had passed the test of utility, novelty and not obviousness, it was patentable — being still deemed a product of human ingenuity, not of a natural process This ruling was unique, because it provided a life-science exception to the general rule on patenting.

While hailed by US business, the Decision generated fears of US monopolies of biotechnology industries. Anxious Europeans responded with regulations that apparently built a grand barrier to American engineering.”

In Monsanto v. Schmeiser, the Supreme Court of Canada held that a farmer’s unauthorized use of Canola seeds developed and patented by Monsanto was actionable. The seeds contained an “alien gene” that made them resistant to a herbicide known as “Round-Up.” The Canadian judiciary turned down the defendant’s claim that the patent should be disregarded, because the seeds were products of nature and not of human intervention.

On the question of whether a mouse (an “oncomouse”) that had been injected with a cancer-promoting gene could be patented, US, Japanese and several European courts answered yes. But in Commissioner of Nuents v. Harvard College, the Canada Supreme Court, voting 5-4, said no. It grounded its ruling on the arguments that only “manufactured” objects or “compositions of matter” could be patented within the meaning of the term invention under the Patent Act.

Distinguishing this case from Chakrabarty’s bacterium, the Canadian high court said that the US patent in the latter case had referred only to a single-cell microbe.

On the other hand, the minority opined that the oncomouse was patentable. It maintained that the “extraordinary achievement of altering every single cell in the body of an animal, which does not in this altered form exist in nature x x x is an inventive ‘composition of matter’ x x x and is entitled to the patent.”

## Conclusion: The Need for Overall Excellence

In closing, may I say that in order to decide the judicial challenges of the third millennium, it is no longer enough for judges to be walking encyclopedias of constitutions, laws and judicial doctrines. Given the high-velocity advances in all fields of human endeavor, it is essential that they must also have a working knowledge of most branches of human knowledge, including the humanities, economics, accounting, medicine, world history, computer science, telecommunication, the digital sciences in general, mathematics, physics, and now the biosciences and biotechnologies. Today, this seminar signals the beginning of judicial excellence in resolving controversies brought by the dawning of the bio-age in our world.

I thank you.
