## Successional Rights of Illegitimate Children

The Family Code recognizes only two kinds of children: legitimate and illegitimate. Prior to the effectivity of the Family Code on August 3, 1988, however, the Civil Code further classified illegitimate children as (1) natural, (2) natural children by legal fiction or (3) spurious (or bastards).

A child born within a valid marriage is legitimate, while one born outside of it is illegitimate. If, at the time of the child's conception, the parents were not legally barred from marrying each other, such illegitimate issue is referred to as a "natural"child. And if the parents actually marry each other later, then the child is "legitimated" and will enjoy all the rights and privileges of a legitimate child. If the parents do not actually marry, the natural child's rights depend on whether the parents "acknowledge" or “recognize” him or her’ but whether acknowledged or not, such child does not acquire all the rights of one who is legitimate or legitimated.

Under the Civil Code, a natural child "by legal fiction" is one conceived or born of a marriage that is void or is declared a nullity. As such, he or she is accorded the rights of a recognized natural child. Here, a marriage is actually celebrated or entered into by the parties, but considered or declared void because of some legal impediments.

In the case of "spurious" children, no marriage of the parents, whether valid or void, is celebrated at all. However, when the child is recognized by one or both parents, the child acquires by law certain rights, which by no means approximate those of his or her legitimate or even natural counterparts.

There are three rights that children enjoy in varying degrees, depending on their filiation: (1) the use of the surname of their father, (2) support from him and (3) succession to his estate.

The successional rights of natural children by legal fiction were the subject of De Santos v. Angeles. Petitioner Maria Rosario de Santos was the legitimate child of Dr. Antonio de Santos and Sofia Bona who were married on February 7, 1941. Subsequently, the marriage soured and Antonio fell in love with a fellow physician, Conchita Talag. In 1949, Antonio obtained from a Nevada (USA) court a divorce decree which, however, was not recognized by Philippine laws. Nonetheless, he married Conchita in Tokyo, Japan; and, cohabiting with her, sired eleven children. On March 30, 1967, Sofia died in Guatemala. Less than a month later, on April 23, 1967, Antonio and Conchita contracted marriage in Tagaytay City in accordance with Philippine laws. On March 8, 1981, Antonio died.

In a proceeding to settle the estate of Antonio, the trial court, presided by Judge Adoracion Angeles, ruled that Conchita's children were legitimated by the Tagaytay marriage and, thus, instituted them as coequal heirs, along with Sofia's lone legitimate child. Disagreeing, petitioner asked the Supreme Court for a writ of certiorari, contending that since only natural children could be legitimated, her halfbrothers and half-sisters who were only natural children by legal fiction - born out of a void (being bigamous) marriage in Tokyo - could not be legitimated, and thus could not inherit the same amount to which legitimate children were entitled.

By a vote of 8 to 7, the Court agreed with petitioner and, speaking through Justice Romero, ruled that "a natural child by legal fiction cannot rise beyond that to which an acknowledged natural child is entitled insofar as his hereditary rights are concerned." The Court added:

"It is thus incongruous to conclude, as private respondent maintains, that petitioner's half siblings can rise to her level by the fact of being legitimized, for two reasons: first, they failed to meet the most important requisite of legitimation, that is, that they be natural children within the meaning of Article 269; second, natural children by legal fiction cannot demand that they be legitimized simply because it is one of the rights enjoyed by acknowledged natural children."

The majority held that "Illegitimation is not a 'right' which is demandable by a child. It is a privilege available only to natural children proper, as defined under Article 269 [of the Civil Code]," but not to natural children by legal fiction, because "[there was from the outset an intent to exclude children conceived or born out of illicit relations." Granting them the rights of legitimacy would prejudice the petitioner's share in the estate of her father because, instead of being granted one fourth of the total estate of Antonio, she would be forced to inherit only a proportion equal to that which would go to each of her eleven half-brothers and half-sisters.

Justice Hermosisima wrote a Concurring Opinion while Justices Vitug, Kapunan and I wrote Dissents. The dissenters relied on Article 89 of the Civil Code, which granted natural children by legal fiction " the same status, rights and obligations acknowledged by natural children." Since Article 269 in relation to Article 270 granted acknowledged  natural children the right to be legitimated by the subsequent marriage of their parents, then such legitimation should also be accorded to natural children by legal fiction. Wrote Justice Kapunan:

"Since the decedent's 1951 marriage in Tokyo with the private respondent was invalid, being one of those marriages classified as void from the very beginning under the Civil Code, the status of her children clearly falls under Article 89 which puts them on par, at least in terms of rights and obligations, with acknowledged natural children. Since the rights of acknowledged natural children include the right of legitimation - under Article 270 of the Civil Code - by the subsequent valid marriage of their parents, it therefore plainly follows that by virtue of Article 89, in relation to Article 270, the private respondent's children were deemed legitimated by the subsequent valid marriage of their parents in the Philippines in 1967."

Hewing closely to Articles 89, 269 and 270 of the Civil Code, I explained in my own Dissent:

"Under the provisions of the Civil Code, legitimation takes place when three requisites are met: (a) that the child be a natural child; (b) that he be recognized by both parents either before or after a valid marriage; and (c) that there be a subsequent valid marriage of the parents (cf. Paras, Civil Code of the Philippines Annotated, 1984 ed., Vol. I, p. 651). A natural child by legal fiction possesses the first two requisites from inception by virtue of Art. 89, which places him on the same plane as an acknowledged natural child. In that sense, he has an advantage over a natural child as defined by Art. 269, for the latter would still need to be recognized by both parents in order to have the status and rights of an acknowledged natural child. Thus, for the purpose of legitimation, the natural child by legal fiction needs to fulfill only the third requisite: a valid subsequent marriage between his parents (cf. Paras, op. cit., p. 651; Tolentino, Col, p. of Phippines, geT reprints, permanent or perpetual, such as incest or the fact that one or both of the parties have been found guilty of killing the spouse of one of them, no legitimation can ever take place as no valid marriage can ever be made between the parents (Tolentino, op cit., p. 570). But the bigamous character of a marriage is terminable by, among other causes, the death of the first spouse, making a subsequent marriage valid. And that simply was what happened in the case at bench."

Defending the rights of natural children by legal fiction against social ostracism, I wrote:

"Indeed, it is hardly fair to stigmatize and create social and successional prejudices against children who had no fault in nor control over the marital impediments which bedeviled their parents. They are the victims, life. Why then should they suffer the consequences? In the final analysis, there are really no illegitimate children; there are only illegitimate parents. And this Dissent finds its philosophy in this: that children, unarguably born and reared innocent in this world, should benefit by every intendment of the law, particularly where — as in this case - their parents, who originally suffered from a marital impediment, would now want to overcome the improvident social and successional consequences of such condition. Therefore, it is most unfair that these innocent children should be condemned to continue suffering the consequences of the impediment they did not cause, when the very impediment itself has disappeared."

As to the claim that legitimation would prejudice the rights of legitimate children, I said that it was precisely the right to share equally in their father's estate that the law intended.

"The mere fact that such legitimation would impact adversely upon the petitioner's successional rights as the lone legitimate child of the first marriage is no reason to deny the children of the second marriage their own legal right to be deemed legitimated. Precisely, legitimation produces such an effect — i.e., diminution of successional rights of the legitimate children. Article 272 of the Civil Code provides in fact that '(c)hildren who are legitimated by subsequent marriage shall enjoy the same rights as legitimate children.' When the legislature decided to grant to children of void marriages the same status, rights, and obligations as those acknowledged natural children, it is presumed to have carefully weighed precisely these consequences upon the rights of the other children in the family. The policy then was to cast a mantle of protection upon the children of void marriages. That policy is evidently enforced by enabling them to get legitimated in the same manner as 'acknowledged natural children — namely, by the subsequent valid marriage of their parents. If the Family Code, by repealing Art. 89 of the Civil Code, is to be viewed as having reversed or denigrated that policy (although, by and large, it appears to have maintained the policy in many other areas of family law), such reversal or denigration should not, and cannot, in any case, impair rights already acquired by and thus vested in the private respondents."

## Is the Right of First Refusal Enforceable by an Action for Specific Performance?

Normally, a right of first refusal is not enforceable by an action for specific performance. However, the Court ruled in Equatorial Realty Development, Inc. and Carmelo & Bauermann, Inc. v. Mayfair Theater, resent, such right had matured into a contract that could be enforced by a court action. But first, the facts.

Petitioner Carmelo leased to Mayfair for a term of twenty years a portion of a two-storey building it owned, located in Claro M. Recto Street, Manila. In this building, Mayfair constructed two movie houses which it named Maxim Theater and Miramar Theater. The two contracts of lease provided that "if the LESSOR should desire to sell the leased premises, the LESSEE shall be given 30-days exclusive option to purchase the same."

Sometime in 1974, Carmelo informed Mayfair via telephone that it "was desirous of selling the entire Claro M. Recto property," and that a certain Mr. Araneta was willing to buy it for US $1,200,000. It further asked whether Mayfair "was willing to buy the property for six to seven million pesos." In its reply on August 23, 1974, Mayfair reminded Carmelo of its right under the lease contracts to "be given 30-days exclusive option to purchase" the property.

Four years later, on July 30, 1978, Carmelo sold the entire Recto property, including the leased premises housing Maxim and Miramar Theaters, to Petitioner Equatorial for P11,300,000.

Disposing of a suit to annul the sale and to enforce the aforequoted right, the regional trial court (RTC) ruled that the "option clause" could not be deemed binding on Carmelo, because of “lack of distinct consideration thereof.” On appeal, the Court of Appeals reversed the RTC, ruling that since paragraph 8 of the lease contracts "did not state a fixed price for the purchase of the leased premises," then it did not grant a mere option but "a right of first refusal," which was violated by Carmelo's sale of the property to Equatorial. Agreeing with the CA, the Supreme Court, through Justice Hermosisima, ruled that "the requirement of a separate consideration for the option has no applicability in the instant case," viz.:

"An option is a contract granting a privilege to buy or sell within an agreed time and at a determined price. It is a separate and distinct contract from that which the parties may enter into upon the consummation of the option. It must be supported by a consideration. In the instant part of the contracts of lease. The consideration is built into the reciprocal obligations of the parties."

The Court ordered the rescinding of the sale to Equatorial and allowed Mayfair "to exercise its right of first refusal at the price which it was entitled to accept or reject, which is P11,300,000.00." Justices Regalado, Davide, Bellosillo, Melo, Puno, Kapunan, Mendoza and Francisco gave unqualified concurrence in the ponencia, while Justice Padilla and I wrote Separate (Concurring) Opinions.

On the other hand, Justice Vitug, joined by Justice Torres, dissented, while Justice Romero submitted a Concurring and Dissenting Opinion. Chief Justice Narvasa took no part, as he was related to an interested party.

In his Dissent, Justice Vitug stressed that rescission, being "merely subsidiary," should extend only to a grant of damages in favor of the aggrieved "A (breach) of the right of first refusal can only give rise to an action for damages, primarily under Article 19 of the Civil Code, as well as its related provisions, but not to an action for specific performance." He argued that a right of first refusal lacked "the indispensable element of consensuality in the perfection of contracts," and that "no one can be forced, least of all perhaps by a court, into a contract against his will or compelled to perform thereunder."

Defending the Decision of the Court, I wrote that, under the peculiar facts of the case, the right of first refusal, while normally unenforceable per se, could be implemented or enforced by an action for specific performance, because of the palpable bad faith on the part of both Equatorial and Mayfair. I explained:

"As already noted, Mayfair's right of first refusal in the case before us is embodied in an express covenant in the lease contracts between it, as lessee, and Carmelo, as lessor; hence, the right created is one springing from a contract. Indubitably, this had the force of law between the parties, who should thus comply with it in good faith. Such right also established a correlative obligation on the part of Carmelo to give or deliver to Mayfair a formal offer of sale of the property in the event Carmelo decided to sell it. The decision to sell was eventually made. But instead of giving or tendering to Mayfair the proper offer to sell, Carmelo gave it to its now co-petitioner, Equatorial, with whom it eventually perfected and consummated, on July 30, 1978, an absolute sale of the property, doing so within the period of effectivity of Mayfair's right of first refusal. Less than two months later, or in September 1978, with the lease still in full force, Mayfair filed the present suit.

"Worth stressing at this juncture is the fact that Mayfair had the right to require that the offer to sell the property be sent to it by Carmelo, and not to anybody else. This was violated when the offer was made to Equatorial. Under its covenant with Carmelo, Mayfair had the right, at that point, to sue for either specific performance or rescission, with damages in either case, pursuant to Arts. 1165 and 1191, Civil Code. An action for specific performance and damages seasonably filed, fortified by a writ of preliminary injunction, would have enabled Mayfair to prevent the sale to Equatorial from taking place and to compel Carmelo to sell the property to Mayfair for the same terms and price, for the reason that the filing of the action for specific performance may juridically be considered a solemn, formal, and unqualified acceptance by Mayfair of the specific terms of the offer of sale. Note that by that time, the price and other terms of the proposed sale by Carmelo had already been determined, being set forth in the offer of sale that had wrongfully been directed to Equatorial.

"As it turned out, however, Mayfair did not have a chance to file such suit, for it learned of the sale to Equatorial only after it had taken place. But it did file the present action for specific performance and for invalidation of the wrongful sale immediately after learning about the latter act. The act of promptly filing this suit, coupled with the fact that it is one for specific performance, indicates beyond cavil or doubt Mayfair's unqualified acceptance of the misdirected offer of sale, giving rise thereby to a demandable obligation on the part of Carmelo to execute the corresponding document of sale upon the payment of the price of P11,300,000.00. In other words, the principle of consensuality of a contract of sale should be deemed satisfied. The aggrieved party's consent to, or acceptance of, the misdirected offer of sale should be legally presumed in the context of the proven facts.

"To say, therefore, that the wrongful breach of a right of first refusal does not sanction an action for specific performance simply because, factually, there was no meeting of the minds as to the particulars of the sale since, ostensibly, no offer was ever made to, let alone accepted by, Mayfair, is to ignore the proven fact of presumed consent. To repeat, that consent was deemed given by Mayfair when it sued for invalidation of the sale and for specific performance of Carmelo's obligation to Mayfair. Nothing in the law as it now stands will be violated, or even simply emasculated, by this holding. On the contrary, the decision in Guzman supports it."
