# Reforming the Judiciary -- Ch. 7: Transparency, the Key to Sound Judgments
Date:       2002-12-01
Publisher:  Supreme Court Printing Press
Source:     Reforming the Judiciary, Supreme Court Printing Press — ch. 7 (batch-04 split)
By: Artemio V. Panganiban

At the outset, let me preface my remarks with the caveat that I come from a country which, unlike that of Justice Thomas Fitzgerald of the Supreme Court of Illinois and Judge Sarah Evans Barker of the US District Court of Indiana, does not observe jury trials. For my part - and, I suppose, in the case also of Judge Amadeo Santosuosso of Milan, Italy - I will discuss the topic in the light of our no-jury system of dispensing justice.

In many jurisdictions using the adversarial method to resolve legal controversies, like in my country, all information relevant to a court judgment is generally sourced from, first, the pleadings of the parties - like the complaint of the plaintiff and the answer of the defendant; two, admissions made either in those pleadings or during pretrial or discovery procedures; three, issues and questions raised during the course of the proceedings; four, evidence, both testimonial and documentary, adduced during the trial; fifth, oral or written arguments of the parties; and sixth, litigants' citation of authorities. Furthermore, until science puts forth a more reliable method, cross-examination will remain as the main tool for filtering truth and as the essential weapon to test a witness - even an expert witness - credibility, observation, recollection or predilection, and ability to state the truth.

Apart from the foregoing, knowledge of the law and the ability to reason logically and coherently as well as to communicate thoughts orally and in writing are expected of judges themselves. In addition, they are expected to have a more than passing knowledge of — not necessarily expertise in - the humanities, economics, physics, medicine, religion, philosophy, mathematics, the sciences and, in general, every branch of human endeavor .

However, they are not supposed to have prior knowledge of the specific facts of a case pending before them and, even if they do, they are barred from using that knowledge in adjudicating the case. In other words, they must start with a clean slate; they must be objective and independent and rely solely on the information brought forth in the trial according to the rules on evidence .

In the end, to show adherence to these principles, a judgment must normally state (1) the facts culled from the evidence presented, (2) the issues resolved, and (3) the laws upon which the judgment is anchored. Through the use of clear language and lawyerly logic, the judgment then presents its conclusions and grants awards if any. Because of the limitations inherent in this method of arriving at a judgment, it is possible that a legal truth may not be the same as a scientific truth. This is because scientists are not restricted by the same legal norms and due process requirements that confront judges.

In this scenario, the acceptance of the judgment by the litigants and by the public at large depends upon the transparency with which the courts demonstrate this process of discovering the material facts, applying the relevant laws on them, and arriving at an informed judgment.

By transparency, courts enable the litigants and the general public to know the manner in which they handle cases and the process by which they arrive at their decisions. For the individual, transparency requires informed consent at all times.

True, the judiciary must stand, and stand steadfast, on its duty to decide cases according to law and reason. It is true also that litigations must be won or lost, not because of popular palatability or accommodation or friendship - not to mention pecuniary consideration — but because of reasoned arguments flowing from legal principles and precedents.

I daresay, however, that courts are effective only for as long as they retain the faith and trust of the public. Without the support of the populace, they become useless, as they cannot by themselves enforce their own pronouncements. They do not command the army or the police or, for that matter, "the raging mob"; neither do they have the means or the ability to grease political patronage.

There is a difference, however, between the shifting winds of public emotion on the one hand and long-term trust in the institution on the other. While critics and pressure groups may rant from time to time against particular decisions, courts — by their adherence to the policy of transparency — will enjoy long-term residual public esteem and trust. Even if level-headed critics may disagree, they will respect the courts enough to concede the good faith, the wisdom, the probity and the diligence with which the latter perform their work. I say that only with transparency in its proceedings may the judiciary be able to earn and keep residual long term public trust.

This residual public trust is many times presumed in advanced liberal democracies with mature judiciaries. But in developing countries with evolving judicial traditions, the element of public trust bred by transparency cannot be overemphasized. Sometimes, it becomes the very litmus test of judicial independence and effectiveness. Nay, it forms the very core of good governance.

With this background, you probably guessed that I am an advocate of the liberal acceptance of evidence, subject of course to the rules on relevance and materiality, especially with regard to conflicting expert evidence. I leave it to the discretion and the wisdom of the judge to assess the credibility and the weight of evidence in the final judgment. Truly, in a transparent market of free ideas, the truth will emerge and justice will be served.

In the hypothetical case before us, I realize that the parties —specifically Signora Francesca —have privacy rights guaranteed not only by national constitutions and laws, but also by international conventions. I refer especially to those rights that bar the undue exposure of genetic predisposition to certain diseases, psychological disorders or negative behavioral tendencies — any of which may result in employment discrimination, insurance refusal or public ridicule.

However, I believe that this right to be left alone was effectively waived by the filing of various actions: a criminal suit in Milan against Alfredo; other civil actions for damages and personal injury in Italy; and patent invalidation in India, Australia and the U.S. Indeed, how can defendants effectively defend themselves in a public trial if they are barred by plaintiffs' avowal of privacy rights? Moreover, the public-interest elements of the cases present overwhelming arguments in favor of opening all aspects thereof to public scrutiny. In the final analysis, public interest and welfare are at stake in these litigations.

In sum, I believe that courts that acquire knowledge of facts and issues transparently and publicly, and then dispense their judgments in an equally transparent and public manner, will produce informed decisions that form the core of good governance and public weal.

Thank you.
