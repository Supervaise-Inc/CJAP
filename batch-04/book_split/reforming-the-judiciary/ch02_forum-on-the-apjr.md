# Chapter 2: Forum on the APJR

Every year the Ramon Magsaysay Award Foundation - widely considered as the Asian counterpart of the Nobel Foundation - selects Asian individuals and organizations who have distinguished themselves in their respective fields. For the year 2002, the Foundation added to its roster of Magsaysay Laureates six individuals: Dr. Ruth Pfau of Pakistan for Public Service; Mr. Bharat Koirala of Nepal for Journalism, Literature, and Creative Communication Arts; the Venerable Pomnyun Snim of Korea for Peace and International Understanding; Sandeep Pandey of India for Emergent Leadership; Cynthia Maung of Burma for Community Leadership; and our very own Chief Justice Hilario G. Davide Jr. for Government Service. The Awards, which come with a $50,000 cash prize each, are sponsored by the Rockefeller Brothers Fund; a recent addition - the award for Emergent Leadership - is funded by the Ford Foundation.

In the week following the awarding ceremonies, an Awardee Lecture Series is held at the RM Hall of the Ramon Mugsaysay Center on Roxas Boulevard, Manila. In this lecture series, individuals and organizations with related interests are given the opportunity to exchange views with and know more about the work of the awardees. This year the lectures were held from August 28 to September 3, 2002. Each lecture was followed by reactions from a panel of prominent personalities and an open forum participated in by the audience at large.

I decided to reproduce, as Chapter 2 of this book, the lecture of Chief Justice Davide because it explains "straight from the horse's mouth," as it were, the Supreme Court's judicial reform program, as well as the excellent reactions of former Senate President Jovito R. Salonga and Journalist Malou Mangahas. In the next chapter - "Battling Corruption, Incompetence and Delay" - my own thoughts on the same topic are summarized.

## The Lecture

## Strengthening the Credibility of the Justice System?

by Chief Justice Hilario G. Davide Jr.

This lecture is one of the pleasant duties assigned to me by the Ramon Magsaysay Award Foundation as its 2002 Ramon Magsaysay Awardee for Government Service. Your program indicates that the subject of the lecture is "Restoring Credibility in the Justice System."

The impression conveyed is that the credibility of the justice system in the Philippines has been lost. I must forthwith state that it was never lost. Public trust and confidence in the justice system, which is the final measure of credibility, may have only waned, but never ever lost. The best proof of this is that there is no anarchy in this country, and hundreds of cases are filed every working day. People still believe that the Judiciary is the constitutionally designated arbiter of legal disputes in our democratic system of government - the last bulwark of democracy.

If the credibility of the justice system has waned it is because there are some in the system who have forgotten the constitutional principle that a public office is a public trust and all public servants must at all times be accountable to the people, serve them with utmost responsibility, integrity, loyalty and lead modest lives. Faithful adherence to this mandate is much more demanded from those in the Judiciary because the task of administering justice is sacrament. The people then look to the Judiciary with great expectations.

Thus, the misconduct of one judge alone affects the entire justice system. This reminds me of a Russian saying that the thing to fear is not the law, but the judge. This is a problem of impression created by one sad experience with one judge. Of course, such an impression is unfair; yet it is a reality we must face and reverse.

My topic then for this lecture is NOT “Restoring the Credibility of the Justice System,” but “Strengthening the Credibility of the Justice System in the Philippines.”

More than the other branches of government, the Judiciary values the people's trust and confidence. The effectiveness of the courts is firmly rooted in their credibility in the public eye. Without the people’s belief in the justice system, court decisions become mere scraps of paper; and society is brought closer to chaos, as citizens become tempted to take the law into their hands.

The Judiciary is at a disadvantage in its efforts to build credibility. It cannot and should not be swayed by popular movements or lobby groups; generally it cannot, on its own, act on issues of public interest unless these are encompassed in a case before a court; its Members do not have the luxury of explaining their decisions beyond what is already written in the decisions, and neither do they have the power to revise their decisions if these are met with public opposition. Most importantly, it is unbecoming of the Judiciary to engage in public relations campaigns which aim to conjure a positive image of the courts. Evidently, where the administration of justice is concerned, credibility cannot be created or bought, it must be earned.

Credibility is described as the authority to inspire belief. Credibility, therefore, has two components, authority and belief. As applied to the Judiciary, authority must be commanded by the courts, but belief is an element born from the people, it cannot be ordered by the courts to exist. In a way, strengthening credibility is much like gardening.

The gardener, in the exercise of his authority, plants a seed. Despite his authority, the gardener cannot will the seed to blossom into a tree; he cannot coerce it to become a tall oak. The seed, like the people’s belief, cannot grow without nurturing. Thus, the gardener must provide the seed with its requisite nutrients; he must protect the growing tree from pests and contend with the harshness of the elements. Soon the gardener will have his tree, and in return for his labors the tree will give him shade and fruit.

So it is with the people’s belief. Once it is as strong as a mature tree, the courts can rely on the people’s faith to carry them through unjustified attacks.

How have we gone about earning the people’s trust?

To begin with, the Judiciary must command authority. Every single component of the Judiciary must exhibit competence. The Judiciary does not consist only of judges and Justices. Equally important in the administration of justice are the court personnel, from the lowliest utility worker to the most senior court clerk. Each of these public servants contributes to the more effective and efficient dispensation of justice, and all of them must render judicial services with utmost competence.

The quest for competence begins with direction, a map if you will. Upon my appointment as Chief Justice in 1998, I immediately wrote a vision-mission statement for the Judiciary; this would be the path the Judiciary would take during my tenure. This document is now fairly known among judges and personnel across the country as the Davide Watch.

The Davide Watch envisions a Judiciary that is independent, effective and efficient, and worthy of public trust and confidence; and a legal profession that provides quality, ethical, accessible and legal service to our people and is willing and able to answer the call to public service. It warns that dishonesty, immorality, incompetence, inefficiency, or any other form of unbecoming conduct is impermissible and will not be tolerated in the Judiciary and in the legal profession. Those who fail to meet the standards set for members of the Bench and Bar will be relentlessly weeded out.

The Davide Watch declares that the system of administration of justice must be geared to achieve the goal of delivering fair, impartial and swift justice; hence, the core values of the rule of law, equal justice, judicial independence and the pursuit of excellence should be preserved and at all times be predominant.

You will note that earning the people's faith and confidence is among the objectives of the Judiciary’s vision under the Davide Watch, and that such belief requires independence, effectiveness and efficiency on the part of the Judiciary. It is in the midst of all these demands that the Judiciary quests for competence.

Competence is manifested in two areas. First, as public servants, all members of the Judiciary must be so competent as to faithfully adhere to the command in the Philippine Constitution that a public office is a public trust.

Competence is also manifested as expertise in the dispensation of official duties. For judges, this means that they must be knowledgeable in both law and procedure, they must be familiar with recent legal developments as well as emerging trends. Recently, for example, Philippine judges have been required to acquaint themselves with electronic and genetic evidence, matters that were once confined to science fiction. Our judges are likewise keeping themselves abreast with international legal concepts such as the evolving definition of terrorism, economic and sociocultural rights, intellectual property, and many other unusual fields of law.

For court personnel, professional competence is exhibited as their mastery of their roles and functions in the administration of justice. Such mastery includes the recognition that they are part of a team with the singular objective of achieving justice for the people.

To develop competence in these two areas, the Supreme Court supports judges and personnel with a program of continuing education implemented by the Philippine Judicial Academy, the education arm of the Supreme Court. The Office of the Court Administrator, which is tasked with the supervision of all courts below the Supreme Court, meanwhile leads efforts to constitute each trial court as an efficient management team.

At the same time, in order to discourage judges and personnel from straying from the straight and narrow path of justice, the Supreme Court has aggressively cracked the whip on erring employees and officers. From 1999 to April 2002, three hundred sixty-one (361) magistrates have been disciplined for various offenses. The figure includes one Justice of the Supreme who was admonished and one Justice of the Court of Appeals who was dismissed from service seven (307) have been similarly disciplined in various degrees for different offenses from the years 1999 to 2002. As of 31 July 2002, one thousand four hundred fifty-three (1,453) administrative cases against judges and personnel are pending resolution by the Supreme Court.

I state these numbers not to indicate that the Philippine Judiciary is a den of hoodlums. Indeed, out of some 25,000 employees and officers, less than seven hundred (700) have been found guilty of one offense or another for a period spanning about three years. The bad eggs are an absolute minority. Instead I wish to emphasize that the Judiciary has not been sitting idly by in the face of proven charges against its members.

Thus do we plant the seed of credibility in the Judiciary: by ensuring that every judge and employee of the Judiciary will realize their full potential in the service of the people through the administration of justice. In this way we hope to assure the public that the courts will only render justice.

Yet as I stated earlier, we cannot compel this seed to become a tree. Our nurturing comes in the form of policies and programs to promote transparency of court processes. In recent years, the most determined step taken by the Supreme Court in this direction was the creation of the Public Information Office or PIO.

The PIO implements the Supreme Court’s information, education and communication plan, which seeks to educate the public about the role of the Judiciary in society, its powers and duties, as well as its limitations. The justice system is truly a mystery to the people, and it is high time we unraveled the mystery for their benefit.

Over and above these efforts is the comprehensive program for reform being undertaken by the Supreme Court. We are pursuing our Action Program for Judicial Reform or APJR, a six-year program outlining several initiatives that will revolutionize the delivery of justice in the country. The APJR covers administrative concerns, facility requirements, human resources development, and support systems among many other issues.

The APJR has six components. The component on Judicial Systems and Procedures concerns itself with the administration of cases and courts. Initiatives on alternative dispute resolution, computerized case management systems, streamlined court rules, and similar activities are programmed in this section.

Under the component Institutions Development, the Supreme Court seeks to establish mechanisms to strengthen the Judiciary as an institution independent from other branches of government. Here we will create systems to implement the constitutionally mandated fiscal autonomy of the Judiciary, improve judicial accountability, and devise personnel and financial policies that will give the Judiciary the flexibility needed to address the many demands upon it.

We will improve our human resource base, consisting of judges and court personnel, with the component on Human Resource Development. This segment covers the selection, hiring, education, promotion, and remuneration of the Judiciary’s manpower, the very engines which make the wheels of justice turn.

The component on Institutional Integrity Development addresses concerns about graft and corruption in the Judiciary and places mechanisms to detect and punish corrupt practices in the Bar and Bench, while the portion on Access to Justice by the Poor ensures that the marginalized and the vulnerable sectors will always have affordable but efficient means of attaining justice.

The last component, Reform Support Systems, installs the mechanisms to ensure the sustainability of the reform efforts. The focus of this component is public education, information and communication; the principle being that generating public awareness and understanding of the functions and role of the Judiciary in society will encourage the people to support and advocate for the courts.

The Action Program was approved by the Philippine Supreme Court last 8 December 2000. Since then we have made significant steps towards its realization. To minimize the financial impact of these activities on the Judiciary, we are engaging in partnerships with international organizations such as the United Nations, the World Bank, the Asian Development Bank, the agencies for development assistance of the governments of Canada, Japan, Australia, and the United States.

Several activities have already been undertaken towards the realization of the APJR. Some have been accomplished using only the resources of the Supreme Court, while many others have been made possible with the support of various donor organizations.

A few examples include the publication of the Benchbook for Trial Court Judges, a handy manual on court proceedings to help judges quickly decide contentious issues, either during trial or in chambers while the judge is conducting research. Early this year the Supreme Court completed the purchase and distribution of one thousand five hundred (1,500) computer units to trial courts all over the country. Coupled with legal research software, the computers are expected to facilitate the writing of court decisions and, therefore, accelerate the dispensation of justice.

We have instituted court-referred mediation as a faster, cheaper and less stressful way of resolving disputes. We have established partnerships with international institutions to allow an exchange of ideas on judicial concerns. The training of judges on novel issues of law has been realized with the help of international agencies, such as the United Nations Development Programme, which have provided not only funds for training but expert lecturers as well.

Policy-wise, the Supreme Court has promulgated several new procedural rules in light of numerous developments in law. For instance, in response to the transfer of jurisdiction on corporate disputes from the Securities and Exchange Commission to the Regional Trial Courts, the Supreme Court approved rules on intra-corporate disputes and corporate bankruptcy. Following the passage of the E-Commerce Law, the Supreme Court passed the rules on electronic evidence. Considering the heightened international concern for the rights of the child, the Supreme Court approved a range of procedural rules covering child witnesses and children in conflict with the law.

The Judicial and Bar Council, or JBC, has been continuously working to enhance transparency in its proceedings. The JBC is responsible for submitting nominees for judicial posts to the President, who would then appoint a judge, Justice, or an Ombudsman based on the list of nominees from the Council. As early as two years ago, the JBC published its rules to inform the public of the procedure followed in the screening of nominees to judicial posts. The Council is now deliberating on conducting public hearings to make the procedure absolutely transparent to the public.

The Supreme Court has also laid the foundation for an Information Systems Strategic Plan, or ISSP. The ISSP will rationalize the purchase and use of information technology resources for the various courts across the country. The Supreme Court is also formulating a disclosure policy, which will detail what information may be accessed by the public. This measure supports efforts at transparency and accountability while securing those matters which should remain confidential in the interest of justice.

The APJR recognizes that the justice system is not a monopoly of the Bench. The Bar, the body of law practitioners, also plays a significant role in the administration of justice. The Supreme Court has not neglected to require lawyers to continuously improve themselves. Erring lawyers have been disciplined, and the Court is seriously implementing the Mandatory Continuing Legal Education or MCLE program.

The MCLE requires lawyers to complete training hours in various law subjects, including legal ethics, before they can renew their licenses. The program will ensure that lawyers are kept abreast with developments in law and legal practice so that they may serve their clients, and the justice system, effectively and efficiently.

It is quite obvious that many projects under the APJR have already been completed. Yet, the reform program remains an ambitious initiative which will demand much from the Judiciary, and will also require the convergence of various sectors of society. The program will rely on the credibility of the Judiciary for its success, and its success will ensure the strengthening of that same credibility.

With all these efforts it is necessary to ask if the Philippine Judiciary has indeed been able to strengthen its credibility. Judging from news reports and public reactions, it appears we still have a long way to go. Many of the reports are unsubstantiated; thus, conclusions drawn from them are unjustified. Yet doubts remain in the public mind, and this becomes a cause for concern for all of us in the Judiciary. Such doubts affect the morale of all employees, especially those who adhere to every law and rule applicable to their work.

Rather than blame the public for drawing such conclusions, or condemn the media for making such reports, I would rather have the Supreme Court double its efforts at public education. Only when the public fully understands and appreciates the role of the Judiciary can we truly say that we have won the people's faith and trust. Until then, we in the Judiciary will continue to persevere in developing and exhibiting the competencies that will convince the people of our dedication to justice. This is a challenge I welcome and one which I am sure all judges and personnel are ready to face.

Thus have we waged our campaign to strengthen the credibility of the justice system. I feel that we need not restore something that was not lost, but at the same time the Judiciary cannot settle for anything less than the people's full faith and confidence.

I am taking this crusade another level higher today. I invite all of you here to inform us in the Judiciary of your concerns. I invite you moreover to participate in our efforts to enhance the administration of justice. This is the convergence I spoke of. The product of our shared labors will be the tree I mentioned earlier, the tree of justice that will not be felled by any challenge. That tree will be a Judiciary truly and eternally worthy of public trust and confidence, and it will be a bastion of justice for all.

Thank you very much.

God bless you all.

## Reactions

## I

## Chief Justice Davide's Lecture on the Credibility of Our System of Justice: A Comment

by

Sen. Jovito R. Salonga

There are several points in the lecture of Mr. Chief Justice Davide that may require more than a passing comment.

1. Public trust in the justice system, says the Chief Justice, may have waned in the past, but it was never lost, or else, anarchy would have reigned. Hence, his lecture on "strengthening" the credibility of the system rather than on its "restoration," since the latter would presume that its credibility was lost. The Chief Justice did not clarify his frame of reference when he talked about the past, but we may assume that he was probably talking about the justice system since the turn of the last century. With the establishment by the Americans of civil government in 1901, the Supreme Court was headed by Chief Justice Cayetano Arellano and his successors up to December 1941 when the Japanese invaders brought the country to a period of pitch darkness. Not should the years of martial law and authoritarian rule be included since under General Order No. 1 issued on September 21, 1972, former President Marcos assumed all powers of Government and placed all agencies and instrumentalities of Government under his direction and control. The Supreme Court was merely allowed to function; all the other judges were required to submit undated letters of resignation. This was the system that prevailed, more or less, until our people assumed the power to change their own destiny by means of People Power I.

But this merely underscores what I have stated elsewhere. One lesson we learn from People Power I and from People Power II is that a corrupt president and tarnished legislature may not necessarily mean the total collapse of democracy as long as we have a functioning system of justice which, despite its imperfections, remains credible because it can give justice and lend a measure of hope to our oppressed, exploited people.

Last April 2002, we in Kilosbayan and Bantay Katarungan felt it was our duty to expose, in the daily media and in our own monthly magazine the indiscriminate assaults of former President Estrada on the Supreme Court and on the Special Division of the Sandiganbayan. We stated that although we criticized some decisions of the Supreme Court and of the Sandiganbayan, we considered Erap Estrada’s assaults on the Judiciary and his smear against the person of Chief Justice Davide as “baseless and unfair.” I like to think that we have thereby earned the right, as I am wont to say, to "disagree without being disagreeable, to differ without being difficult."

2. The second point I want to stress is the intellectual honesty and candor of the Chief Justice. To the question as to whether the Philippine judiciary has been able to strengthen its credibility, he admits in all candor that "judging from news reports and public reaction, we still have a long way to go." He cites the number of magistrates who have been disciplined during the Davide Watch-three hundred sixty-one (361) judges all in all from 1999 to July 2002, including one Supreme Court Justice who was admonished and one Court of Appeals Justice who was dismissed. But like the statistics on criminality coming from the Philippine National Police which are regarded with skepticism, the Chief Justice says that "doubts remain in the public mind and this remains a cause for concern for all of us in the Judiciary." With due respect to the Chief Justice, the 361 disciplinary cases he cites a cannot possibly include: (1) the complaints against judges which are still pending; and (2) complaints from those who, due to their poverty or lack of connections or influence, believe they do not have any chance to prevail against Justices and judges who, in their perception, are powerful and wellconnected, despite the intrinsic merit of their legitimate grievances. In particular, the admonition of a Supreme Court Justice concerning the irregular conduct of the 1999 bar examinations is no cause for pride — it was just a slap on the wrist, which disappointed many principled lawyers and law students, including our Bantay Katarungan student monitors from five leading law schools.

3. In his August 21, 2002 speech before the American Chamber of Commerce, Mr. Justice Artemio Panganiban says that corruption, incompetence and delay are the three main problems besetting the judicial system. In particular, corruption, he said, is traceable to three "ships" - kinship, relationship, and friendship in other words, utang na loob and pakikisama, leading to influence-peddling, which we call lakaran and palakasan in Filipino. But that will bring us to an interesting subject which was left open by the lecture of the Chief Justice, namely, the conflict between values found in Philippine culture, i.e., our ways of living, our behavior, our habits and beliefs, and our strengths and weaknesses as a people, as against the Anglo-American values found in the Canons of Judicial Ethics which we imported and copied from the United States, based on the concepts of equality and fair play. These democratic concepts are oftentimes against our cultural values of utang na loob and pakikisama, which in turn lead to the practice of influence-peddling, known as lakaran and palakasan.

4. One weakness in the Filipino character is our world in extreme personalism. Filipinos tend to view the terms of personal relationships. It is not the merit of a cause, "not what you know, but whom you know" that matters in many cases. I recall that when my former law partner, Dr. Pedro L. Yap, was appointed Senior Justice and then Chief Justice of the Supreme Court, several close relatives asked me to intercede for them in several cases pending in the High Court. When I explained to them that this would be unethical, if not illegal, and that what they wanted me to do would lead to corruption, they were upset. I learned later that they told our other relatives and friends - "Mahirap lapitan iyan. Hindi pwedeng asahan." (He is difficult to approach; we can't depend on him.

5. In our culture, as analyzed in 1988 by ten eminent Filipino social scientists led by Dr. Patricia Licuanan, who put out a paper entitled "Building a People, Building a Nation," we want to establish personal relationships first and above all. We are uncomfortable with rules and regulations, especially standard procedures since they are generally impersonal. This explains why we are inclined to skirting the rules, ignoring them or claiming exemptions.

According to our Canons of Judicial Ethics, which we borrowed from the Anglo-American system of justice, a judge or justice should not allow outside matters or his private interests to interfere with the prompt and proper performance of his office. In every particular, his conduct should be above reproach (Canon 31). He should refrain, so far as reasonably possible, from all relations which would normally tend to arouse the suspicion that such relations warp or bias his judgment or prevent his impartial attitude of mind in the administration of his judicial duties (Canon 25). He should not accept any presents or favors from litigants or from lawyers practicing before him (Canon, 29). In pending or prospective litigation before him, he should be scrupulously careful to avoid such action as may reasonably tend to weaken the suspicion that his social or business relations or friendships constitute an element in determining his judicial course (Canon 30).

6. Last August 13, 2002, an article written by Ms. Jane Magturo - perhaps a pen name of someone who knows a lot about the Supreme Court and the Court of Appeals — appeared in Today, a national daily which has been publishing credible articles about the judiciary. Instead of writing about canons of ethics, the reporter cited specific cases which depict an ugly reality —the fixing of cases by Justices, incumbent and retired, inside the very temples of justice. Let m e take up some of these specific cases.

"In Metro Manila," says this article, "the more fixers of cases before courts — especially before the Court of Appeals and the Supreme Court -- are retired, even incumbent members of both tribunals. Only recently, an incumbent Court of Appeals Justice (whose name had been specifically mentioned several times in the same newspaper) was charged by a litigant before the Supreme Court for being ‘a power broker and an influence peddler, engaging in the practice of case fixing." This CA Justice was nominated by the Judicial and Bar Council for the appellate court at the behest of a retired top official of the Supreme Court. What the article omitted to say is that the other CA Justice - who was also named in a column published the other day (August 31, 2002) by former Senior Justice Isagani to A. Cruz - had been allegedly bribed by the litigator for the tune of 1.5 million pesos but on a motion for reconsideration filed by the adverse party, the Bank of America, he set aside his own order and asked for a bigger amount to undo what he had done. The case against the two justices in the Court of Appeals filed by the litigator himself is now pending in the Supreme Court.

A reverse situation reportedly occurred not too long ago. A member of the Supreme Court (now retired) was exposed by an incumbent CA justice, Justice Cosico, for pressuring him to act in favor of a telecommunications firm. CA Justice Cosico, whose moral courage should be emulated, did not pull his punches — he even stated the facts and named the Supreme Court Justice in his decision on the case. Though the matter was reported in several newspapers, the High Court, under the former Chief Justice, apparently ignored the whole thing. The Supreme Court Justice who pressured Cosico has since retired from the Court but this is the paradox — through the courtesy of the former Chief Justice, this retired Justice of the Supreme Court who pressured Cosico was appointed and remains a key official of the Philippine Judicial Academy, or PHILJA, which is supposed to be the "training school for justices, judges, court personnel, lawyers and aspirants for judicial posts." Fortunately, Chief Justice Davide is now in a position to do something about this aberration.

7. Let me go back to the unusual Filipino cultural value of gratitude. There is nothing wrong with gratitude — in fact, it is an admirable virtue in all cultures. But because of our extreme personalism, we tend to give undue favors to the members of our family, our own relatives, our "brods" from the same fraternity or class. Some Filipino judges and justices, like many Filipinos, go to the very extreme in expressing their gratitude which they like to call pagtanaw ng utang na loob (paying a debt from within, which can never be fully paid). But unless we restrain our generous impulses and practise the virtue of moderation, we may find it difficult to strengthen the credibility of our judiciary. To paraphrase the erudite Learned Hand, without the virtues of self-restraint, self-discipline and moderation, Constitutions, statutes, and courts could become worse than useless. They could represent nothing but false hopes.

8. In the Action Program for Judicial Reform, Chief Justice Davide's vision is of "a judiciary that conducts its business with dignity, integrity, accountability and transparency." In his Message to Identify Judicial Reform Priorities, the Chief Justice stated: "National governmental reforms must start somewhere, and where better than in the Judiciary, the alpha and omega of justice and fair play.

Spoken by others, these statements would be nothing but empty rhetoric. The good thing is that the Chief Justice is our role model in discarding our cultural weaknesses as a people. He was the very example of dignity and moral integrity when he presided at the Impeachment Trial of former President Estrada who had appointed him Chief Justice on November 30, 1998. And only a few days ago, when there was a motion to reopen the proceedings of the Judicial and Bar Council, of which he is the Chair, to enable the incumbent president to add the name of a former presidential legal counsel who did not get the required number of votes, he hit the meddling of Malacañang in the decisions of the JBC and said emphatically on television - "It cannot be done. In no way, in no way, for as long as I am the Chairman of the JBC."

Many responsible quarters and distinguished authorities, including Father Joaquin Bernas, S.J. have urged the Judicial and Bar Council be abolished as its proceedings have not been fair and transparent. But abolition will require an amendment of the Constitution, which is unlikely in the next few years. In our last Monitoring and Evaluation Report on the Aspirants for the Two Vacancies in the Supreme Court dated July 23, 2002, we in Bantay Katarungan gave the highest accolade to those who - apart from their remarkable competence, moral integrity, and independence of mind — did not approach JBC members, Supreme Court Justices or VIPs to help them. We also stated that where there is authentic documentary evidence against a particular aspirant, "a mere interview by the JBC members will not suffice..." We are gratified by the news that beginning September 1, 2002, interviews of aspirants for the Supreme Court and the Ombudsman “shall be open to the public," as proposed by Senator Pangilinan, a member of the JBC. After the other JBC members agreed with his proposal, the Senator said: “This is a timely decision, especially now that the public demands transparency in the government, not to mention the diminishing reputation of the Judiciary" (Today, August 15, 2002). Let us hope and pray that in the forthcoming proceedings of the Judicial and Bar Council, utang na loob, pakikisama, lakaran, and palakasan — these cultural weaknesses which, in our view, have tainted its performance — will now give way to real merit.

For doing what he had stated, namely, that he would not allow Malacañang to meddle with the decisions of the JBC as long as he is the Chairman, we thank the Chief Justice: he is the living example of what he preaches. We extend to him our best wishes.

## II

## "Tree of Justice" Won't Grow on Arid Soil

by

Malou Mangahas

Philippine Center for Investigative Journalism

The GODS of Padre Faura. That is how we journalists, in both awe and scorn, refer to the justices of the Philippine Supreme Court, the off-white edifice that stands on rustic Padre Faura Street in the capital city of Manila.

In every sense of the word, the men and women of the high tribunal are Gods in our midst. Or could be Gods. Or over the many constitutional and legal debates that have embroiled this nation over the last century played God.

But there are false Gods, and there are true Gods. There are those who inspire full faith and respect, and faith that the courts should stand for in our lives.. There are Gods who build, and Gods who destroy the courts.

In Padre Faura's pantheon of Gods, one stands out as most venerable - Chief Justice Hilario Davide Jr., this year’s Awardee for Government Service of the Ramon Magsaysay Foundation.

The editors of the Philippine Center for Investigative Journalism are abundantly stingy with praise for public officials. In my life, as a journalist, I could count by the fingers of one hand Filipinos in public service that inspire absolute respect in our people. Mr. Davide belongs to this circle of few.

I had the good fortune of covering Mr. Davide at work, as a member of the Commission that drafted the 1987 Constitution, as head of the Presidential Fact-Finding Commission that looked into the failed coups that hobbled the Aquino administration and as chairman of the Commission on Elections.

I last saw him as presiding justice of the Senate impeachment trial where, leave aside the Cebuano accent, "Your Honor" helped demystify the jargon of the bench and bar. Who could forget subpoena duces tecum and subpoena ad testificandum? Even more important, he exemplified work ethic, courtesy, decency, humility, probity and competence in public service.

Because I have been generous with praise for him, I hope Mr. Davide would not mind now if I should sound a bit insolent in my response to his presentation. My great blessing is I am not a lawyer and therefore do not fall under the discipline of the high court.

Credibility, Mr. Davide says, is the authority to inspire belief. He calls the credibility of the courts — or the people's trust in the judiciary — the most important thing of all, the antidote to chaos and people taking the law into their own hands.

In his view, the courts' credibility rests on the competence, mastery of procedures, unflinching dedication to the Constitution, and fairness of all court personnel, down to the lowest utility worker. While that credibility has not been lost, it has waned and must be refurbished, he adds.

According to Mr. Davide, the court's credibility, much like planting a tree, is vulnerable to "harsh elements." The thoughtful gardener must thus assure adequate sunlight and water so this "tree of justice" will grow sturdy roots and branches. Mr. Davide is in fact a green thumb who turns to gardening in his spare time, court sources reveal.

The chief justice's analogy is also a riddle on two counts.

First, who should plant this tree of justice? I believe Mr. Davide does not wish to cast lawyers and justices alone in stellar roles as gardeners, and leave us the citizens applauding, or crying, from the sidelines.

Today, transparency, efficiency, and honesty drive the courts' reform agenda under M.r Davide but there was a time, not too long ago, when even the high tribunal was called "the best Supreme Court that money can buy" and judges, "hoodlums in robes." Some of predecessors were of the kind few journalists called “Your Honor" in all sincerity. The appellation "Your Horror" seemed more appropriate.

Just last month, Mr. Davide's court stopped the national association of judges from soliciting corporate and private donations to a golf tournament conceived supposedly to raise funds for retired members of the bench.

According to the Code of Judicial Conduct, judges and justices should be "the embodiment of competence, integrity and independence." Drafted by a 12-person committee in 1969 on directive of the Supreme Court, the Code states that magistrates should "avoid impropriety and the appearance of impropriety in all activities."

Yes, Mr. Davide has set the right tone at the top but across the board, we see a mixed picture of the good, the bad and the not so pretty in the judiciary.

In journalism, we speak of credibility often in tandem with another big word - ethics. Credibility is what people say we are, and ethics, what kind of people we are.

Ethics - practices and behavior based on accepted beliefs and available choices — is a lot of things to a lot of people. Some say Ethics has roots in two domains: morality and the law.

Morality as in both faith and culture, the canons, values, beliefs, customs and traditions that define our character as a people. And Law, as in the Constitution, jurisprudence, ordinances, the government, the police, the prosecution service, the courts.

If we must talk about planting a tree of justice, a tree of credible courts, we must also talk about reviewing the ethics of the courts, which reflect in large measure our ethics as a people. While anti-graft laws define how public officials should behave, people have far more exacting expectations of judges and justices. Brilliant and competent are not enough attributes of the ideal officer of the court. Honesty is the greater expectation. Like Caesar's wife, judges and justices are expected not only to be above the level of corruption, but also above suspicion of corruption.

That is not quite the case yet, even as Mr. Davide in all candor says that an absolute minority - or 700 of the 25,000 court personnel — have been disciplined for misconduct. It must be stressed that a majority of them are senior officers of the court. But a graver , if subtler, misconduct unfolding every day is the unseemly high profile in public life some justices and judges maintain. They play golf and tennis with litigants, serve in corporate boards, or even join political parties, and see nothing wrong with all this.

It does not help bolster the independence of the courts that some appointments by the Judicial and Bar Council seem so driven by politics and personal loyalty to the sitting president or politicians from the Executive branch and Congress. Politics remains a stain on the ethics of the courts.

Even worse, it does not help foster respect for the rule of law and due process when Presidents — not just police officers anymore - have started to parade suspects as if they have been convicted of their alleged offenses. For that inimitable "photo op" apparently in aid of reelection, politicians have started to milk due process and the rule of law dry.

M.r Davide's analogy is a riddle for a second reason. On which soil, or ground, should we plant our tree? What, or where, is that soil in fact?

Any gardener knows that if we plant on rich, good soil, our tree will grow. But what if our ground is fallow, and our soil arid? I wonder if soil refers in this instance to the role or meaning — or lack of role or meaning — the courts play in our lives.

Most Filipinos from birth to death would never have any need or occasion to step into a courtroom. And when they do, it is often because they have been wronged, or they seek to right wrong. And so while poverty rankles as the age-old scourge of 40 percent of our people, injustice, the lack of speedy, impartial justice, diminishes us all.

The people expect much less of the Executive and Congress, where it is generally assured that leaders are often only as strong or as weak as their ambition, wealth, political machine, and propensity for corruption. The failure of the Executive and Congress may be explained in part as a failure of persons, human error.

In contrast, officers of the courts are measured against established jurisprudence, the laws, the Constitution. When the courts fall short, the results are far more devastating. The failure of the courts is often viewed as a failure of law and order, a systems failure.

The expectation is that the courts must be a paragon of all of the positive things we aspire for as a people - honest, independent, competent, fair.

But do we in fact accord the courts a paramount role in our lives, a fertile ground where a tree of justice could flourish? It looks like unless we file suit or are sued, the courts do not figure at all in our daily lives.

For one, we do not understand the clubby Latin chatter of lawyers and judges. For another, we have not taken pains to understand the courts and live by its lessons.

In history and civics classes, we don't ever teach our children the rule of law or due process. Instead, we hold them in thrall of our tradition of revolution and revolts. We tell them about the exploits — and betrayals —of our politicians. We train them to think in terms of good and bad, right and wrong, but hardly ever, legal and illegal, their rights and responsibilities as citizens before the law. We make them memorize the names  and personal histories of our presidents, and grasp the powers of the Executive and Congress. And as an aside, only as an aside, we give them an idea that yonder lies yet a third branch of government — the courts.

Our children grow up with a vague concept of the rule of law, and worse, the movies and the media of the project a flawed public construct of the law — that of a glib lawyer in dapper suit, but not of a quiet, plodding, reflective justice, not the majesty of the law. Our basic instinct is not to follow the law but to find loop holes. successful lawyers.

A tree grows or dies on account of more than just water and sunlight. We must all be gardeners of our tree of justice. We must rein in the worms and pests that threaten its growth. Most important of all, we must plant it on soil as fertile as the heart and mind of every Filipino man, woman and child.

If only Mr. Davide and the Gods of Padre Faura could, perhaps we would wish that out of nothing they built our tree of justice for us. But like us, they are Gods with feet of clay. They can create only that which we give them; indeed, we deserve not just the government but also the courts that we have.

But while all other institutions may falter and fail, for all our sakes, the rule of law, due process and justice must triumph. Chief Justice Hilario Davide Jr. gives us at least one good reason to keep faith in time - hopefully in our time — our tree of justice will yield firmer roots and stronger branches.
