# Chapter 5: Excellence Is Not Enough

Thank you, Dr. Sonia Yuson de Leon, president of the Phi Kappa Phi Honor Society, for inviting me to be your guest speaker in today’s Round Table Conference. I met Toti and her husband Gus during the late Fifties, when both were UP scholars and leaders of the Student Catholic Action. Then as now, they have been devoted to the call of the intellect and of the spirit.

As some of you may know, this is my second time to appear before Phi Kappa Phi; the first was on March 15, 1994, during your Diamond Year Round Table Symposium. You made me sweat and blush that time; nonetheless, I always consider it a great honor to be “roasted” by the intellectual elite of our country. And so here I am again, ready for your fire and coal.

Ladies and gentlemen, in its search for the “Person of the Century,” Time magazine came up with three finalists; first, Franklin D. Roosevelt who embodied the triumph of freedom over totalitarianism; second, Mohandas Gandhi who represented the crusade for civil and individual liberties; and third, Albert Einstein who personified advances in science and technology. All three were great nominees, but I think the eventual choice of Einstein was a triumph of pure excellence.

Undoubtedly, the contribution to civilization of the first two can never be overestimated in terms of humanizing man, but I believe that Einstein’s most famous equation in all of physics — energy equals mass multiplied by the speed of light squared or E=mc² — and his space-time discoveries provide a glimpse of the divine: the more we learn of the cosmos, the more we know that there is much more we do not know.

Einstein is known for his intellectual probe not only of the vast universe, but also of the minute atom; and light quanta which provide underpinnings for television, laser, the semi-conductor and, yes, the microchip — the “brain” of the new millennium. In this sense, Einstein and his successors are credited with the founding of the information age which now directly influences our lifestyles, habits, careers, social mores, cultures and even philosophies. I can spend the whole day theoretically explaining the impact of the computer, the Internet and the “dotcom” revolution, but that will bore you. Let me then illustrate some of these changes in the e-society in a more personal and experiential way.

## Personal Experiences in the e-Society

Last Christmas season (1999), my wife and I visited our children and grandchildren in the United States. You see, four of our five children live and work in New York City. Even while visiting that big distant city, | was up-to-date on Philippine current events, because the newscast “The World Tonight” was available on New York cable TV. Even more amazing, I could instantly read the latest news items appearing on our national dailies by simply accessing the Internet homepages of the Inquirer, the Star, or the Bulletin. In fact, I was able to read the news earlier than most Philippine readers.

You see, the news stories of these dailies are automatically posted on the Internet as they are being composed. By 12:00 midnight, Philippine time, all the stories are already written and are in the process of being printed by rolling printing presses. Hence, while in New York I was able to read the contents of the major Manila newspapers at 12:00 noon, New York time, which was 1:00 a.m. Manila time — hours before Philippine readers could purchase their printed copies. One day, I called a friend at 4:00 a.m., Manila time, and he was surprised I could quote verbatim over the phone the news headlines for that day.

After Christmas, my entire family planned to go to DisneyWorld in Florida. We were billeted at the Coronado Resort, which was a gigantic complex composed of several hotel buildings built around an artificial lake. Upon check-in, we were each given a small plastic card, similar to a credit card, which enabled us to access and enjoy all the one thousand and one facilities of the Magic Kingdom. It allowed us to charge any meal or shopping; to gain entry to any of the exhibition halls, amusement centers and studio rides in the various theme parks; to rent battery-operated people movers, motorboats and quadcycles; and to buy or use practically anything in DisneyWorld, a virtual entertainment city larger than Makati.

In May this year (2000), my wife and I toured Turkey for two weeks. I was amazed that through my mobile phone issued in Manila, I could call and send text messages anywhere and at any time during our bus tour of more than 2,000 kilometers in that country.

## Research Made Easy in the e-Age

Decades ago, I was taught in school that if I wanted information on a specific topic, I needed to research volumes of encyclopedia with millions of words and thousands of illustrations, This method of research has been rendered obsolete by Encarta, a single-ounce CD-Rom,‘ which includes “26,000 topics, nine million words of text, eight hours of sound, 7,000 photographs, 800 maps, 250 interactive charts and 100 animations and video clips. It costs less than $100.”

Even Encarta and its CD-Rom cousins will be phased out, once broad-band fiber optics are connected to home computers. By that time, the Internet will become the source of libraries, current events, movie, sports, shopping and entertainment; yes, it will have information on every phase of life, work and leisure.

In the face of these informational and technological advances, our country, sadly enough, is still struggling in its agricultural and industrial transformation. Its socio-economic and political systems are still mired in the same maladies that have plagued it for over a century: poverty, rebellion, corruption and criminality. It seems that these maladies have even worsened over the decades, resulting in stagnation in our economic, social, cultural and political lives.

By their conference theme, the International Honor Society of Phi Kappa Phi and the Civil Service Commission have singled out “Excellence in Government Service” as a major solution to our national ills and as the “software,” as it were, to propel us into the e-age. I partially agree. I believe however, that excellence should not be confined to government, but to anything or everything we say or do even as plain citizens and as private individuals. More than that, I say that excellence is not enough. I explained during my commencement address last April 26, 2000, before graduates of the University of the East, that aside from excellence we need two other E words; namely, ethics and eternity. Let me take up each of these three Es.

## A Passion for Excellence

According to the Merriam-Webster International Dictionary, excellence “indicates a high degree or highest degree of good qualities; of qualities that make for especial worth or merit, x x x.” It is synonymous with “virtue or perfection.”

Excellence is a value that demands the best performance from all of us all the time. True, not everyone has been endowed with the same amount of gray matter or artistic talent or motor skill or athletic prowess, but it is our responsibility to develop to the maximum whatever natural gift we have been given by the Lord. By becoming experts in our chosen lines or careers, we become self-confident and self-assured. Excellence in everything we say or do becomes our source of self-dignity, self-worth and self-fulfillment.

Again, I would like to speak intimately from the heart. Let me relate my only son’s quest for excellence. After high school, he finished a four-year course in management engineering in Ateneo, then he honed his piano talent with a bachelor’s degree in music from UP, summa cum laude. Later, he took advantage of a German government scholarship and finished a graduate piano course in Munich. After his German studies, he went to Stanford University for further studies — this time no longer in music, but in the sentences. After a year, he obtained a master’s degree i) Operations research. Not satisfied, he took up another master’s degree and, after several more years of studying, graduated in June 1999 with a Ph.D. in Engineering Economic Systems with the coveted Stanford Centennial Award for excellence as a teaching Assistant.

During all those long years of schooling, I frankly began to worry about him, because he might have been overdoing his search for academic excellence. I thought that seventeen years of studying after high school and finishing two bachelor’s and three master’# degrees and then a doctoral degree simply took too long. I thought that instead of just enrolling in various universities, however great, he should have spent half of those seventeen years to build a career, a family and a niche in the real world.

I was thinking about my own student days. After high school, I took up only two years of preparatory law and four years of legal studies. Thereafter | plunged immediately into building my career = starting as a lowly associate at the law office of my former dean, at the same time teaching law and dabbling in business. At age 36, I was already fairly established: I had my own modest law firm, my own little business, my wife and five young children, my own house and my first Mercedes Benz.’ I continued to inch up slowly in my career ladder until I reached the summit as a Supreme Court justice.

I was worried that at the same age of 36, my only son would just be out of school and just about to begin his career, still single and still penniless, while many _ of his high school chums were already well-settled in their own careers, homes and family lives.

But my fears suddenly vanished when, right out of Stanford, my son was swamped with tempting offers. Unlike me, his entrance into his chosen career did not begin at the bottom, but immediately at the top — as vice president for information technology at the New York-based Bear Stearns, one of the biggest investment banks in the United States. Now I no-

longer worry. I now know he will have his own career, family and home at his own pace and his own time, in a manner I hope the Phi Kappa Phi, of which he is a member, will be proud of. He had his own way and I had mine.

What do our contrasting career paths prove? I believe they show that there are various ways of achieving excellence. My son’s career path was one of undertaking long formal education and then beginning right at the top of his chosen profession; mine consisted of taking up a short basic schooling, then starting at the bottom of my chosen field, climbing the ladder step by step until finally reaching the top. Which is the better way? You tell me!

## Excellence in the Humanities, Specialization in Careers

Whatever be your answer to my last question, the point is: excellence in the highly accelerative e-age is a must. “Education [is] the center of the knowledge society and schooling its key institution.” Social thinkers from Wells to Toynbee have repeatedly argued that the global society is in a race between education and catastrophe. Those stakes become higher as the new century dawns, because the computer, the Internet and the dotcom mania have compressed the earth into one global village where one’s fate is determined by one’s ability to cope.

We need a general background in the humanities, social sciences, history and current events, even as we specialize in a chosen field. This combination of excellence in the arts, literature and the humanities in general, combined with specialized knowledge in one discipline is not really new. In describing an ideal magistrate, one of the most eminent jurists in the United States, Judge Learned Hand, declared:

“T venture to believe that it is as important to a judge called upon to pass on a question of constitutional law, to have a bowing acquaintance with Acton and Maitland, with Thucydides, Gibbon and Carlyle, with Homer, Dante, Shakespeare and Milton, with Machiavelli, Montaigne and Rabelais, with Plato, Bacon, Hume and Kant as with books that have been specifically written on the subject. For in such matters, everything turns upon the spirit in which he approaches the question before him. The words he must construe are empty vessels into which he can pour nearly everything he will. Men do not gather figs of thistles, nor supply institutions from judges whose outlook is limited by parish or class. They must be aware that there are before them more than verbal problems; more than final solutions cast in generalizations of universal applicability. They must be aware of the changing social tensions in every society which make it an organism; which demand new schemata of application; which will disrupt it, if rigidly confined.””°

I dare say that a similar statement can be made of a good physician, scientist, philosopher, thinker or educator. Excellence demands a combination of deep general knowledge and a mastery of a particular or specialized branch of knowledge. In short, excellence in the humanities and specialization in a career are demanded by the e-society.

## Ethical Principles, Not Image-Building Tactics

Let me now discuss the second E which stands for ethics. Every profession has a code of ethics. All these codes, or canons as we prefer to call them in the legal profession, are in turn based on character ethic, on “old-fashioned” principles like integrity, humility, fidelity, courage, prudence and hard work. These are basic norms that must be internalized as the primary sources of true success and enduring happiness. All great persons, heroes and saints have integrated these principles deep within themselves and have depended on them in times of stress and crisis.

I must, however, distinguish ethical values from image-building tactics. Nowadays, we frequently hear of public personalities who are advised to change their images in order to win elections and public acceptance. So, they employ so-called public relations experts who manipulate and sometimes deceive others to promote their clients’ interests. Some public figures are projected as great orators, when they merely read or memorize ghost-written speeches; others are pictured as charitable persons giving food to flood victims and Alri to the poor, when in truth they are merely dishing out crumbs from ill-gotten money; still others are hailed as war heroes, when they were nowhere near the battle sites; and there are those who are promoted as model husbands or wives and parents, when all the while they are cohabiting with several lovers with whom they have several children.

Such duplicity is fundamentally flawed. People will eventually see through it. It cannot be the source of success. Truth will always triumph, and deception will be discovered and condemned. At the very least, those who seek recourse to it know that they are not what they pretend to be. Hence, they feel hollow and cheap. In the language of the young, they are “plastic.”

## A Change in Men More Than a Change of Men

Indeed, to change society, to change our government, what we need is not just a change of men but a change in men. A change in persons is possible only if they accept and practise these ethical values, if they begin the change from inside their hearts, if they change their old habits and embrace ethical principles and values.

Some people are intrigued when they see others become rich, famous or talked about as role models; or when they simply have happy homes, happy marriages and successful children. So they ask, “How did you do it? Give me some quick advice so I can solve my own predicaments and succeed like you. Is there a motivating seminar or yoga class you can recommend?”

Motivational speeches and quick-fix solutions, like Band Aid and aspirin, have their limited uses in easing pain or its symptoms, but the real solution lies deep inside each of us — patiently cultivating and actually living the basic ethical values I mentioned earlier.

In the final analysis, what we are communicates far more eloquently than anything we say or do. In the words of Emerson, “What you are shouts so loudly in my ears I cannot hear what you say.” Indeed, a swindler cannot talk about integrity, a chatterbox cannot teach about contemplative life, and an atheist cannot preach about God.

There are people we trust because we know their character, because we know them to be honest, fair and gentle. They may not be eloquent, they may not know public relations techniques, they may not know how to polish our egos, but we trust them because they have integrity and fidelity.

## Eternity — Our Final Destiny

Let me now discuss the third and final E, which stands for eternity. I was born and raised poor. I sold newspapers, shined shoes and peddled textbooks to my classmates to support my education. I could not study in the school of my choice, UP, because my father could not afford the then 15-centavo bus fare between UP Diliman and our rented apartment in Cataluña Street, Sampaloc, Manila. I was educated in public elementary and high schools and in a nonsectarian university, FEU. In short, I had no catechetical background. My education was purely secular. My real introduction to God came rather late in my life — only some 15 years ago.

But now I know and I believe. Just as there are human and physical laws, there are also spiritual laws. And just as there are consequences for violations of human and physical laws, there are penalties for the transgression of God’s commands. For example, civil laws protect contracts, and a party who breaks them may be liable for damages. In the same manner, one who injures another violates criminal laws and is jailed as a consequence. So too, a violation of the laws of physics impels consequences; hence, when one jumps out of a window, one falls in accordance with the law of gravity.

By the same token, spiritual laws operate on our spirits, whether we like it or not. When we transgress the Ten Commandments or the Beatitudes proclaimed by our Lord Jesus, our spirits suffer the necessary consequences by being damned in eternity. Just as there are reparations for violations of human and physical laws, there is also salvation from our sins — by repentance, restitution and reformation."

Never for a moment believe that our lives on earth are the only ones we have been gifted with. Rather, believe that there is life after death, that there is eternity where our final destiny lies. Friends, let me assure you: God watches over all of us and awaits us in eternity. We may not see or hear Him, but He is present here today. He is amongst us, above us to inspire us, before us to lead us, beside us to guide us, behind us to protect us, and within us - in our hearts and our spirits - to possess us and bring us to His everlasting Kingdom.

## Accountability and Transparency in Public Service

Ladies and gentlemen, your conference theme is not only about excellence (to which I add ethics and eternity), but also about accountability and transparency in public service. I fully agree this time: indeed, public officials, as distinguished from private citizens, must be infused not only with excellence, ethics and eternity, but also with transparency in their public and private lives. Even for the competent and the well-meaning, transparency is a must and a burden. In public service, there is no such thing as a private deal, no such thing as an under-the-table transaction. The Constitution and the law subject to scrutiny all transactions involving public interest and impose transparency and accountability as desirable standards of public service.

Hence, in its Declaration of Principles and State Policies, the Constitution mandates that “[s]ubject to reasonable conditions prescribed by law, the State adopts and implements a policy of full public disclosure of all its transactions involving public interest.” It also recognizes “[t]he right of the people to information on matters of public concern” and, in more commanding language, it ordains that “[p]ublic officers and employees must at all times be accountable to the people x x x.”

Thus, the Ethical Standards Law (RA 6713) imposes penal and administrative sanctions on government officials who violate disclosure provisions of the Constitution and specific provisions of this law. Such sanctions include fine, imprisonment, dismissal from office and disqualification from holding future public positions.

## Epilogue: GG=TE³

During the last twenty-five or so minutes, I have tried to discuss what I believe are the essentials of good governance; namely, excellence, ethics and eternity, with a liberal dose of transparency. To help you remember, I have coined a formula or equation reminiscent of Einstein; thus, “GG=TE³” or good governance equals transparency multiplied by excellence, ethics and eternity.

Before I conclude, I would like to propose a practical discussion topic for all of you or even a project for Phi Kappa Phi and the Civil Service Commission. Perhaps, as a distinct result of this conference, you could conduct a Search for Ideal Public Servants, past and present, who personify our equation of GG=TE³. Off-hand, I can start the search by proposing former Senate President Jovito R. Salonga.

Senator Salonga is excellent. He was Number One in the bar examinations, finished his Bachelor of Laws at UP, his Master of Laws at Harvard, and his doctorate at Yale with honors; was elected three times as senator and, in each election, he was Number One in the list of winners. He was very ethical both in the practice of his profession and in his conduct as a public official. Upon reassuming public office after the EDSA revolution, he voluntarily dissolved his very prestigious law office; he did not merely take a leave of absence, as others did. He believes in eternity — he is a leader of the Cosmopolitan Church and a firm follower of our Lord Jesus Christ in thought, word and deed. He could have enriched himself — no, not at the expense of the public treasury, but by his practice of law; nonetheless, he chose to be poor by giving his legal services for free to the disadvantaged and oppressed, even up to now that he has retired from active public and professional life. And, yes, he is an ardent advocate of transparency and accountability; in fact, while he was a senator he authored the Ethical Standards Law.

He was my professor and dean in law school, but more than that, my guru in a life worth living, both for the here and now, as well as for the hereafter and forever. That we are of different religious faiths, he being a devout Protestant and I a fledgling Catholic, has not adversely affected our more than four decades of enriching friendship and my own regard and esteem for him as the personification of the equation GG=TE’.

I close my address with a hope and a prayer that each one of you in the audience today —- some 300 strong — will demand from our public officials an approximation of GG=TE. It is also my hope that you will be able to prepare an honor roll of TE? officials, reminiscent of Longfellow’s “Psalm of Life,” which we memorized in high school:

Lives of great men all remind us

We can make our life sublime

And, departing, leave behind us

Footprints on the sands of time.

I thank you.
