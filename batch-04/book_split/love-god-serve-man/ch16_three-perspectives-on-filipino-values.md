# Chapter 16: Three Perspectives on the Role of Filipino Values in Economic Development

Editor's note: To celebrate its 60th anniversary, the Honor Society of Phi Kappa Phi, University of the Philippines Chapter, called a roundtable conference to discuss the role of traditional values in shaping economic development. Dr. Felipe Landa Jocano, a professor of the UP Asian Studies Center, was the presenter. Five outstanding scholars were asked to be reactors: Local Government Secretary Rafael Alunan, journalist Loren Legarda-Leviste, UP Prof. Belen T. G. Medina [Department of Sociology], UP Prof. Natalia Lourdes M. Morales [Department of Political Science] and Atty. Artemio V. Panganiban. The conference was held on March 15, 1994 at the UP Law Center. The author showed his versatility by presenting his paper from three perspectives: as a Catholic lay leader, as a part-time businessman and as a full-time practising lawyer. The editor recommends a close reading of this paper.)

The thesis of Dr. F. Landa Jocano is that "traditional values have positive roles in stimulating industrialization and that lack of appreciation of these roles is what impedes rapid economic growth." It is difficult to disagree with the first part, i.e., that "traditional values have positive roles in stimulating industrialization." What could become controversial is the rather absolutist and exclusivistic implication of the second part, i.e., that this "lack of appreciation of these roles" is the single force or matter that inhibits rapid economic development. Dr. Jocano approached the problem from that of an anthropologist. Let me give my reaction to his paper from my perspectives as a Catholic layman, as a part-time businessman, and finally, as a full-time practising lawyer.

The Catholic Solution First, as a Catholic lay person, let me say that like Dr. Jocano, the Catholic Church looks into our history and our values in its own study of why our country, which is so rich in potential, is so poor in reality and why our Christian people, so beholden presumably to a loving and sharing God, are so afflicted with violence, poverty and injustice.

## The Second Plenary Council

of the Philippines In early 1991 (January 20 to February 17, 1991), the Catholic Church convened the Second Plenary Council of the Philippines or PCP II to find out how the Church could be more relevant to the country and to the people. PCP II was the equivalent of a Constitutional Convention or a law-making Parliament. It was called "to renew the Church," or in more "laymanish" language, to chart new directions and pass new laws for the Church and its people. PCP II is significant because it was the first time in the 2,000-year history of Catholicism that laymen and laywomen (and for that matter, ordinary priests) took part in the "renewal" or lawmaking of the Church. Heretofore, only cardinals and bishops participated in these councils. I consider myself fortunate to have been one of these lay delegates in PCP II.

In recognizing the role of values in influencing social, economic and political change, PCP II declared: "We see today, especially after EDSA, a strong concern for broad social change. These past few years the realization has grown that change in social structures is not enough without change too in cultural values. In the soul-searching on reasons why we seem to be unable to move on our besetting social problems, there has been a tendency to look inward at the values that motivate us

and to blame them as the main hindrances to movement.

Possibly it is more correct to say it is not our values so much that are at fault as the exaggeration and the wrong uses we have put them to. For it is precisely in their excess and abuse that our social ills arise." (Acts and Decrees of the Second Plenary Council of the Philippines, CBCP, 1992, par. 20)

"It is in the field of economics and politics that we see most clearly how our socio-cultural conditioning - or, more correctly, its negative aspects - strongly affects behavior. It leads to, in a word, inequalities, often of the grossest kind." (PCP II, 23).

"Thus, on the economic side: The poverty and destitution of the great mass of our people are only too evident, contrasting sharply with the wealth and luxury of the relatively few families, the elite top of our social pyramid. And on the political side: Power and control are also elitist, lopsidedly concentrated on established families that tend to perpetuate themselves in political dynasties." (PCP II, 24)

Faith Must Be Inculturated in the People's Value System And even while Catholicism is deemed universal and while the truths it espouses transcend time and geography, PCP II recognized the importance of inculturating faith in the people's value system. Said PCP II: "For faith to mature in love, it must be interiorized. Church teachings and practices must be personally appreciated and appropriated by us, as a people with our

own particular cultures, with our own ways of thinking and valuing. Faith must take root in the matrix of our Filipino being so that we may truly believe and love as Filipinos. But for this to happen, the Gospel must be presented with tools, methods and expressions coming from the culture itself.

It must be accepted within a person's cultural heritage. This is what we mean by an inculturated faith, a faith that is transmitted and expressed through our people's culture or cultures." (PCP II, 72)

A 'Sick Society' That

Professes to Follow Christ Eighty-three percent of the Filipino people are Catholics. In fact, if we include our non-Catholic Christians, we can say that over 90 percent of Filipinos profess to know, to follow and to serve Jesus Christ; the God who preached sharing of resources with the poor and the oppressed; the God who stigmatized as a fool the rich man who had thoughts only for himself and his possessions; the God who urged His followers to "give unto Caesar what is Caesar's and to God what is God's"; the God who condemned murder, violence, adultery, robbery, corruption and theft as unworthy of His Kingdom; and the God who taught His disciples to "love your enemies, do good to those who hate you, bless those who curse you, and pray for those who mistreat you."

Indeed, the Philippines is proud to be the only Christian nation in Asia. Why then is it also the poorest, most violent, most graft-ridden and most economically depressed? To quote the sad observation of PCP II, "(W)hile the vast majority of our people are Catholics and our churches are filled on Sundays, our society remains a sick society." (PCP II, 192)

The Church's answer to this paradox is that Christians lead dichotomized lives: they are Christians only in their thoughts, but

not in their actions; Christians only in their faith, but not in their deeds; Christians only while worshipping in church, but not while working in their offices and particularly in their government jobs; Christians only in their thoughts, but not in their works. Hence, PCP II called for a massive re-evangelization of our people as the solution to our national traumas and problems. There is an urgent need to re-educate our people so they may know and follow Jesus; trace His footsteps; utter His words to others; do His deeds; love with His love; and live with His life in our time and country.

## Total Integral Development

PCP II, furthermore, decreed that the Church shall "promote total human development, integral liberation, justice and peace in society and the integrity of creation." (PCP II, 192)

PCP II also underscored the need for man's total integral development. Simply put, this means that a Christian should act out his faith in all aspects of his life, whether spiritual, temporal, economic, social, cultural or political. In other words, a Christian's integral development requires him to apply gospel values wherever he is and whatever he does - whether at home, in school, in the street or in the office; or particularly when elected to public office.

Hence, the Church must be wherever there is sinfulness, persecution, injustice and violation of human dignity. It is concerned not only with the "life hereafter" but also with the temporal life, here and now.

It is in its work of temporal liberation that the men and women of the Church are called to inculturate and use new methods, new fervor and new expressions of faith and life so they could be understood and internalized by our masses.

The reforms of PCP II are to be implemented over the next 10 years from 1991. So, by the year 2001, most of these reforms,

including the new theology of temporal liberation and the inculturation of new methods and expressions of evangelization, should be in place.

## A Business Perspective

Now let me give a few comments from a businessman's point of view. Dr. Jocano says that historically, our attempts to industrialize began in 1898 when the Americans came. But almost a century thereafter we have not been able to develop. Today, in 1994, the Philippines remains the "sick man" of Asia. Examining the last 100 years, Dr. Jocano says that we as a people did not lack leaders or visions or technological capabilities and concludes that the problem boils down to a matter of values.

The Need to Focus on the Last 25 Years Let me say that, compared to our Asian neighbors, we did not fare so badly until the late '60s. It is really during the last 25 years that our national poverty became truly unbearable, that our national debt, both internal and external, outpaced our ability to pay, that graft and corruption became a bastardized way of life and that justice and human dignity were trampled upon so unconscionably. Up to the late '60s, the Philippines was the show window of democracy in Asia and the economic leader of our part of the Pacific, surpassed only by Japan and Hong Kong. The exchange rate was less than four pesos to one dollar; inflation was single digit; and interest rates hardly exceeded 10 percent. Education in public high schools was the equivalent of, if not better than, that available in the best Jesuit or Dominican universities. Employment was still available to the truly diligent, and corruption was minimal. The presidential system of government and the free market economy seemed to work well then. There was no call against the negative use of our values. Our Asian neighbors sent their students and

public officials to copy from our visions, institutions and technologies. We must have been doing right then what we are doing wrong now. If you agree with this, should we not then limit our values study to the last 25-30 years only and not to the last 100 years and delete the Americans, Quezon, Roxas, Osmena, Magsaysay and perhaps Macapagal from our hit list?

The Filipino is Basically

Productive and Law-Abiding The Filipino in 1994 is still basically teachable, productive, diligent, self-reliant and law-abiding. Hence, we hear of Filipinos abroad who are earning more than their white-skinned counterparts in the US more productive than most other nationals working in the Middle East more desired than even local equivalents in Europe and more dependable than anyone else in caring for the children of their employers in Hong Kong, Singapore and Japan.

But place the same US-based Filipino doctor or the same Saudi contract worker, or the same Hong Kong-based Filipino domestic here and he will perform less efficiently; worse, he will be viewed as inefficient and indolent.

The Filipino driver may go through a red light in Metro Manila or bribe his way with customs at the airport. But he will do no such thing in San Francisco or Singapore.

The threshold question is: Why is it that the Filipino behaves when he is abroad but not when he is home? Why is the Filipino more productive in other lands than in his own homeland?

Is it because he carries his native values during his travels? Or could it be because he is not properly rewarded and appreciated in his own country? Could it be because in his country productivity and good citizenship do not bring him prosperity and advancement in life? More to the point, could it be that political connection,

wealth and gibberish are more useful than hard work, discipline and grit?

Doing Business with 'Lusot, Lakas, Lagay' Let me illustrate with my favorite taxi driver Kardo. Kardo is a survivalist in the confused and confusing traffic of Metro Manila. He weaves in and out of lane, ignores red lights and runs in the opposite traffic whenever the through lane is crowded. But the wonder of it all is that, in his 20 years of driving, he has never been fined or even issued a traffic violation receipt (TVR).

Like all of you, I marvel at Kardo. So I asked him how he is such a superman. Here is his answer. "Alam po ninyo, madali iyan. Parati namang walang pulis. At kung may pulis man, sa iba nakatingin. At kung tatawagin niya ako wala naman siyang laban sa akih. Bakit ka ninyo? Pag ho tinawag niya ako, eh, magmamakaawa ako at sasabihin ko na wala pa akong kita, kaya pagbigyan na niya ako. O, di kaya makikiusap ako at sasabihin kong pasensiya na siya at hindi na maulit. O, di naman kaya, mangangatuwiran o lulusot ako at sasabihin kong wala namang batas o ordinansa akong sinuway.

Kung di ko siya makuha sa LUSOT, eh gagamitan ko siya ng LAKAS. Sasabihin ko na kumpare ko si Major Cruz, iyong po bang chief ng traffic. Ipakikita ko sa kanya iyong litrato kong kasama si Major at iyong calling card na ibinigay niya. Tiyak ho tiklop iyong pulis. At sasabihin na lang, "Malakas ka pala, pare. Hindi mo sinabi agad. At kung makita mo si Major, sabihin mo naman na limang taon na akong walang promotion."

Attorney, kung di siya makuha sa lusot o sa lakas, eh gagamitin ko na ang pinakamahusay na paraan: iyong LAGAY. Sasabihin ko sa kanya, "Mamang pulis, hindi mo ako pwedeng hulihin sapagkat marami akong abogado." Tiyak itatanong niya,

"Eh sinu-sino naman ang mga abogado mo?" Ang isasagot ko naman ay "Si Presidente Manuel Quezon at si Presidente Sergio Osmena." Kung minsan nga kung masyadong matapang iyong pulis ang ilalaban ko ay si Presidente Manuel Roxas.

Ngunit kamakailan lamang, bago ho magkaroon ng rollback, napilitan akong gamitin si Ninoy Aquino na abogado.

"Hindi ba matagal nang patay sina Quezon, Osmeiia, Roxas at Aquino? Papaano mo pa silang magagamit na abogado?" I asked him.

"Aba, buhay ho sila. Buksan ninyo ang wallet ninyo at makikita ninyo sila doon!"

Ladies and gentlemen, it is these three L's - LUSOT, LAKAS, LAGAY - that have taken the place of hard work, discipline and obedience to law in our country. In one way or another, our people have come to believe that one can talk, influence or bribe his way out of trouble. In fact, he could also use these three L's more effectively than any virtue or talent to secure jobs, assure profits and get almost anything in our society. Therefore, why should he work hard? Why should he study? Why should he follow the law? That would bring him nowhere. But with the three L's, he can transact business, lessen red tape, get approval for his projects, secure the esteem of people and make enormous money.

There are no 'National Values,' Only Regional Ones One more thing about Dr. Jocano's cultural ideals of galang, pagbabahala, pananagutan, balikatan, bayanihan and malasakit; as well as his core values of asal, halaga, diwa, along with damdamin, dangal, kapwa, pakikisama, pakikitungo, hiya, delikadesa, awa, pagbabahala, utang na loob, tiwala, sigla and kaya. Aren't these basically Tagalog? Aren't these cultural ideals and core values preponderant only in the Tagalog-speaking regions? Are all these accepted in the Ilocos, in Cebu, in Iloilo, in Basilan and in Jolo? I know that there is a raging debate on what our national language - and what our national soul - should be. And not wishing to be drawn into that raging battle, I will stop asking questions.

## From a Lawyer's Mind

Finally, I shall now give my reaction as a practising lawyer for the last 33 years. Dr. Jocano said that there seems to be two conflicting models of governing our behavior. On the surface, we have an exogenous model that is "legal, formal, rigid, confrontational and individualistic." On the deeper side, we have an indigenous model that is "customary, non-formal, flexible, non-confrontational and consensual." So, too, we have trained our bureaucracy to be "procedural, legal and adversarial" when on the other hand, our Filipino community is "consultative, persuasive and consensual. "

Though Based on Foreign Models, Our Legal System Conforms with Local Customs and Beliefs I do agree that many of our laws are based on American, Spanish, French (Napoleonic) and even Roman sources. But this is not really unusual. The juridical systems of many other countries have also copied from the world's great legal models, invariably of their colonizers and conquerors. Hence, Malaysia, Singapore and Hong Kong have based many of their laws on the Anglo-Saxon model. To their credit, of course, they have also localized these laws as much as practicable. In the Philippines too, we have tried to calibrate our borrowed Civil Code, Penal Code and commercial laws to conform with local customs. To agree with our monogamous Catholic tradition, absolute divorce is not recognized; to institutionalize respect and filial relations, our laws require support

between spouses and between parents and children; to allow for differences in judicial procedures, Muslim Shari'a Courts have been created; to show sensitivity to the different cultures of minorities, special autonomous governments have been established in parts of the Cordilleras and of Mindanao; to recognize the ability of the masses to uplift themselves, the Local Government Code has been enacted to enable our people to use their local customs. In fact, one wonders whether these localizations are not self-defeating in terms of the movement towards globalization and internationalization of trade and commerce. The 1987 Constitution Also Recognizes Local Cultural Values The 1987 Constitution features some cultural ideals and values like: '

1. In Art. II, the Constitution for the first time "recognizes the sanctity of family life"; "promotes total human liberation and development"; and "encourages (the formation of) non-governmental, community-based, or sectoral organizations that promote the welfare of the nation."

2. In Art. XI, it institutionalizes. the principle of "pananagutan" or "accountability" by declaring that a "public office is a public trust (and that) (p)ublic officers and employees must at all times be accountable to the people, serve them with utmost responsibility, integrity, loyalty and efficiency, act with Patriotism and justice and lead modest lives."

3. In the spirit of "pagbabahala" and "malasakit", the Constitution decrees in Article XII, "a more equitable distribution of opportunities, income and wealth; a sustained increase in the amount of goods and services produced by the nation for the benefit of the people; and an expanding productivity as the key to raising the quality of life for all, especially the underprivileged." The Supreme Court Has

Demonstrated Sensitivity to the Social Environment Even the Supreme Court of the Philippines has shown some sensitivity to national goals and the social environment. For example, in a case decided in 1919 (Rubi vs. Provincial Board of Mindoro, 39 Phil. 660), the High Court upheld the validity of a law requiring members of a certain non-Christian tribe to reside within the confines of a reservation. As aptly pointed out by retired Supreme Court Justice Jose C. Campos Jr., this decision could be a shock to civil libertarians today who would consider this a gross violation of the liberty of abode. (See Jose C. Campos Jr., "The Judiciary in a Changing World," Work Paper presented to the 16th Biennial Conference on the Law of the World, Manila, October 24- 29, 1993).

And yet the same Supreme Court promulgated a decision less than a month later (Villavicencio vs. Lukban, 39 Phil. 778), which struck down the act of the mayor of Manila in forcing certain women of ill repute to transfer their residence from Manila to Davao, on the ground that his act violated the liberty of abode.

In a long line of cases, the High Court sustained the validity of child labor laws, minimum wage laws and other regulations of labor against avowals of individual and Western concepts of property rights and the freedom to contract, all in the pursuance of the

value of "malasakit".

More recently, on October 22, 1992, the Supreme Court (Central Mindanao University vs. DARAB et al, G.R. No. 100091, October 22, 1992) speaking through Mr. Justice Campos, declared as illegal the distribution of a portion of a land reservation of a state agricultural university to certain teachers and employees on the higher value, inter alia, that "state colleges and universities are the main vehicles for our scientific and technological advancement in the field of agriculture so vital to the existence, growth and development of this country."

The point is that although theoretically the Supreme Court does not advocate policies or enact laws, it really looks at cases brought before it in a national and historical context and decides them not in isolation but in magisterial consideration of contemporaneous situations, ever mindful not only of the abstractions of law but also of the national welfare and people's values, culture and heritage. For indeed the law should not be used to destroy what is dear and valuable to man, but as a brick in the building of the Structures of society.

## Summary

In closing, let me summarize. In direct response to Dr. Jocano, I may agree that the lack of appreciation of the role of traditional values is one factor that impedes rapid economic growth but it is not the only one; in fact, it is not the most critical one. To the paradox of why the only Christian nation in Asia is also the poorest, most violent and most underdeveloped, the Church calls for new inculturated methods and expressions to re-evangelize our people in the time-tested gospel values of love, sharing, compassion and non-violence.

It also urges the promotion of total integral liberation as the theology for the renewal of our sick society. "Through these gospel values," confidently declared Archbishop Leonardo Legaspi, the brilliant president of PCP II (Speech on the

closing day of PCP II, 17 February 1991), "the structures of injustice and corruption in our midst can be firmly dismantled." Rather than look for "national" values, the Church, which is divided into some 80 autonomous dioceses in the Philippines, tends to localize its use of indigenous culture to express its truths.

On the other hand, business is more empirical in its approach and observes that when the Filipino is abroad, he is industrious, productive, law-abiding and prosperous. And yet, this prosperity can hardly be attributed to his traditional values, whether these be Tagalog, Cebuano or Maranaw, because obviously these are not observed in his work environment abroad. On this basis, productivity does not seem to depend on the role of traditional values but on the more Western reward system for work well done. Incidentally, the Church has taken notice of migrant workers and has in fact credited their Christian values as the inner source of their strength and dependability while working abroad.

Finally, as a lawyer, may I stress that where there are values and ideals that appear to be critical to national welfare, the framers of our Constitution and our lawmakers have not hesitated to include them formally in our legal system. In doing so, do they not in fact break the barrier between the exogenous and indigenous models or between the bureaucracy and the community? Also, it is worth emphasizing that the Supreme Court has taken and, I believe, will continue to take into account, proven cultural ideals and community values in interpreting laws and deciding controversies brought before it. As Dean Roscoe Pound of Harvard Law School advised, "Law must be stable, but it cannot stand still."

I thank you.
