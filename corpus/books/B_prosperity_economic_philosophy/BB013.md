# Battles in the Supreme Court -- Ch. 6: BATTLES ON THE ECONOMIC FRONT

## The Constitutionality of the Oil Deregulation Law

During my three years in the Court, no decision was more criticized by big business than that issued in Tatad v. Secretary of Energy and its companion case, Lagman v. Torres, in which the Court through Justice Puno invalidated RA 8180, the Oil Deregulation Law, by a vote of 9 to 2. The “Ayala Avenue critics” accused the Court of undue interference in economic matters, a subject allegedly beyond the competence and the authority of the Court to decide.

The Court observed that RA 8180 allowed the three big oil companies - Petron, Shell and Caltex - to act as a monopoly or, more precisely, an oligopoly, because they acted in concert in their overt dealings with the public, particularly in price-fixing. By allowing the "Big Three" oil companies to combine their operations and marketing, RA 8180 offended the constitutional prohibition against monopolies and combinations in restraint of trade. In the words of Justice Puno, "The Constitution mandates this Court to be the guardian not only of the people's political rights but their economic rights as well."

In my Concurring Opinion I stressed the following:

"1. The Issue Is Whether Oil Companies May Unilaterally Fix Prices, Not Whether This Court May Interfere in Economic Questions

"With the issuance of the status quo order on October 7, 1997 requiring the three respondent oil companies—Petron, Shell and Caltex— ‘to cease and desist from increasing the prices of gasoline and other petroleum fuel products for a period of thirty (30) days,’ the Court has been accused of interfering in purely economic policy matters or, worse, of arrogating unto itself price-regulatory powers. Let it be emphasized that we have no desire—nay, we have no power—to intervene in, to change or to repeal the laws of economics, in the same manner that we cannot and will not nullify or invalidate the laws of physics or chemistry.

"The issue here is not whether the Supreme Court may fix the retail prices of petroleum products. Rather, the issue is whether RA 8180, the law allowing the oil companies to unilaterally set, increase or decrease their prices, is valid or constitutional.

"Under the Constitution, this Court has—in appropriate cases—the DUTY, not just the power, to determine whether a law or a part thereof offends the Constitution and, if so, to annul and set it aside. Because a serious challenge has been hurled against the validity of one such law, namely RA 8180—its criticality having been preliminarily determined from the petition, comments, reply and, most tellingly, the oral argument on September 30, 1997—this Court, in the exercise of its mandated judicial discretion, issued the status quo order to prevent the continued enforcement and implementation of a law that was prima facie found to be constitutionally infirm. Indeed, after careful final deliberation, said law is now ruled to be constitutionally defective thereby disabling respondent oil companies from exercising their erstwhile power, granted by such defective statute, to determine prices by themselves.

"Concededly, this Court has no power to pass upon the wisdom, merits and propriety of the acts of its co-equal branches of government. However, it does have the prerogative to uphold the Constitution and to strike down and annul a law that contravenes the Charter. From such duty and prerogative, it shall never shirk or shy away.

"By annulling RA 8180, this Court is not making a policy statement against deregulation. Quite the contrary, it is simply invalidating a pseudo-deregulation law which in reality restrains free trade and perpetuates a cartel, an oligopoly. The Court is merely upholding constitutional adherence to a truly competitive economy that releases the creative energy of free enterprise. It leaves to Congress, as the policy-setting agency of the government, the speedy crafting of a genuine, constitutionally justified oil deregulation law.

"2. Everyone, Rich or Poor, Must Share in the Burdens of Economic Dislocation

"Much has been said and will be said about the alleged negative effect of this Court's holding on the oil giants' profit and loss statements. We are not unaware of the disruptive impact of the depreciating peso on the retail prices of refined petroleum products. But such price-escalating consequence adversely affects not merely these big oil companies which occupy hallowed places among the most profitable corporate behemoths in our country. In these critical times of widespread economic dislocations, abetted by currency fluctuations not entirely of domestic origin, all sectors of society agonize and suffer. Thus, everyone, rich or poor, must share in the burdens of such economic aberrations.

"I can understand foreign investors who see these price adjustments as necessary consequences of the country's adherence to the free market, for that, in the first place, is the magnet for their presence here. Understandably, their concern is limited to bottom lines and market share. But in all these megacompanies, there are also Filipino entrepreneurs and managers. I am sure there are patriots among them who realize that, in times of economic turmoil, the poor and the underprivileged proportionately suffer more than any other sector of society. There is a certain threshold of pain beyond which the disadvantaged cannot endure. Indeed, it has been wisely said that 'if the rich who are few will not help the poor who are many, there will come a time when the few who are filled cannot escape the wrath of the many who are hungry.' Kaya't sa mga kababayan nating kapitalista at may kapangyarihan, nararapat lamang na makiisa tayo sa mga kapos-palad at mahihirap sa mga araw ng pangangailangan. Huwag na nating ipagdiinan ang kawalan ng tubo, o maging ang panandaliang pagkalugi. At sa mga mangangalakal na ganid at walang puso: hirap na hirap na po ang ating mga kababayan. Mahiya naman kayo!"

Still many business leaders misunderstood the Decision and flung critical darts at the Court, in spite of the herculean effort of Justice Puno to explain the said ruling in simple English. Prior to my appointment to the Court, I had been exposed to the business community, even immersed in it, having served as vice president of the Philippine Chamber of Commerce and Industry and governor of the Management Association of the Philippines.

I therefore took it upon myself to explain the Court's ruling in a speech I delivered on January 6, 1998 before the Rotary Club of Makati, which had as members the big guns of finance and business. I explained that "policies of the legislative and executive departments of the government—call them deregulation or liberalization or globalization—must conform to the Constitution. When such policies and their specific implementing laws or regulations ran contrary to the Charter, the Supreme Court, in appropriate proceedings, [was duty-bound to] strike them down." Going to great lengths to discourse on the doctrine of separation of powers and its corollary principle of checks and balances, I said: "That justices are not economists or businessmen cannot lessen the cogency or binding effect of their decisions. By the same token, justices are not physicians or theologians, but they render conclusive judgments involving medicine or theology. x x x It is up to the parties concerned to enlighten the Court, argue their sides and convince the justices of the merits of their causes. That is the nature of judicial process and that is how decisions are and will be made."

I went to the extreme to explain that: "the Court will not declare an economic matter void simply, but only because it contravenes the Constitution, or it was executed with grave abuse of discretion amounting to lack or excess of jurisdiction. These are the two grounds-unconstitutionality and grave abuse of discretion-which may impel the Court to void the policies and acts of any department, office or entity touching on any matter including economics."

During the open forum that followed, I was pleased with the number and quality of searching questions hurled at me even past the usual time. I was even more pleased that after the program was over, I was approached by a number of Rotarians thanking me for the explanation. None satisfied me more, though, than the smile and the words of a big business executive: "Thank you for the explanation. In the past, I severely criticized that Decision. But now, you have given me refreshing parameters and new paradigms in looking at the Supreme Court. I shall definitely pass on your excellent explanation to the members of the Makati Business Club."

Although the audience may have been satisfied with my discourse, the issue of the Court's alleged unwelcome "intrusion" on economic matters may resurface. But I feel that when and if that situation comes up again, the Court would be in a better position to be understood.

In this spirit, I would like to include as a "postscript" the full text of my speech before the Rotary Club of Makati.

## May The Supreme Court Decide Economic Issues?

I am grateful for, but at the same time humbled by, the generous introduction of my good friend, Rotarian Bubi Krohn. However, he could have shortened it by simply introducing me as a fellow Rotarian. That would have said it all. True, indeed, I am very proud to be identified with Rotary. It was during my incumbency as president of the Rotary Club of Manila (RCM) when I gathered the courage to proclaim publicly my personal creed “Love God, Serve Man,” which later became the title and theme of my first book. On July 12, 1990, during my inaugural as RCM president, I announced: "Upon this credo LOVE GOD, SERVE MAN, I shall be judged, not only in the Rotary Club of Manila but, I daresay, in my whole unworthy life here on earth."

That was why upon my appointment as a magistrate, I asked my colleagues in the Court to permit me to retain my Rotary membership—one of three I was allowed to maintain, the other two being my affiliations in the Political Council for the Laity and the Bantayog ng mga Bayani Foundation. Consistent with long-standing tradition, members of the Supreme Court, by policy and practice, divest themselves of nonjudicial activities upon their assumption of office. Although no longer busy with Rotary activities, I manage to attend enough functions to retain my senior active status.

In my stint in the Court, I echoed my credo "Love God, Serve Man" and reworded it as "Justice and Faith," words that encapsulate my vision and mission in the judiciary. Justice and Faith, incidentally, is the title of my new book which was released by the Supreme Court Printing Services just three weeks ago, a copy of which I now present to President Sid Garcia.

I thank Bubi Krohn, your program chairman, for his persistence in his invitation. Originally, I was supposed to speak on August 19, 1997, but a massive flood all over Metro Manila cancelled that meeting and, for that matter, all work and other activities in the whole metropolis. Bubi would not be daunted; h e called and wrote me to reset my talk to November 12, 1997, but I had to beg off because the Supreme Court was also scheduled to vote on the Oil Deregulation Law around that date, and I did not want to be absent. But Bubi persisted and we finally agreed on January 6, 1998 - a day when the Supreme Court is still on its Christmas break.

With that background, I thought I should thus speak to you about the Court 's rather contro versia l Decision nullifying RA 8180, the Oil Deregulation Law, a Decision which is now final and no longer sub judice, and about the Tribunal's alleged "intrusion" on economic policy. I realize that your club, the Rotary Club of Makati, is the bastion of the financial elite of the country. And I know, too, that many of the criticisms against that ruling came from big business. But I speak in the spirit of Rotary, with the four-way test in mind and with a view to explaining the Decision in layman's terms with as little legalese as possible.

Let me, however, emphasize that the opinions I will express are mine and not directly those of the Court, except when I directly quote portions of Decisions of the Tribunal. Let me also say that the Court's judgment (on the Oil Deregulation Law), written by the gutsy and erudite Mr. Justice Reynato S. Puno, was carried by a vote of 9 in favor and 2 against (JJ Jose A.R. Melo and Ricardo J. Francisco dissented). I should add that one of the nine - Mr. Justice Santiago M. Kapunan - submitted a Separate Opinion invalidating not the entire law but only three provisions thereof, which I will discuss later. This address should also explain why I, a former counsel of some of the country's "Top 1000," wrote a separate Concurring Opinion.

### Proceedings Before the Supreme Court

Let me now summarize the proceedings before the Court.

One. The petitioners led by Reps. Enrique Garcia, Joker Arroyo, Edcel Lagman, and Wigberto Tañada; Sen. Francisco Tatad and others demonstrated to the Court, verbally during the oral argument and in writing in their pleadings, that the Big Three oil companies - PETRON, SHELL and CALTEX were producing and processing almost identical products which they were selling to the general public at identical prices. When one company adjusted its prices upwards or downwards, the other two followed suit at practically the same time and by the same amount. The Big Three were able, among other things, to determine gas prices by themselves because RA 8180, the Oil Deregulation Law, lifted government controls over the downstream oil industry and adopted a laissez-faire policy, which gave the players complete freedom in their operations and marketing, particularly in pricing their finished products.

Two. Asked by the Court to refute the foregoing jarring allegations were the respondent oil companies which, most significantly, were supported by the government through the solicitor general. In our view, however, they failed to do so.

Three. The Constitution of the Philippines, specifically Article XII, Sec. 19, mandates that "[the State shall regulate or prohibit monopolies when the public interest so requires. No combinations in restraint of trade or unfair competition shall be allowed."

Four. Prescinding from the above premises, the Court concluded that RA 8180 allowed a combination in restraint of trade; it enabled the Big Three to act as'a monopoly or, more precisely, an oligopoly; that is, the three companies, while legally separate, were acting as one in restraining trade by carrying out in unison their overt dealings with the general public, particularly price-fixing. Being offensive to or inconsistent with the Constitution, RA 8180 had to be voided.

Five. The Court found that three specific provisions of RA 8180 tended to protect and perpetuate the monopoly of the Big Three by obstructing the entry and the competitiveness of new players in the market. These were: first, the provision on tariff differential which imposed only a 3 percent burden on the crude oil importations of the Big Three and a 7 percent tariff on imported refined petroleum products of new entrants to the market, thereby giving the Big Three a 4 percent differential against new players, an advantage that translates to about twenty centavos for every liter of retail gasoline; second, the minimum inventory clause, which required new players to maintain in storage in the country at least 10 percent of their annual sales volume, or a forty-day supply, whichever was lower; and third, the predatory pricing scheme, which prohibited the selling of any product at a price unreasonably below the industry average cost, so as to attract customers to the detriment of competitors.

In sum, the Court found that RA 8180 enabled the three oil companies to combine their operations and marketing and thereby effectively sanctioned a monopoly or a cartel. Additionally, the three specific provisions tended to obstruct the entry or competitiveness of new players. Clearly then, RA 8180 offended the constitutional prohibition against monopolies and combinations in restraint of trade.

### Criticisms of the Court's Decision

On the other hand, our critics advance the following contentions:

One. In striking down RA 8180, the Court improperly intervened in an economic and business policy and ignored established legal doctrines like the principle of separation of powers.

Two. The Court should have listened to "objective" expert advice on the alleged adverse economic implications of its Decision, particularly on foreign investors who rely on the government's economic programs as their investment guide.

Three. By invalidating the tariff differential of 4 percent provided under RA 8180, the Court effectively revived the 10 percent differential imposed under the old law, thereby increasing, instead of decreasing, the retail prices of oil products and further discouraging new players from entering the market.

Four. Assuming that the three offending provisions of RA 8180 - tariff differential, oil inventory and predatory pricing — are unconstitutional, they should have been the only portions invalidated and not the entire law.

Five. The Court should have merely extended the transition period provided under RA 8180, instead of shifting to Congress the responsibility of crafting a new Oil Deregulation Law.

Let me now answer these objections briefly and reserve for the open forum a more detailed discussion.

### Did the Court Improperly Intervene in Economic Matters?

First, let me take up the most basic objection; that is, that the Court improvidently interfered in an economic policy laid down by Congress and the President. In answer, I immediately point to the express statement of our Decision — that "the Court did not condemn the economic policy of deregulation as unconstitutional. It merely held that, as crafted, the law Iran] counter to the constitutional provision for fair competition." In my own Concurring Opinion, I stressed: "By annulling RA 8180, this Court is not making a policy statement against deregulation. Quite the contrary, it is simply invalidating a pseudoderegulation law, which in reality restrains free trade and perpetrates a cartel, an oligopoly. The Court is merely upholding constitutional adherence to a truly competitive economy that releases the creative energy of free enterprise."

Verily, policies of the legislative and executive departments of government — call them deregulation or liberalization or globalization - must conform to the Constitution. When such policies and their specific implementing laws or regulations offend the Charter, the Supreme Court, in appropriate proceedings, shall strike them down. Accordingly, the Court, in its November 5, 1997 Decision, intoned: "Combinations in restraint of trade and unfair competition are absolutely proscribed (by the Constitution) and the proscription is directed both against the State as well as the private sector. x xx. Monopolistic or oligopolistic markets deserve our careful scrutiny, and laws which barricade the entry points of new players should be viewed with suspicion."

Heavily attacked is the rather strong emphasis of the Decision - that "xxx the Constitution is a covenant that grants and guarantees both the political and [the] economic rights of the people. The Constitution mandates this Court to be the guardian not only of the people's political rights but of their economic rights as well."

If our critics will peruse our Constitution, they will find it unique in many ways. One unusual feature is its generous invocation of the economic rights of our people, along with their political, civil and human rights. A full article with 22 sections 196 underlines these principles which the Supreme Court has the duty to uphold. Unlike our previous three Constitutions197 - the 1935, the 1973, and the socalled 1986 Freedom Constitution ordained by President Corazon C. Aquino - the present Charter expressly and unmistakably focuses on economic issues, and it underscores in unmistakable strokes the economic directions of the nation as well as the economic rights of the masses.

### The US Supreme Court Criticized Also for Its Economic Decisions

True, the American Constitution, present Charter, is a model of conciseness that hardly contains any comparable provisions on economic policies and rights. However, this lack did not prevent the US Supreme Court from "intruding" on the realm of economics and from striking down laws passed by the US Congress for being "oppressive," "violative of due process" or "offensive to the voluntariness of contracts." To fight back, the US President tried his "court-packing" strategy - naming sympathetic jurists to the Court and threatening radicals with impeachment and public pressure.19 Undaunted, the US Supreme Court fought back to preserve its independence and to protect the people's rights. The strategy failed and, to this day, the US judiciary continues to rule on all constitutional issues, including those with an economic dimension.

While scrutiny of judicial decisions delving on economic matters is to be expected in a democratic environment,200 it can hardly deter the Supreme Court of the Philippines from exercising its constitutional duty and power to thumb down unconstitutional statutes, armed as it is, not only with American and Philippine precedents as to the extent of its inherent authority, but also with new express mandates in the 1987 Charter.

The principle of separation of powers, it is true, limits the prerogatives of each department of government to those granted to it by the Constitution. However, the very Constitution which prescribes such limitation impels the Supreme Court to uphold the supremacy of the Charter and to strike down any act of any branch of government that offends constitutional principles, including those of economic, social and political rights. As aptly pointed out by Mr. Justice Puno, the Constitution did not exempt laws with an economic dimension from the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court.

Indeed, the principle of separation of powers is justified by the corollary doctrine of checks and balances. The people's welfare, liberties and interests are best served by a careful scrutiny of any act that violates the most basic law of the land. The power of Congress to enact and that of the President to implement any business policy are restricted by the Constitution, and the duty and the power to determine when and how such policy so offends the Charter have been lodged in the Supreme Court. From this responsibility, the Court will never shirk or shy away. "It is the unyielding duty of the Supreme Court to uphold the supremacy of the Constitution, not with a mere wishbone but with a backbone that should neither bend nor break."

To the critics' lament that such economic "intrusion" will drive away foreign investors, I can only point out that the Philippine system of government is not dictatorial or authoritarian. It is not dependent on the wishes or the commands of one man or one department. It breathes and lives within democratic space where the rule of law, not the decree of man, prevails. Such a system is notanathema to free enterprise. In fact, it finds its roots in the American system, with which most foreign investors should be familiar. And investors, whether local or foreign, should find comfort in a system of government that allows individual rights and liberties to prevail against even the awesome might of a majoritarian government, or of mob rule and temporary popular sentiments or, for that matter, of despots and dictators.

### Ignorance of Adversarial System of Litigation

We now come to the second objection; namely, that the Court should have listened to external expert economic advice, before ruling on a nationally sensitive issue. This critique obviously emanates from ignorance either of our adversarial system of litigation or of the procedure actually employed by the Court before it reached the oil deregulation Decision. Our system of adversarial litigation is not like that used during congressional investigations, in which the general public may come forth and speak up during hearings. In litigations, only the parties and generally those with locus standi or legal standing may participate. Kibitzers, however well-meaning, cannot be heard. Editorials, popularity surveys and broadcast commentaries cannot be the basis of decisions. As Justice Hiller B. Zobel of the Massachusetts Superior Court explained:

"Elected officials may consider popular urging and sway to public opinion polls. Judges must follow their oaths and do their duty, heedless of editorials, letters, telegrams, picketers, threats, petitions, panelists and talk shows. In this country, we do not administer justice by plebiscite."

Let us remember that, as a rule, a case is decided only on the basis of facts, issues and arguments raised by the parties before the courts. In fact, issues and arguments not brought before a lower court cannot be raised for the first time on appeal before a higher tribunal. Due process requires that a judgment should be rendered only on the basis of matters actually heard and discussed. As I often say, "Each case has its own flesh and blood and cannot be decided simply on the basis of isolated clinical classroom principles.' Two groups of litigants who, on the surface, appear to be similarly situated could be recipients of different decisions depending on the facts, issues and arguments raised.

Let me give you a very recent example involving the strike of public schoolteachers. In Jacinto v. Court of Appeals, the mentors insisted that their strike — meaning, their absence, without authority, from their classrooms in order to publicly agitate for better working conditions — was justified by their freedom of speech and of assembly. In a Decision I wrote, the Court en bane unanimously ruled that the Constitution did not grant government employees, including public schoolteachers, the right to strike. Hence, the disciplinary penalty imposed upon them by the Civil Service Commission was upheld.

Au Contraire, in Fabella v. Court of Appeals, another group of teachers assailed the "guilty" verdict violation of their freedom of speech or of their alleged right to strike, but because the investigating committee was not properly constituted. They argued that the Department of Education (and the CSC) had not included, as a member therein, a representative of the schoolteachers' association, as prescribed by the Magna Carta for Public Schoolteachers. A unanimous Supreme Court, in a judgment I also penned, upheld the mentors (and reversed the Civil Service Commission), because the illegal composition of the investigating committee deprived the respondents of due process. Striking teachers lost the first case on the issue of their alleged right to strike, while they won the second case on the issue of due process two separate cases promulgated in the same month (November 1997) by unanimous vote and written by the same ponente.

In the oil deregulation case, the parties were given more than sufficient time and opportunity to argue their causes orally and in writing. To underline the significance of the case, the Court even called for oral arguments (which happens only in less than one percent of cases it decides), in addition to the usual voluminous written pleadings the parties had filed. But the Big Three failed to rebut the constitutional issues raised by petitioners, especially the damning argument that RA 8180 virtually granted them a monopoly, and that they in fact acted as a cartel.

### Supreme Court Decisions on Political, Economic, Social and Other Matters

That justices are not economists or businessmen cannot lessen the cogency or binding effect of their decisions. By the same token, justices are not physicians or theologians, but they render conclusive judgments involving medicine or theology.

For example, in the July 25, 1997 case People v. Ortega, the Supreme Court used medical science to establish the culpability of an accused who had not taken part in stabbing the victim. The accused arrived at the crime scene after the stabbing had taken place. He merely wanted to conceal the crime by throwing the victim's body in a well and thereafter covering it with stone and dirt. In the face of medical evidence that the victim's lungs and stomach imbibed muddy particles identical to the residue at the bottom of the well, the Court ruled that drowning, not stabbing, was the immediate cause of death, because the "water and fluid contents in the stomach corresponded to the medium where the body was found."

So, too, in Republic v. Molina, the Court interpreted with finality the meaning of Canon Law, specifically Canon 1095, which laid down a provision, later borrowed by our Family Code, for declaring a marriage void. Hence, even if the justices were not canonists or theologians, they had to render a decision on whether the marriage of Spouses Roridel Olaviano and Reynaldo Molina could be voided on the ground of "psychological incapacity."

As a third example, in Iglesia ni Cristo v. Court of Appeals, the Supreme Court magistrates - although not experts in broadcasting, religion or social sciences — ruled on the issue of whether the Board of Censors acted properly in "X-rating" certain television programs.

The point is: The members of the Court need not be experts in medicine, religion or economics in order to decide cases involving those disciplines. It is up to the parties concerned to enlighten the Court, argue their sides and convince the justices of the merits of their causes. That is the nature of the judicial process and that is how decisions are and will be made.

### The Tariff Differential

Our critics allege, as a third point, that the Court was ignorant of the fact that by ruling out the 4 percent tariff differential in RA 8180, it shamelessly decreed the more burdensome 10 percent differential under the old law. I have news for our critics. Quite the contrary, the Court was only too well aware of this. It acknowledged that the natural consequence of the invalidity of a new law was the revival of the old. Unfortunately, the Court has no power to repeal or alter the old law. While it has the power to declare a law unconstitutional, it has no authority to modify a law which in its opinion is unwise or inconvenient. Under the principle of separation of powers invoked by the critics, only Congress has the power to make, amend, or repeal laws that are unwise or detrimental to the general welfare. So, too, the President is empowered to fix or modify tariff rates. That was why the Court in its Decision repeatedly pointed to Congress and to the Office of the President as the venues for abrogating or annulling the 10 percent differential.

In this connection, the media reported a few days ago that the President had in fact reset the tariff differential to a uniform 3 percent only. In any event, the old law merely regulated - not deregulated - the oil industry. And part of the regulation was the 10 percent tariff differential. Whether that was wise or unwise is a matter within legislative and executive prerogatives, and not of the judiciary. It is up to these policymaking departments to determine whether we should have a regulated or a deregulated oil industry, the caveat being that in any choice they make, they should not offend the Constitution.

### Partial or Total Unconstitutionality?

The fourth criticism is: Why did the Court declare the nullification to the three offending provisions? The brief answer to that question is: Had the Court voided only the provisions on tariff differential, oil inventory and predatory pricing, and allowed the rest of the law to remain, it would have enabled the oil companies to continue acting as an oligopoly and to determine pump prices by their cartelized selves. They would have been able to raise their retail prices by any amount, without any real competition or government regulation. The oligopoly would still have been able to inflict itself on the hapless and helpless public. Let us recall that RA 8180 was voided for two main reasons: (1) it allowed a combination in restraint of trade or a cartel, and (2) it obstructed the entry or competitiveness of new players. Invalidating the three provisions would have satisfied the second reason but not the first. Conformably, the Court preferred to give Congress and the President the leeway and discretion to craft a new law and/or executive order free of these two çonstitutional flaws.

### Partial or Total Deregulation?

The fifth objection, ironically enough, was foisted by the main petitioner and prevailing party in this case, Rep. Enrique Garcia. He contends that the Court should not have declared the entire law unconstitutional, but (in addition to the three aforecited provisions) should have condemned Sec. 15 which mandated full deregulation. In his two Motions for Reconsideration, Representative Garcia passionately argued for a return only to "partial deregulation," in which the main features of deregulation would be allowed free rein, but the retail prices of oil products would still be regulated through the Energy Regulatory Board. In this scheme, there would have been no necessity to pass a new deregulation bill. He was apprehensive that Congress would not be able to pass a constitutionally justified deregulation law.

The Court carefully deliberated on this point, and some of the justices, including me, found Garcia's plea to be wise and prudent. However, it was bereft of legal underpinnings. In other words, the Court did not find any legal basis for such alternative, simply because it appeared to be wise, convenient and beneficial. To do what Representative Garcia wanted, the Court would have had to legislate, a power granted only to Congress and not to the Supreme Court. To the relief of its main critics, the Court realized only too well the limits of its authority. In the relatively recent case Tanada v. Angara, in which the Court upheld the constitutionality of the Senate ratification of the Philippine adherence to the World Iimitsofour power thus:

"We should stress that, in deciding to take jurisdiction over this petition, this Court will not review the wisdom of the decision of the President and the Senate in enlisting the country into the WTO, or pass upon the merits of trade liberalization as a policy espoused by said international body. Neither will it rule on the propriety of the government's economic policy of reducing/removing tariffs, taxes, subsidies, quantitative restrictions, and other import/trade barriers."

### Grounds for Judicial Intervention in Economic Matters

As ponente or writer of the aforecited Decision, I would like to stress that Tañada v. Angara, which was concurred in unanimously by the fifteen members of the Court, clearly spelled out the Court's doctrine in regard to economic matters, and that is — at the risk of being repetitive - the Court will not declare an economic matter void simply because it is unwise or improper or inconvenient, but only because it contravenes the Constitution, or it was executed with grave abuse of discretion amounting to lack or excess of jurisdiction. These are the two grounds — unconstitutionality and grave abuse of discretion that may impel the Court to void the policies and acts of any department, office or entity touching on any matter including economics.

Fellow Rotarians, I have tried to explain in plain language the Court's ruling in the oil deregulation case and, for that matter, my own perspective on the responsibility of the Supreme Court to decide on economic issues. To those who are convinced by this little talk and who now understand our role, I give my thanks. To those who are still unconvinced or are doubtful, I can only cite a quotation from a 1967 Decision of the Court:

"We concede that a (critic) may think highly of his intellectual endowment. That is his privilege. And he may suffer frustration at what he feels is others' lack of it. That is his misfortune. Such a frame of mind, however, should not be allowed to harden into a belief that he may attack a court's decision in words calculated to jettison the time-honored aphorism that courts are the temples of right."

### May the Supreme Court Predetermine the Constitutionality of Statutes?

Thank you. I am now ready for the open forum; and to prime the pump, I will answer immediately a question which was earlier sent to me. It reads: "Congress is now in the process of crafting a new law on oil deregulation. One version simply restates the verbatim text of RA 8180 minus the three offending provisions on the 4 percent tariff differential, predatory pricing and minimum inventory. Would such bill satisfy the Constitution?" This is an interesting question, but it was not raised by the parties and was thus not answered by the Court. May I just point out, however, that under the said bill, the Big Three may still be able to act as a monopoly and engage in combinations in restraint of trade. Thus, Congress may have to place sufficient safeguards in the new deregulation law to forestall the oil giants from taking advantage of deregulation as a shield for cartelizing their operations and marketing.

What these safeguards are or should be I leave to the creative imagination of Congress and the President. The job of Congress is to craft the law; the president, to implement it. The Supreme Court's duty is to determine, in appropriate cases, whether the law, as crafted or as implemented, will pass the constitutional test. That is separation of powers at work!

A corollary question pops up: To assure constitutionality, why can't the Supreme Court pass upon the bill prior to its passage by Congress? Again, under our system of government, that is not allowed. The Court is not an adjunct of Congress or of the President. It is a coequal branch, with a distinct constitutional duty in the scheme of checks and balances. If the Court prejudges the constitutionality of a law, then what chance would litigants have when they challenge actions of our legislators?

Indeed, elementary rules of fairness demand that the judge or referee should not play or take sides in the game. He cannot prejudge the matter prior to hearing the parties. After all, Congress may hire its own legal eagles; and the President has the secretary of justice, the solicitor general and the presidential legal counsel to give him adequate legal backing. That is why, in case of doubt, the Court always rules in favor of constitutionality. In the present case, however, on the basis of the facts, issues and arguments raised by the parties, the Tribunal observed that it had not encountered a law more constitutionally infirm than RA 8180. Hence, the Supreme Court had to strike it down.

Thank you. Now. I am ready for your other questions.

## Summary

Chapter 6 of 'Battles in the Supreme Court' recounted the ruling that big business criticized most during CJP's first three years: Tatad v. Secretary of Energy and its companion case, in which the Court through Justice Puno invalidated RA 8180, the Oil Deregulation Law, by 9 to 2 for allowing Petron, Shell and Caltex to act as an oligopoly and for erecting barriers against new players through its tariff differential, minimum inventory and predatory pricing provisions. CJP reproduced his Concurring Opinion, which insisted that the issue was not whether the Court could fix oil prices but whether a law permitting unilateral pricing was constitutional, that the Court has the duty rather than merely the power to annul such a law, that it was striking a pseudo-deregulation law which perpetuated a cartel rather than opposing deregulation itself, and that in a time of economic dislocation everyone, rich or poor, must share the burden -- ending with an appeal in Filipino to wealthy countrymen and a rebuke to greedy traders. Because he had come from the business community, he took it upon himself to explain the ruling to the Rotary Club of Makati on January 6, 1998, and included that speech in full as a postscript. In it he summarized the proceedings in five steps, listed five criticisms and answered each: economic policy must conform to a Charter that uniquely guarantees economic rights; the demand for outside expert advice misunderstands the adversarial system, in which justice is not administered by plebiscite; the Court knew that voiding a new law revives the old but cannot itself amend; striking only three provisions would have left the cartel intact; and partial deregulation, though wise, would have required the Court to legislate. He closed with the doctrine from his own Tanada v. Angara that only unconstitutionality or grave abuse of discretion may void an economic act, and with the reasons the Court cannot prejudge a pending bill.
