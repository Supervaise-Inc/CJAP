# Chapter 2: Role of the Supreme Court in Economic Development

Sometime ago, someone passed on to me this interesting story. God was supposedly asked three questions by a prospective investor in the Philippines. Initially, the capitalist asked, “Lord, how long is a million years to you?” God replied, “One second.” Then our investor posed this second query: “How much is one billion dollars to you?” the Almighty quickly answered, “One cent.”

The smart investor then raised his third and final plea: “My Lord, would you kindly give me a cent?” And God quickly countered, “Yes, but wait a second please.”

I related this digitally sourced joke, not just to entertain and relax you for my talk, but also to point out two lessons. First, in law as in life, time and money are often intertwined; and second, investments are made depending on the timing and the returns involved. Indeed, investors place their capital and their trust in ventures that, in general, bring the best returns at the best possible time.

On the other hand, governments welcome investments that create jobs, increase national incomes, build infrastructure and generate many other economic benefits.

‘Today, I have been invited by the Central Bank of the Philippines (Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas) to discuss the role of the Supreme Court in creating a favorable investment climate and ultimately in generating economic opportunities for our people. To explain this role clearly, I have divided the general theme into two subtopics: (1) powers and duties of the Supreme Court and (2) jurisprudence that affects investments.

## Functions and Duties of the Supreme Court

Let me now take up the first topic, the general functions and duties of the Supreme Court. Like the United States of America and unlike many of our neighboring countries, the Philippines observes the doctrine of separation of powers. ‘Thus, the power to make laws is lodged in Congress; the power to implement them, in the Presidency; and the power to interpret them, in the Supreme Court. These three great departments of government — Congress, the Presidency and the Supreme Court — are coequal and co-independent with one other. Upon the proper coordination of their functions — through a system of checks and balances — hinges transparent, effective and efficient governance of the country; and ultimately its economic development.

May I stress that the independence of the judiciary is a zealously guarded attribute. By independence, I mean that the Supreme Court’s decisions are final; they are not subject to review, modification or reversal by any other instrumentality or agency of government. When a person’s rights are violated and all remedies in the other offices of government fail, the last and final recourse is the Supreme Court. In this sense, the Court is normally referred to as the last bulwark of democracy. If it must perform such ultimate function credibly, then it must do so independently, (free from any and all extraneous influences. It must issue decisions based only on the specific facts and law involved in the controversy.

In this country, there is only one Supreme Court that is the final authority in the interpretation of the (Constitution and the laws. Other countries have divided the dispensation of judicial power among several tribunal, like Constitutional Courts that decide constitutional questions; Supreme Courts that review appeals from lower courts; and High Courts of Arbitration that rule on arbi-(trial awards. In the Philippines, the structure is simpler: all judicial power is ultimately vested in one Supreme Court.

## Five Specific Functions

‘To the question “What is the extent of the authority of the Supreme Court?” the answer is that it exercises judicial power in five general ways:

1. Ordinary review. Like most high courts in other countries, our Supreme Court exercises what is generally referred to as ordinary judicial review of lower court actions. Thus, directly appealable to it are decisions of the Court of Appeals, the Sandiganbayan, the Court of Tax Appeals and, to a limited extent, the Regional ‘Trial Courts.

2. Extraordinary review. By writ of certiorari, the acts of any branch or instrumentality of government — including the legislative and the executive departments — are passed upon, modified, reverse of nullified by the Supreme Court on the ground of grave abuse of discretion. Grave abuse of discretion may be committed in either of two general ways: (a) when the Constitution, the laws or settled judicial doctrines are violated; or (b) when acts are done whimsically, arbitrarily, or out of personal bias and animosity and of such gravity as to deprive the complaining party of due process. In the latter case, the office concerned loses its objectivity and its right to decide fairly. Thus, the Court is mandated to nullify its acts.

3. Administrative supervision of all lower courts. The Supreme Court is also vested with the power to dismiss, suspend or otherwise impose disciplinary penalties on all lower court judges. It also appoints, supervises and disciplines all other judicial personnel. Through

4. Control of admission to the practice of law and discipline of erring lawyers. The Supreme Court administers and controls bar examinations, a prerequisite for admission to law practice. The Court thereby affects the curricula of law schools. Further, it is given the sole authority to disbar, suspend or fine lawyers for violations of legal ethics. Through its supervision of the Board of Mandatory Continuing Legal Education (MCLE) Program, it is able to upgrade the standards of the law profession.

5. Rule-making power. The Constitution grants the Court the power to promulgate rules concerning pleading, practice and procedure in all courts. Furthermore, it empowers the Tribunal to enact rules concerning the protection and the enforcement of constitutional rights. This lawmaking authority enables the Court to safeguard the most vital part of the Constitution, the Bill of Rights.

## Effect of Supreme Court Decisions on Business

Because of its many functions, especially its duty to strike down grave abuse of discretion committed by any agency of the government, the Supreme Court inevitably affects business and investment. Whenever it does, economic indicators — like the stock market index and the foreign exchange rate — react. As a result, businessmen and business groups make known, usually through media, how they feel about the Court’s actions as shown in the following instances:

1. When certain laws, treaties of executive orders that regulate or deregulate certain industries are declared unconstitutional by the Court

2. When the Court invalidates mega contracts of — infrastructure projects

3. When it issues injunctions or restraining orders involving infrastructure, foreclosure of mortgage, or other specific business concerns

4. When it dismisses, suspends or otherwise disciplines justices and judges for violation of the Code of Judicial Conduct — especially as it relates to corruption, incompetence and delay of judgments

## Unconstitutionality of the Oil Deregulation Law

Let me cite some specifics. A few years ago, the Supreme Court in Tatad v. Secretary of Energy’ nullified the first Oil Deregulation Law. Immediately, it was denounced by big business for its alleged interference in economic matters that were allegedly beyond its competence to rule on. ‘The criticisms were so harsh that some even proposed to impeach the justices for supposedly overstepping their functions. Others wanted the Constitution amended to remove the authority of the Tribunal to decide economic issues.

What our critics overlooked was that the law had been invalidated precisely because it was anti-business, in the sense that it perpetuated a monopoly of the existing big, three oil companies in the country: Shell, Petron and (Caltex; and restrained the entry of new ones. Though not inclined to “interfere” in business, the Court had to uphold the Constitution, which mandated free trade and frowned on monopolies (or, in this case, oligopolies) as well as combinations in restraint of trade.

The net result? Congress had to enact a new Oil Deregulation Law, which avoided the objectionable and monopolistic provisions struck down by the Court. As § consequence, the industry was truly deregulated. Several new players — like Total, Flying A and Seaoil — entered the industry, thereby presaging a more competitive environment. Thus, the Court upheld free enterprise and was soon understood and eventually praised by the critics.”

## Nullification of Mega Contracts

More recently, the Court again crossed the radar screen of business when it nullified certain mega contracts, like those for the construction of Manila International Airport Terminal III’ and for the reclamation of a portion of Manila Bay.* Once more, the Court was be-rated by some business groups for allegedly violating the sanctity of contracts entered into by the government itself.

Again, the explanation is simple. When a so-called agreement violates the Constitution or the law, it is not a contract at all. Hence, the sanctity that characterizes contracts does not come into play. The Constitution and the law do not protect purported agreements (or the signatories thereof) that violate or ignore the very Constitution and law from which they seek protection.

By analogy, one who kills or maims another cannot be immune from prosecution simply because one spent money buying the gun used in the crime. Neither may sellers be entitled to collect the purchase price of a stolen vehicle simply because they invested funds in acquiring the car from the robber. The law shall not only stop them from collecting, but also hold them criminally liable as accomplices or accessories in fencing stolen goods.

In regard to injunctions, you may be pleased to know that the Supreme Court has issued strict orders banning trial courts from restraining government infrastructure projects.” Hence, those who invested in those projects need not worry that temporary restraining orders may unduly delay their projects and thus adversely affect their investments.

Moreover, at the request of the Bankers Association of the Philippines, the Supreme Court is now reviewing the rules governing the issuance of restraining orders in regard to the foreclosure of mortgages of nonperforming assets. I have been personally tasked by the Supreme Court to coordinate this effort.

Before I leave this topic, may I just add that a preliminary injunction is intended to protect the people — investors included —from harassment by the strong arm of the government. It is based on the notion that, unless restrained, the brute power of the police or the military may inflict upon the weak and the unprotected what is legally called “irreparable injury.” A preliminary injunction is thereby a necessary counterbalance to abuse and arbitrary action; it is not meant to prevent the lawful exercise of rights and prerogatives.

Viewed in this light, restraining orders are therefore really tools of justice and equity. Of course, as in anything, tools and instruments are in themselves neutral; they become oppressive and reprehensible only when misused and abused.

## Corruption, Inefficiency and Delay of Decisions

Let me now move on to a more widespread criticism of the judiciary, which comes not only from the business sector, but from the population at large. I refer to allegations of corruption, inefficiency and delay in judicial decisions. In my easier speeches delivered over the last several years, I have repeatedly condemned these plagues of corruption, inefficiency and delay — the “CID” — of our judicial system.

The effort of our Supreme Court to stamp out these CIDs is discussed extensively in our “Action Program for Judicial Reform.” Essentially, the program can be summed up as one that seeks to promote vigilance in disciplining erring judges and, at the same time, in improving the working and personal environment of desirable ones — particularly their remuneration.

On the punitive side, the Supreme Court has been relentless in its campaign to penalize judges who have fallen short of the Canons of Judicial Ethics. Thus, for the first time in its 100-year history, the Supreme Court admonished one of its own members in connection with the conduct of the 1999 bar examinations;’ dismissed from the service a member of the Court of Appeals;* and suspended in 2002 the presiding justice of the Sandiganbayan from his administrative functions for unreasonable delays in the disposition of cases before him.’ During the last three years, the Court has dismissed, suspended, fined or reprimanded more than 350 judges of lower courts for various administrative offenses.

I do not have the time to discuss all the various aspects of our judicial reform program covering such topics as judicial remuneration, continuing judicial education, alternative dispute resolution methods, revision of the Rules of Court to speed up processes, computerization of courts, re-engineering of administrative systems, and others.

Suffice it to say that our reform program has earned the support not only of the entire Philippine government, but also of multinational agencies like the World Bank, the Asian Development Bank, the United Nations Development Program, and the Asia Foundation; as well as foreign governments like Canada, the USA, Japan, the European Union, the Netherlands, and Australia.

Indeed, our Court’s vision is far-reaching. It aims at producing a corps of competent, dedicated, honest and courageous judges; and at speeding up the delivery of quality justice. Its ultimate goal is a judicial system that will enjoy the full faith and confidence of the people because its actions and decisions are judicious and correct, even though they may at times be unpalatable to current public sentiment.

## Epilogue

As my Epilogue, let me plead for your cooperation and help in reforming and understanding our judiciary. Specifically, may I suggest a few items for your consideration.

One. Please favor us with your opinions and suggestions on how to further improve our delivery of quality justice and how to recruit the best and the brightest to join the judiciary. At the very least, please support our reform effort to achieve world-class status by the year 2006.

Two. The Supreme Court has been relentless in its campaign to weed out the plagues of corruption, incompetence and delay. If you have been victimized by these evils, or should you know of others who have fallen prey to them, please write the specifics to the Supreme Court — through the Office of the Chief Justice or the Office of the Court Administrator — so that the matter can be addressed properly.

Three. Equally important, please do not agree to bribe judges. Sometimes, unscrupulous lawyers make such indecent proposals to their clients. Do not believe them; many times, they merely want to earn extra fees for themselves in the guise of lining the pockets of magistrates.

Four. Consult a reputable and ethical law firm before making a specific investment, in order to be sure that your venture or agreement does not collide with the Constitution, the law and jurisprudence. Often, investors make the mistake of consulting competent lawyers only after the fact. Even in medicine, of no help are the best physicians who are called when the patient is already in a coma.

Five. Regardless of the promised high profitability of prospective investments, please do not agree to dubious shortcuts in legal and judicial processes. When public biddings are held, be sure to comply with the requirements. Fortunately, our laws and rules are almost always couched in understandable English. Abide by them and by the rule of law.

Six. May I call your attention to the Court's numerous but largely unnoticed decisions that uphold the constitutionality of laws and the sanctity of contracts, level the playing field, and protect intellectual and other property rights of business. In particular, I ask you to peruse one major decision, Tañada »: Angara,' in which the Court en banc ruled firmly and unanimously in favor of the Philippine adherence to the new paradigms of trade liberalization, deregulation and privatization embraced under the World Trade Organization. More recently, it has repeatedly and summarily dismissed baseless complaints against the Central Bank governor” — complaints that, when first reported by the media, somehow attracted knee-jerk criticisms.

Let me close with the assurance that despite occasional misplaced attacks from disgruntled litigants or from uninformed and misinformed critics, our Supreme Court is sensitive to the need for good governance as a prerequisite to lasting economic growth. For this reason, it will continue to uphold the rule of law and the cause of predictable and stable jurisprudence in our country.

I thank you.
