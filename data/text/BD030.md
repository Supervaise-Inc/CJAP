# Battles in the Supreme Court -- Ch. 11: Epilogue: THE REAL VICTORY
Date:       1998-11-01
Publisher:  Supreme Court Printing Services
Source:     Battles in the Supreme Court, Supreme Court Printing Services — ch. 11 (batch-04 split)
By: Artemio V. Panganiban

In the heat of the deliberations on one of the foregoing cases, a fellow justice privately asked me how I made up my mind and thereafter wrote my Opinion in said case. "I study, I reflect, I pray and stick to my conscience" was my brief reply. My esteemed colleague was in the crossroads of making up his mind on whether to uphold his previous vote in the main Decision or to change it in the resolution of the Motion for Reconsideration. So I elaborated, "Fifty years from now when we will all be gone from this world, our decisions and opinions in this Court will still be discussed and dissected by lawyers, law students and the public. How do you want to be remembered especially by your grandchildren and great-grand children? More important, when we knock at the pearly gates, our Lord and Supreme Judge will ask us to account for our actions and decisions. How will we answer Him? If He should judge us according to the manner and standards we judged others, will we be allowed to enter the heavenly gate?”

Indeed, I have endeavored in all good faith to follow my conscience in all my actions and opinions in the Court. During the early months of my incumbency, I struggled to conform to precedents and conventional judicial wisdom. But I learned, as the months and years passed, that I found more fulfillment in expressing myself openly and decisively, even if it meant being in the minority or even being alone. As I said earlier, jurists are never diminished by their dissents, only by their reckless and thoughtless concurrences.

As can be gleaned from the discussions I wrote earlier, particularly my summaries of the "battles" in the Court, there were several justices who, like me, opted to participate actively in the deliberations and to write their opinions voraciously. May their tribe increase, and may they never falter in expressing their views diligently and courageously. Our Constitution and our people, I believe, expect no less.

Since I entered the Court three years ago, a number of respected senior justices have retired, including Justices Feliciano and Padilla. Soon, Justice Regalado, Chief Justice Narvasa and Justice Romero will also hang their black robes upon reaching compulsory retirement on October 13, 1998, November 30, 1998 and August 1, 1999, respectively.284 I will miss their wise counsel and measured responses to the challenges that faced the Court.

But I find consolation and hope in the judicial probity, balanced approach and fearlessness of their relatively younger successors, whose names were mentioned in the foregoing pages. I had crossed judicial swords with them, and many times I had found myself on the opposite end of the issues. Such majority, increased my respect and esteem for them.

Indeed, as shown in the "battles" I described earlier, we had freely discussed various subjects with varying degrees of passion, effervescence and tone, but always with the same commitment to truth and justice as we perceived them in the best light of our individual consciences. Chief Justice Narvasa and his contemporaries of ten years in the Court may be leaving us. That is the bad news. But the good news is that new and bright lights will be taking their places, perhaps cautiously at first, but fearlessly always.

This is not to forget the justices who have served and retired after a few years. Indeed, Justices Hermosisima, Torres and Francisco (and in the same vein, Justice Martinez who will retire on February 2, 1999) have had stints too brief, but that is not their fault. The important thing is that they, too, have contributed their share in enriching jurisprudence of this country; and they, too, have left their indelible imprints in my memory of great men.

As I close this book, let me say that the ultimate mission of a jurist and, in a larger sense, the Court itself does not depend on the number of unanimous decisions written and promulgated. Verily, speaking with a uniform and hackneyed voice on volatile and controversial issues is not expected of a free and thinking Court. Rather, the viability of our tripartite system of government and the meticulous safeguarding of our people's rights are best served by a divided Court and by magistrates who expound from various and varying viewpoints and perspectives. Indeed, the Court should be a veritable marketplace of free ideas, where jurists unrestrainedly express their most profound thoughts and ideas.

Thus, the real victory of jurists does not consist in their inclination to conform to conventional wisdom, but in their ability and determination to proffer and express their deepest thoughts, however extravagant or unorthodox. In the midst of seemingly insurmountable majoritarian precedents, presidential displeasure and even public ridicule, their willingness and dogged courage to sail into the stormy seas of jurispruden tialcontrov ersies, to pursue lonely causes and to risk their careers in the shoals of nonconformism bespeak an independent and objective Court. Magistrates should have no interest in litigants and in their victories or defeats, but only in principles, causes and philosophies — all with the ultimate objective of upholding the rule and majesty of the law. Justice is best served and jurists attain their real victory in the free and untrammeled expression of their scruples and credos, unaffected in the least by the fortunes or misfortunes of individual litigants.

I will have, in the normal course of events, over eight years more in the Court. I have immensely enjoyed the first three. With the Lord's grace, I look forward with hope and fulfillment to participating proactively in the future battles of consciences in the coming years.
