I am greatly honored to be invited to deliver this address before the First Global Biotechnology Forum — jointly organized by the United Nations Industrial Development Organization (UNIDO) and the Government of Chile — on the topic “Globalization of Justice.” To be able to come to Concepcion, Chile, I had to fly from halfway across the globe: from east to west and from north to south.

## Close Affinity Between the Philippines and Latin America

However, I find instant social, cultural and e identification with each one of you. You may want to know that my country, the Philippines, is often referred to as “the only Latin American nation in Asia.” And rightly so, because our people find unity with you in terms of being the only Christian nation in our part of the globe; and the only one colonized and directly influenced by both Spain and the United States. Hence, we love to brag that we possess the Latin American passion for life and love, obsession with music and dance, flair for the arts and culture, and craving for all things beautiful and soul-filling.

The Filipinos’ affinity with this great southern continent is probably one major reason why the Forum for last Asia-Latin American Cooperation (FEALAC) or Foro de Cooperacion America Latina del Este (FOCALE), composed of 32 countries,’ was held a month ago in Manila on January 28-31, 2004. That gathering marked only the second time that the foreign ministers of those 2 countries met; the first one had been held in Santiago, Chile, on March 16-18, 2000.

Similarly, I am greatly honored to be the only Filipino jurist invited to speak before this gathering. While the FOCALE ministers explored trade and tourism prospects between our two regions, today in this conference intend to discuss another area of cooperation — the preparation of our judiciaries in resolving the bioscientific and biotechnological issues that will surely start reading the courtrooms within this first decade of the twenty-first century.

## Territoriality Diluted by Recent Events

Let me start with the well-known postulate that traditionally, laws and judicial decisions are territorial 1 scope and are binding only within the jurisdiction of the issuing country. This concept flows from the centuries: old view that only within a state’s boundary is sovereignty absolute. ii

There is an emerging trend toward the establishment of international tribunals like the International Court of Justice, the Appellate Body of the World Trade Organization, and the International Criminal Court. By and large, however, the work of judges has been bounded by national legal systems; and their judgments, valid only within national borders.

But even within the national realm, judges have been confronted with many controversies that transcend their borders. ‘The advances in telecommunication, migration of people, the rapid changes in technology and the scientific realities of our ever-shrinking work have modified the absoluteness of the territorial doctrine. As a result, there is an ever-growing demand for the universalization of laws and of the dispensation of justice. Verily, issues concerning diverse subjects like international trade, banking, intellectual property, immigration, human rights, human dignity and criminal law enforcement have built up a burgeoning literature on the globalization of justice.

Ladies and gentlemen, the word globalization is most often associated with trade. The most vocal debates on the streets and in other public fora is whether MeDonald’s and Starbucks are proliferating too rapidly in developing nations, to the detriment of local fast-food establishments. Thus, the borderless economy has become a cliché in economic policy formulations.

Globalization, however, is actually “an interconnected process of institutional, demographic and cultural connection — not just economics.” It “represents the perception of the world as an interconnected whole and the consciousness that a number of issues can no longer be addressed at a national level.”

## Human Rights Promoted by Globalization

The truth is, more than trade and even ahead of trade, the promotion of human rights has been pro-polled by globalization. The global recognition of human rights has helped bring down the Soviet empire, transformed long-suffering South America, and strengthened international institutions like the United Nations.

Professor Hans Köchler, in his recent book Global Justice or Global Revenge, observes that the trend towards globalization has resulted in the erosion of national sovereignty and the primacy of human rights. He argues that “the concept of ‘national sovereignty’ must be redefined in the light of a general system of norms that is based on the universal validity of human rights. As norms of jus cogens, human rights are the roots of national legal: systems as well as of the international legal order — and thus transcend the confines of traditional state-centered international law.”

Apart from trade and human rights, globaliza 10 has also revolutionized other human endeavors like bank ing, finance, criminal law enforcement (especially the & tradition of fugitives from justice), commercial transactions; and recently, the war on terror, especially as it I conceptually been expanded to include the subjugation of Afghanistan and Iraq.

The wars on terror in these two countries have metamorphosed the concept of a just war: from an armed response to an invasion, to a more subtle “seat and destroy” operation against groups of terrorists wherever they may be found — in disregard of traditional state sanctuaries.

Indeed, the war on terror — like the emergence of human rights, the promotion of trade, and the enforcement of criminal justice — has eroded traditional “national sovereignty” and has given impetus to the need for a supra-national delivery of justice.

On the part of the judiciary, the fact that this globalization trend has never been more felt is reflected in our conference today. Indeed, the advances in the sciences and the cross-pollination of technology are bringing conflict cases before the courts of the world.

## Globalization and the SARS Epidemic

In meeting the challenges of the biosciences and biotechnologist, courts will need not only their traditional competence in legal norms and processes, but advanced knowledge of the new disciplines as well.

The SARS epidemic in 2003 demonstrated how erstwhile “scientific and medical issues are, at heart, questions about human rights and social policy.” The SARS virus respected no territorial boundaries, as it spread quickly to various areas in Asia (including China, HongKong, Singapore, Vietnam and Taiwan) and to Canada. The response of state authorities was determined not by science alone, but by law and politics.

While the SARS problem can be seen mainly as a question of containing the spread of a virus, and therefore a purely technical issue better addressed by scientists and physicians, a comparative study of our experience in Asia shows that political freedoms helped deter the spread of the disease.

Some countries initially suppressed the open reporting of the illness and allowed local clinics and health officers to present glowing but inaccurate reports to the higher-ups. Thus, the state was not able to respond to the danger promptly and properly; the disease became a virtual epidemic that could no longer be kept in secrecy.

Some other countries that had a free and robust press could not suppress the ugly SARS reports. And yet, built into the same open democratic system were barriers to health measures that could otherwise contain the problem — barriers like court challenges to the power of the state to quarantine suspected virus carriers, seal off infected neighborhoods, and condemn and slaughter the animal carriers of the virus.

The SARS phenomenon demonstrated the need for global cooperation to contain the epidemic. Authorities monitored passengers at points of origin, transit and destination. Countries of destination reserved the power to) reject suspected non-nationals and to quarantine their own suspected citizens.

In issuing worldwide alerts and advisories, the World Health Organization (WHO) virtually decided the fates of economies and industries. Yet, faced with the situation of every state belittling the extent of its SARS infection, the WHO alone became the most credible authority on the presence and the extent of the SARS infestation in a given country. The one lesson learned from the epidemic was that the universal “right to health” — protected under the worldwide Economic, Social and (Cultural Rights Covenant and guaranteed by the freedom provided under the Civil and Political Rights Covenant — could actually be secured by citizens through their local courts.

Similar phenomena of global proportions can be observed in the spread of AIDS, the mad cow disease and the avian flu.? As countries cope with the spread of the viruses involved in these diseases — not to mention (their mutations — the more we can foresee the powerful role bio-epigenomics will play in the economies and the well-being of all peoples in all nations of this planet.

## Biological and Computer Viruses Compared

The international reach and widespread consequences of SARS, AIDS, avian and mad cow viruses are matched only by another borderless virus in the information technology sector. The truly global extent of this technology, which in turn necessitated the global reach of justice, was demonstrated by the “ILoveYou” Internet virus, which crippled entire computer systems all over the world in the year 2000. Investigators were later shocked to discover that the virus had been created by a student in a computer school in the Philippines.

Yet, despite efforts of Philippine authorities, the culprit apparently got off scot-free. Our officials were unable to prosecute him, because he had not violated any Philippine law, which at the time did not yet punts computer crimes. When US authorities attempted to ask for his extradition, they were stumped by the traditional “double criminality” clause in the Philippine-US Extradition Treaty. The clause required that the request: state — the Philippines in this case — should have likewise criminalized the act. Even now as I speak, the web sites of the world are anxious over a new computer virus called “MyDoom.”

I speak about these two viruses — of the biological and the cyber-space varieties — to stress the need to globalize justice in all fields and to orient judges of various nations on the specifics of the new disciplines, particularly the biosciences and biotechnologies. Since viruses do not respect territorial or judicial borders, there is obviously a need for national courts to address and decide cases similarly, if not identically.

## Science Questions Needing Juridical Answers

To be sure, the people of today find themselves in unusual and unprecedented predicaments deeply entrenched in the overwhelming complexities and intricacies of the bio-age.

Disputes spawned by genetic engineering — whether of humans, animals or plants — will occur worldwide. Ideally then, the common problems brought about by the new sciences should be resolved uniformly by various national tribunals in order to avoid the evils of forum shopping. Indeed, conflicts of jurisdictions and judgments could be avoided by globalizing justice.

Controversies involving the biosciences and biotechnologies range from the ability to invent and influence life forms at the molecular level; to the technical deployment of reversible and permanent changes in crops, plans and animal life; and to the structure and function of human biology. To repeat, the consequences and the implications of these decisions transcend territorial jurisdictions.

Thus, courts have been tasked to decide cases relating to embryo ownership and disposition, including questions of fertility, abortion, reproductive rights, paternity and heirship. Indeed, during the last few years, people have begun to ask who should have access to the different forms of reproductive technology and -- the most controversial question — whether we should clone humans. Also, with the advent of cryogenic technology, questions relating to conception and child-bearing have become relevant. Similarly, in this age of biosciences questions relating to heirship become even more complex.

Then there is the question of the people’s access to goods and services — especially consumer product drugs and treatments brought about by scientific innovations. More specifically, we have seen the emergence and the regulation of genetically modified organisms (GMOs).

In Europe, GMOs have been regulated during. last ten years. In fact, the European Union recognizes the consumer's right to information and labeling as a tool to make an informed choice. Thus it has mandated specific labeling requirements to indicate the presence of GMOs in a product."

Courts around the world have seen the dawn of deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) testing. Considered elemental in the biosciences, it has gained recognition as a judicial tool for identification in both civil and criminal cases. In the United States alone, according to Time magazine, nearly 80 convicts by final judgments were subsequently exonerated, because DNA evidence had proven that they were completely innocent of the crimes attributed to them.

Recently, various controversies have been bred by access to genetic information researches done on individuals and the inevitable implications thereof on body part, tissues and genetic testing.

Finally, courts all over the world are swamped to-(day with cases relating to patents, trademarks and intellectual property rights brought to the fore by these new technologies. Thus, we are confronted with the question of whether an object is deemed a product of hun ingenuity -- thus, patentable — or a product of natural process. We have also seen a sudden increase in (questions of patent and trademark infringement.

## Globalization Not Antagonistic to Nationalism

Ladies and gentlemen, it is now time to summarize. During the last twenty-five minutes, I have discussed the emerging globalization, which is projected to extend to practically all branches of knowledge, and the resulting dilution of the territorial doctrine in law. Since human rights, trade, health, sciences and information are promoted on a worldwide scale, so must law and justice be globalized.

Before I end, may I just say that in my view, globalization is not necessarily antagonistic to nationalism; there is just the need to deemphasize the concept of sovereignty rooted in artificial borders. National identity is never homogeneous; segments of a country’s population easily find various political, spiritual, cultural, moral or economic values shared with other countries. Such values, however, do not necessarily include the entire national identity. Enough evidence of the merging of our national identity with foreign values is provided by the Filipino diaspora as exemplified by, among others, physicians and other professionals in America and overseas construction engineers in the Middle East.

As I close, may I congratulate the sponsor: this forum for their foresight in anticipating the judicial issues that may be brought about by the new discoveries and inventions of the biosciences and biotechnologies; and in preparing judges to resolve them properly and competently. May I also felicitate the participants for recognizing early on the need to combine science and law in responding to these problems. Armed with the awareness instilled by these meetings, they can go back to their respective countries, better prepared to meet the challenges of the bio-age.

I thank you.
