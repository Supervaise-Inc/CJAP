# Reforming the Judiciary -- Ch. 17: Republic v. Cocofed: Coco Levies Are Prima Facie Public Funds

One of the most important decisions issued by the Supreme Court during my seventh year in office was entitled Republic of the Philippines v. Cocofed et al., Ballares et al., Eduardo Cojuangco Jr. and the Sandiganbayan (in short, “Republic v. Cocofed”). This Decision, which I had the honor of writing for the Court, ruled that the controlling shares in the United Coconut Planters Bank - one of the largest banks in the country - should be voted by the government, because they were purchased with funds that were prima facie public in character. This meant that UCPB would be controlled by the government as trustee for more than “one million coconut farmers,” from whom these public funds - known as coconut levy funds - had been exacted during the regime of the late President Ferdinand E. Marcos. Former Senate President Jovito R. Salonga, who was the first chair of the Presidential Commission on Good Government which sequestered these shares, wrote an extensive summation of this landmark case. With his permission, I am reprinting his article which was first published in the January 2002 issue of the Kilosbayan magazine.

## Understanding the Supreme Court Decision: "Coconut Levy Funds Are Prima Facie Public Funds"

by

Jovito R. Salonga

Last December 14, 2001, the Supreme Court, by a divided vote in the case of PCGG v. COCOFED, et al., held that coconut levy funds are prima facie public in character and therefore, the government should be allowed to continue voting the sequestered shares in the United Coconut Planters Bank, while the main case for the reversion to the State is pending in the Sandiganbayan. Incidentally, the main case has been pending there since July 31, 1987 when PCGG filed an action against the bank for reconveyance, reversion, accounting, and damages. To make sure that there will be no undue delay this time, the High Court ordered that the Sandiganbayan (or SBN, in short) decide with finality the civil cases involved in the litigation within a period of six (6) months from notice and submit a progress report every three (3) months, on pain of contempt.

## Definition of Prima Facie

Some observers, including reporters and commentators, do not seem to appreciate the meaning of prima facie. Literally, it means "at first sight" or "on the face of it." In law, a prima facie case is such that it will prevail until contradicted and overcome by better evidence. Prima facie evidence is one that is good and sufficient on its face and which, if not rebutted and contradicted, will remain sufficient. In short, since the coco levy funds were collected by virtue of Marcos' presidential decrees issued during martial law which imposed the coconut levy, the Supreme Court ruled that coco levy funds should be considered public in character and, therefore, the Government should continue exercising its voting rights over shares acquired with such funds.

## The Background Facts of PCGG v. Cocofed

On our first post-EDSA journey to the United States on behalf of the PCGG, the State Department, through Undersecretary Michael Armacost, delivered to the PCGG on March 18, 1986, copies of 2,300 documents brought by the Marcoses from Malacanang which had been impounded by the Customs officials in Honolulu on or around February 26, 1986. After my appearance before the Solarz Committee in Washington DC on March 19, I testified by deposition at the New York Federal Courthouse in the civil suit for injunction filed by the Philippine government against the Marcoses. The purpose of the suit was to prevent them from transferring or disposing of the ill-gotten properties, specifically the huge buildings and real estate they had acquired in New York. On our way back to Manila, we passed by Los Angeles, California, around March 27-30. On March 28, Mr. Noble Soriano, a friend from Morong, Rizal, called me and informed me that his friend, Mr. Eduardo "Danding" Cojuangco, would like to talk with me. I said this would be no problem. Danding Cojuangco came and I listened to him with the least interruption. He justified his acts and told me about how he became close to the Marcoses. At the end of his explanation, I told him I would appreciate getting his fair and full disclosure in writing about his ties with Marcos and of his alleged ill-gotten wealth so the PCGG could verify the same and act on his case. He said he had a lawyer, Gaby Villareal (the son of Ex-Speaker Cornelio Villareal, who could submit such a. disclosure to the Commission. He would contact Villareal and instruct him to prepare it. Unfortunately, no such disclosure was submitted up to my last day in the PCGG on March 9, 1987 (See Presidential Plunder, 2d printing, 2001, p. 54).

In the meantime, pursuant to EO 1 (creating the PCGG and defining its functions), EO 2 (defining, enumerating the ill-gotten assets and requiring and/ or prohibiting certain acts), and BO 14 (empowering the PCGG, with the assistance of the solicitor general and other government agencies to file and prosecute all cases investigated by it under EO 1and 2) - PCGG sequestered the so-called Cojuangco firms, including the shares in the United Coconut Planters Bank, registered in the names of (1) the alleged "one million coconut farmers," (2) the Coconut Industry Investment Fund Companies (CIIF), and (3) Respondent Eduardo "Danding" Cojuangco. The levy and the coconut monopolies were imposed by means of a series of presidential decrees issued by Marcos during martial rule, namely, PD 276 as amended, then PD 414, PD 582, PD 623, PD 755, PD 1468 and PD 1699.

In accordance with Section 26 of the Transitory Provisions (Article XVIII) of the 1987 Constitution, PCGG filed a civil action for reversion, accounting, restitution and damages (reconveyance, in short), docketed as Case No. 0033 in the SBN.

On November 15, 1990, upon motion of Private Respondent COCOFED, the SBN issued a Resolution lifting the sequestration of the subject UCPB shares, on the ground that the private respondents (including Cocofed) had not been impleaded by the PCG G as parties-defendants in its July 31,1987 complaint for reconveyance. SBN ruled that the writ of sequestration issued by PCGG was lifted for its failure to commence the corresponding judicial action within the 6-month period ending on August 2, 1987 under Section 26, Article XVIII of the 1987 Constitution. SBN said that although these entities were listed in an appendix appended to the Complaint, they had not been named as parties-respondents.

PCGG assailed said SBN resolution in a Petition for Certiorari docketed as GR No. 96073 in the Supreme Court. On the motion of Cojuangco, SBN ordered the holding of elections for the Board of Directors of UCPB. PCGG applied for and was granted a Temporary Restraining Order (TRO) enjoining the holding of the election. Subsequently, the Supreme Court lifted the TRO and ordered the UCPB to proceed with the election of its Board of Directors. Furthermore, it allowed the sequestered shares to be voted by its registered owners.

But on motion for Clarification/ Manifestation of the Solicitor General, the High Court issued a resolution on February 16, 1993, declaring that "the right of (the private respondents) to vote stocks in their names at the meetings of the UCPB cannot be conceded at this time. That right still has to be established by them before the Sandiganbayan. Until that is done, they cannot be deemed legitimate owners of CPB stock and cannot be accorded the right to vote them.”

On January 23, 1995, the Court, through CJ Narvasa, rendered its final Decision in GR 96073, nullifying and setting aside the November 15, 1990 SBN Resolution which, as stated earlier, lifted the sequestration of the UCPB shares. The expressed impleading of Respondents COCOFED and others was deemed unnecessary because "the judgment may simply be directed against the shares of stock shown to have issued in consideration of ill-gotten wealth." Furthermore, the companies "are simply the res in the actions for the recovery of illegally acquired wealth, and there is, in principle, no cause of action against them and no ground to implead them as defendants in said case."

Six years later, on February 13, 2001, the Board of Directors of UCPB received a letter from the ACCR A Law Office, written on behalf of COCOFED and the alleged nameless one million coconut farmers, demanding the holding of a stockholders' meeting for the purpose of electing the board of directors. In response, the board approved a Resolution calling for a stockholders' meeting on March 6, 2001, at 3:00 p.m. On February 23, 2001 COCOFED and others filed the "Class Action Omnibus Motion" asking the court a quo, that is, the SBN, (1) to enjoin the PCGG from voting the UCPB shares of stock registered in the names of the more than one million coconut farmers; (2) to enjoin PCGG from voting the SMC shares registered in the names of the 14 CIIF holding companies including those registered in the name of PCGG. On February 28, 2001, the Respondent SBN, after hearing the parties on oral argument, issued the assailed ORDER. Hence, this petition for certiorari by PCGG.

The case had initially been raffled to the Court's 3rd Division which, by a vote of 3-2 (Justices Vitug, Panganiban, and Gonzaga-Reyes in favor and Justices Melo and Sandoval-Gutierrez against), issued a Resolution requiring the parties to maintain the status quo existing before the issuance of the SBN Order dated February 28, 2002. On March 7, 2001, COCOFED and the other respondents moved that this petition be heard by the Court en banc (the whole court). The Motion was unanimously granted by the 3rd Division; on March 13, the Court en banc accepted the referral. Oral argument was held on April 17, 2001 in Baguio City, where the Court defined the issue as follows: "Did the Sandiganbayan (SBN) commit grave abuse of discretion when it issued the disputed order allowing respondents to vote the UCPB shares of stock registered in the names of said respondents (COCOFED and others)?"

## The Decision of the Court

Simply stated, the issue is — who may vote the sequestered UCPB shares while the main case for their reversion is pending in the Sandiganbayan (SBN)?

The Supreme Court, through Justice Panganiban, held that "the government should be allowed to continue voting those shares inasmuch as they were purchased with coconut levy funds - funds that are prima facie public in character or, at the very least, are affected with public interest." The Decision reviewed the various cases in support of its ruling, particularly the BASECO and the Cojuangco, Jr. vs. Roxas cases, and then said: "To avoid misunderstanding and confusion, the Court will even be more categorical and positive than its earlier pronouncements: the coconut levy funds are not only affected with public interest; they are, in fact, prima facie public funds."

How did the Supreme Court arrive at this categorical decision after so many years of apparent indecision and vacillation?

1. First, it restated the general rule, namely, that the registered owner of corporate shares exercises the right to vote. This rule applies even to shares that are sequestered by the government, over which the PCGG. as mere conservators, cannot exercise acts of dominion. But the PCGG is authorized to vote these sequestered shares registered in the names of private persons and acquired with allegedly ill-gotten wealth if it is able to satisfy the two-tiered test, devised by the Court in the Calpo and Cojuangco, Jr. cases: (1) is there a prima facie evidence that said shares are ill-gotten and thus belong to the State? and (2) Is there an imminent danger of dissipation, necessitating the continued sequestration and voting by the PCGG, while the main issue is pending with SBN?

2. But from the foregoing principle, the Supreme Court, in the cases of BASECO and Cojuangco-Roxas, provided two clear "public character" exceptions under s: which the government is authorized to vote the shares: (1) where government shares are taken over by private persons or entities who/which registered them in their own names; and (2) where the capitalization or shares that were acquired with public funds somehow landed in private hands. These exceptions are based on the common-sense principle that legal fiction must yield to truth; that public property registered in the names of non-owners is affected with trust relations; and that the prima facie beneficiary owner should be given the privilege of enjoying the rights flowing from the prima facie fact of ownership.

3. The ruling does not give us details of the BASECO case. But it may be good for our readers to know the documented facts of this case. The Bataan Shipyard and Engineering Company (BASECO) was incorporated as a domestic corporation on August 30, 1972; barely 6 months after its incorporation, BASECO acquired from the National Shipyard & Steel Corporation (NASSCO), a government-owned and of dom controlled corporation, the latter's shipyard at Mariveles, Bataan; on October 1, 1974, BASECO acquired 300 hectares of land from the Export Processing Authority; then, the other assets of NASSCO were acquired on July 15, 1975, with the intervention of President Marcos. Various loans were obtained by BASECO from the NDC ($19 million in May 1975 and P30 million in September 1975; and P 12.4 million in January 1976 from the GSIS). A certain Captain A.T. Romualdez, who does not appear to be a stockholder or officer of BASECO, made reports and recommendations to the President, which were promptly and favorably acted upon by the latter. The evidence submitted by the solicitor general, based on Malacanang documents now in the custody of the PCGG, "proves," said the Supreme Court in its decision y private of May 27, 1986, "that President Marcos not only r e d t h e m in their exercised control over BASECO, but also that he actually owns well nigh one hundred percent of its outstanding capital stock."

4. As quoted in the present case: "The facts show that the corporation known as BASECO was owned and controlled by President Marcos during his administration through nominees, by taking undue advantage of his public office and / or using his powers, authority or influence, and that it was by and through the same means that BASECO had taken over the business and/or assets of the National Shipyard and Engineering Co., and other government-owned or controlled entities." The Court, said Mr. Justice Panganiban in his Decision of December 14, 2001, granted PCGG the right to vote the sequestered shares because they appeared to be "assets belonging to the government itself." Cited with approval was the concurring opinion of Justice Ameurfina Melencio Herrera, concurred in by Justice Florentino P. Feliciano:

“I have no objection to according to the right to vote sequestered stock in case of a takeover of a business actually belonging to the government or whose capitalization comes from public funds but which somehow landed in private hands.. "

5. The exception was cited again by the Supreme rule in Court in Cojuangco, Jr.-Roxas, as follows: "The rule in this jurisdiction is, therefore, clear. The PCGG cannot perform acts of strict ownership of sequestered property. It is a mere conservator. It may not vote the shares in a corporation and elect the members of the board of directors. The only conceivable exception is in case of a takeover of a business belonging to the government or whose capitalization comes from public funds, but which landed in private hands, as in BASECO."

6. By way of summary, the High Court said: "In short, when sequestered shares registered in the names of private individuals or entities are alleged to have been acquired with ill-gotten wealth, then the two-tiered test is applied. However, when the sequestered shares in the name of private individuals or entities are shown, prima facie, to have been (1) originally government shares, or (2) purchased with public funds or those affected with public interest, then, the two-tiered test does not apply. Rather, the public character exceptions in Baseco v. PCGG and Cojuangco, Jr. v. Roxas prevail; that is, the government shall vote the shares.

7. In the present case, it is not disputed that the money used to purchase the sequestered UCPB shares came from the Coconut Consumer Stabilization Fund (CCSF), otherwise known as coconut levy funds — a fact admitted by respondent's counsel (Atty. Teresita J. Herbosa) during the Oral Argument on April 17, 2001. Also in the October 2, 1989 case of Cocofed v. PCGG this court declared that the UCPB was acquired "with the use of the Coconut Consumers Stabilization Fund in virtue of Presidential Decree No. 755, promulgated on July 29, 1975."

8. As the sequestered UCPB shares were purchased with coconut levies, the Supreme Court held that "these funds and shares are, at the very least, affected with public interest," — thus, "private respondents, even if they are the registered shareholders, cannot be accorded the right to vote for them." As stated in Republic v. Sandiganbayan, "it seems plain that the lifting of sequestration has no relevance to the nature of the coconut levy companies or their stock or property, or to the legality of the acquisition by private persons or their interest therein, or to the latter's capacity or disqualification to acquire stock in the companies or any property acquired from coconut levy funds. This being so, the right of petitioners to vote stock in their names at the meetings of the UCPB cannot be conceded at this time. That right still has to be established by them before the Sandiganbayan. Until that is done, they cannot be deemed legitimate owners of LICPB stock and cannot be accorded the right to vote them.”

9. Regarding the contention that this Resolution was in the nature of a TRO and being interlocutory in character, it became functus officio when the Court decided GR 96073 on January 23, 1995; the High Court held that the truth, as stated in the COCOFED case in 1989 and quoted by the Court in the February 16, 1993 Resolution, remains the same, namely, the coconut levy funds, having been raised by the police and taxing powers, are "certainly the concern of the Government" and are "clearly affected with public interest." Private respondents have not "demonstrated satisfactorily that they have legitimately become private funds."

10. If they really believed that the final decision of the Court in Republic v. Sandiganbayan, promulgated on January 23, 1995, granted them the right to vote, why did they wait for the lapse of six years before asserting it (1) through their letter to the UCPB Board demanding the holding of a stockholders' meeting on March 6, 2001 and (2) through their Omnibus Motion of February 23, 2001, asking the SBN to enjoin PCGG from voting the sequestered shares during the stockholders' meeting? Note that the assailed SBN Order of Feb. 28, 2001 allowing respondents to vote is not based on any finding that the coconut levies and the shares "have legitimately become private funds." Nor is it based on the alleged lifting of the TRO issued by the High Court on February 16, 1993. Rather, "it is anchored on the grossly mistaken application of the two-tiered test mentioned earlier." The two-tiered test is applied only when the sequestered assets in the name of a private person are alleged to have been acquired with ill-gotten wealth, not when they are purchased with coco levy funds. Hence, in PCGG v. Cojuangco, the Court allowed Eduardo Cojuangco, J,.r to vote the sequestered San Miguel Corporation shares on the basis of his representation that he had acquired the shares registered in his name with borrowed funds and upon failure of PCGG to satisfy the two-tiered test. The test was, however, applied to sequestered SMC shares that were purchased with coco levy funds. In the present case, the sequestered UCPB shares are confirmed to have been acquired with coco levies, not with alleged ill-gotten wealth. Hence, the right to vote them is ged ill-gotten the two-tiered test but to the public character of their acquisition, which must first be determined.ter of their It was at this point where the Supreme Court decided to be "more categorical and positive " than its earlier pronouncements: coconut levy funds are not merely affected with public interest; "they are, in fact, prima facie public funds."

This brings us to the question — what is meant by the term "public funds"? The Court first gave a general definition, namely, the money or to any political subdivisions belonging to the State of the State raised by operation of law for the support of the government or for the discharge of its obligations. Then, it itemized the reasons why coco levy funds are public funds:

1. They are raised with the use of the police and taxing powers of the State, citing PD 276 (creating the Coconut Stabilization Fund - CCSF Consumer ); PD 961 (empowering the Philippine Coconut Authority to impose and collect the levy) ; and PD 1468 of June 11, 1978;

2. They are levies imposed by the State the benefit of the coconut industry for farmers;

3. It was judicially admitted by the respondents that the sequestered shares were purchased with public funds;

4. The Commission on Audit (COA) reviews the use of coconut levy funds;

5. The BIR, with the acquiescence of private respondents, has treated them as public funds;

6. The very laws governing coco levies recognize their public character. Thus, the 3rd Whereas clause of PD 276 treats them as special funds for a special public purpose. PD 771 transferred to the general funds of the State all existing special and fiduciary funds, including the CCSF. And PD 1234 specifically declared the CCSF as a special fund for a special purpose, which should be treated as a special account in the National Treasury.

Even former President Marcos himself, as the sole executive and legislative authority during martial rule, crossed out the phrase "which is a private fund of the coconut farmers" and initialed the deletion — as may be seen in "Attachment C" of petitioner's Memorandum - thereby manifesting the legislative intent to regard the CCS as public, not private, funds.

Since the government is the prima facie owner of the funds used to purchase the shares, and as one of the recognized rights of an owner is the right to vote at meetings of the corporation, for the purpose, among other things, of electing and removing directors, amending the charter, or making or amending by-laws, tho shares the government should continue to vote until and unless private respondents are able to demonstrate, in the main cases pending before the SBN, that the sequestered UCPB shares have legitimately become private (Cocofed v. PCGG, supra).

On the procedural issue of whether SBN committed grave abuse of discretion as required by Rule 65 of the Rules of Court, the Supreme Court held it did commit such an abuse, when it did not follow the latter's rulings in the Baseco and Cojuangco-Roxas cases. On the contention that the public nature of the coconut public funds was not raised as an issue in SBN, the Court held that the main issue of who may vote the shares cannot be determined without resolving the question of the public or private character of the shares and the funds used to acquire them. As decided in previous cases, the Court has the authority to waive the lack of proper assignment of errors if the unassigned errors clearly relate to errors properly pinpointed or refer to matters upon which the determination of the questions raised by the errors properly assigned depends (citing cases).

## Caveat - Limitations of the Decision

The Supreme Court sounded a caveat, defining the limitations of its decision: (1) The determination is merely prima facie, and should not bar SBN a final ruling, after proper trial and hearing, on the prayers and issues in the civil cases (Nos. 0033-A, 0033B, and 0033-F), particularly in reference to the ownership of the shares in question; (2) the decision that coco levy funds are prima facie public in character does not involve the classification of these funds, whether they are general, or trust, or special funds - since such classification is not an issue in the case; (3) this decision does not settle the question of whether the shares held by respondent Eduardo Cojuangco, Jr. are, as claimed by him, the result of private enterprise; this factual matter is to be determined in the cited cases that are to be taken up in SBN. The only issue settled in this case is the right of PCGG to vote the sequestered shares, pending the final outcome of the main cases in SBN for the reconveyance, reversion, and restitution to the State of these UCPB shares.

## Further Delay Will Not Be Permitted; Main Cases Must Be Decided by SBN Within Six Months from Notice

The resolution of these cases, which have been pending for about 15 years - since the administration of President Corazon C. Aquino when PCGG sequestered these shares in 1986 and filed the corresponding judicial action in July 1987 - has long been overdue. Every effort should now be exerted to finally settle this controversy. Petition for Certiorari granted and the assailed Order of SBN set aside. PCGG shall continue voting the sequestered shares until SBN cases (0033-A, B, and F) are finally and completely resolved. Furthermore, SBN “is ordered to decide with finality the aforesaid civil cases within six (6) months from notice. It shall report to the Court on the progress of these cases every three (3) months on pain of contempt. Petition for Intervention is dismissed inasmuch as the reliefs prayed for are not covered…. by this case.”

Concurring with Justice Panganiban: CJ Davide; Buena, De Justices Bellosillo, Mendoza, Quisumbing, Leon and Carpio.

## The Dissent

We quote pertinent excerpts of the dissenting opinion by Justice Melo, which four Pardo - who supported: namely, Justices Kapunan, tiago and joined "the result of the dissent" - Ynares-Santiago and Sandoval-Gutierrez:

"The BASECO case does not support the petitioner's position. It was proven in the BASECO case that 95.82% of the outstanding stock of BASECO, endorsed in blank by the owners thereof, were inexplicably in the possession of then President Marcos. Moreover, deeds of assignment of practically all the stock of the corporations owning the aforementioned 95.82% were also inexplicably in the possession of then President Marcos. Thus, in the case of BASECO, the directors thereof were merely Marcos nominees or dummies, it having been proven that he (Marcos) not only exercised control over BASECO but also that he actually owned almost 100% of BASECO's outstanding stock. Then, too, it was proven that BASECO had been able to take over and acquire the business and assets of the National Shipyard and Steel Corporation and other government-owned or controlled entities through the undue exercise by then President Marcos of his powers, authority, or influence. Upon these premises,, the Court held that the government properly exercised control and management over what appeared to be properties and assets owned by and belonging to the government itself.

"In contrast, respondents in the instant case are the registered stockholders. No evidence was presented before SBN showing that respondents were mere "tools of President Marcos and were no longer owners of any stock in the firm if they ever were at all... Respondent Cojuangco, Jr. obtained his shares by virtue of an agreement with the Philippine Coconut Authority (PCA) whereby, as compensation for exercising his personal and exclusive option to acquire UCPB shares, Cojuangco, Jr. would receive 1 share for every 9 acquired by PCA. The UCPB’s shares of stock in the name of the 1,405,366 coconut farmers, on the other hand, were distributed to them by virtue of PD 755, which authorized the distribution of UCPB's shares of stock, free, to coconut farmers. Other UCPB acquired by the CIIF companies. It is precisely the validity of these acquisitions which is under litigation in the main case pending with SBN.

"The view expressed by the majority is that the UCPB shares, having been acquired with the use of coconut levy funds, may turn out to be correct. However, since these issues are pending litigation at SBN, it would be premature to rule on this point at this time. Verily, the validity of the acquisition by Cojuangco, Jr., et al. of their UCPB shares is the very lis mota of the action for reconveyance filed by PCGG with SBN. To rule on this matter would be to preempt the said court. The argument that the coconut levy funds used to purchase the sequestered UCPB shares are public funds does not appear to have been raised before SBN; consequently, SBN did not rule on gravely abused its It would be absurd to hold that SBN gravely abused its discretion in not holding that the sequestered shares belonged prima facie to the government, the issue not the having been raised before it.

"To recapitulate, evaluated in accordance with applicable jurisprudence, the issuance by respondent SBN of its impugned Order dated February 28, 2001, is clearly not an act committed in grave abuse of discretion. Petitioner PCGG failed to persuade SBN, on the basis of the 'two-tiered test' enunciated by the Court in the San Miguel case, supra, that it was entitled to vote the UCPB sequestered shares. Verily, SBN was duty bound to comply with the jurisprudence laid down by the Court on the matter. This is certainly not a case of abuse, much on the part of more grave abuse of discretion, respondent SBN.. I vote to dismiss the instant petition."

## Excerpts of the Separate Opinion of Justice Vitug (Concurring in Part and Dissenting in Part) Joined by Justice Puno

"The fundamental rule is that the tax proceeds may only be used for a public purpose, which may either be a general public purpose to support the existence of the state or a special public purpose to pursue certain legitimate objects of government in the exercise of police power and none other. As a measure to ensure the proper utilization of money collected for a specified public purpose, the 1987 Constitution, restating another general principle, treats the proceeds as a special fund to be paid out for such a purpose. If, however, that purpose has been fulfilled or is no longer forthcoming, the balance, if any, shall be transferred to the general funds of the government, which may thereafter be appropriated by Congress and expended for any legitimate purpose within the scope of the general fund. An entity, whether public or private, which holds the tax money has no authority to disburse it or to pay any of it to anyone, the power to dispose of such money being vested in the legislature. Thus, the 1987 Constitution... mandates that no money shall be paid out of the national treasury except in pursuance of an appropriation made by law.

"Respondent Eduardo Cojuangco, Jr., in claiming ownership over a certain portion of the sequestered UCPB shares, advanced two documents: (1) an agreement in May 1975, where he appeared to have exercised his option to acquire the UCPB shares of stock amounting to 72.2% equity holding in the bank, at ₽200 owned by the family of the late Don Jose Cojuangco, Jr., per share; and (2) the 'Agreement for the Acquisition of a Commercial Bank for the Benefit of the Coconut Farmers of the Philippines, dated May 25, 1975, whereby the PCA purchased with funds from the CCSF the aforesaid UCPB shares from Eduardo Cojuangco, Jr., also at ₽200 per share. In the latter Agreement, it was stipulated that as compensation for exercising his personal and exclusive option to acquire the UCPB shares and for transferring such shares to the PCA as additional equity in the bank. In sum, correlating the two agreements, Eduardo Cojuangco, Jr., would contend, in effect, that he retained title over roughly 10% equity holding in the bank and established his prima facie right over the corresponding shares independently sourced from the coconut levy funds. Even if it were to be conceded that the said 10% holding in UCPB of Eduardo Cojuangco, Jr. could be assailed, pending a conclusive determination of the legality of such a retention, however, it would neither be right nor just to deprive him from meanwhile exercising his right to at least vote the same. For these reasons, I vote to grant the petition in part, and to deny it insofar as the shares of stock pertaining to the 10% of the 72% equity retention in the name of Eduardo Cojuangco, Jr., are concerned.

## Summary

Chapter 17 of 'Reforming the Judiciary' presented Republic v. Cocofed, which CJP identified as one of the most important decisions of his seventh year on the Court and which he 'had the honor of writing.' The ruling held that the controlling sequestered shares in the United Coconut Planters Bank should be voted by the government, because they had been bought with coconut levy funds exacted from more than a million farmers during the Marcos regime -- funds the Court declared 'not only affected with public interest' but 'in fact, prima facie public funds.' Rather than restate the case himself, CJP reprinted with permission the Kilosbayan summation written by former Senate President Jovito R. Salonga, the first PCGG chair, who had sequestered the shares. Salonga defined prima facie for lay readers, recounted the PCGG's 1986 recovery mission and his own Los Angeles meeting with Eduardo Cojuangco, and traced fifteen years of litigation from the 1987 reconveyance suit through the 1990 lifting of sequestration, the 1993 resolution, the 1995 Narvasa decision, and the 2001 demand for a stockholders' meeting that produced the assailed Sandiganbayan order. He then laid out the reasoning step by step: registered owners normally vote, the PCGG is a mere conservator, sequestered shares alleged to be ill-gotten are tested by the two-tiered test of prima facie ill-gotten character and imminent dissipation, but shares shown to be originally governmental or bought with public funds fall instead under the public character exceptions of BASECO and Cojuangco-Roxas, on the principle that 'legal fiction must yield to truth.' Since respondents' counsel had judicially admitted the shares came from the Coconut Consumers Stabilization Fund, the Court itemized six reasons the levies were public -- the police and taxing powers, the purpose of the levy, the admission, COA review, BIR treatment and the decrees themselves, with Marcos's own deletion of the words calling the fund private. The Court added a caveat limiting the ruling to the voting question and ordered the Sandiganbayan to decide the main cases within six months. Salonga then reprinted Justice Melo's dissent and Justice Vitug's partial dissent.

## Notable Anecdotes

### Anecdote 1

The Honolulu documents: Salonga recounts the State Department delivering 2,300 documents the Marcoses had carried from Malacanang, impounded by Customs in Honolulu, to the PCGG on March 18, 1986.

### Anecdote 2

The meeting in Los Angeles: on March 28, 1986 a friend arranged for Danding Cojuangco to see Salonga, who listened with the least interruption and asked for a full written disclosure through Cojuangco's lawyer -- a disclosure never submitted before Salonga left the PCGG.

### Anecdote 3

Marcos's pen stroke: the Court noted that Marcos himself crossed out the phrase 'which is a private fund of the coconut farmers' and initialed the deletion, shown as an attachment to the petitioner's memorandum.

### Anecdote 4

The six-year silence: Salonga asks why, if the respondents believed the 1995 decision gave them the right to vote, they waited six years before demanding a stockholders' meeting in 2001.

### Anecdote 5

Fifteen years and a deadline: with the cases pending since the Aquino administration, the Court ordered the Sandiganbayan to decide within six months and report every three months on pain of contempt.
