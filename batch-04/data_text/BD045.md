I am also grateful to the newspapermen and women who gave me the happy surprise of mentioning the book, by way of short citations and even extended critiques, in their well-read columns. Friends and family have also told me that they heard television and radio commentators mention the book on air. To the media practitioners and opinion leaders who have read and reviewed the book through print, I rest assured that I have taken note of your comments and helpful suggestions which I can use for my future books. ## Public Accountability and Transparency

It has been my practice every year to write and come out with a book to report on my work as a jurist during the year past. The seed of this idea came about in 1995, when I was sworn in as a member of the highest court in the land. As a jurist, I recognize that I- like all government servants - am accountable to the public and should therefore adhere to a policy of transparency in the performance of my duties. The public deserves nothing less from me than the certainty that I carried out my work conscientiously and to the best of my ability - letting only truth, the law, reason and my conscience dictate my actions.

The first fruit of this commitment to public accountability and transparency is Justice and Faith, which came out in 1997. It has since been followed by and radio, Battles in the Supreme Court (1998); Leadership by Example: The Davide Standard (1999); Transparency, Unanimity & Diversity (2000); A Centenary of Justice, (2001); and this book, Reforming the Judiciary (2002). I hope to continue this practice in the coming years until my retirement.

The Supreme Court Press publishes my books which have always had a limited press run. Copies of each new book that comes out are sent to government offices, public and private libraries, law schools, nongovernmental organizations (NGOs) and media. Now and then, various organizations and private individuals write letters to my office requesting copies, sometimes of all five books! This has always been a source of surprise and delight to me, as my books can hardly be described as literary potboilers meant for leisurely reading.

I believe that the primary reason for this phenomenon is that different sectors of society have become more and more aware of the need for transparency in government and even private institutions that have a bearing on the public weal. A good deal of the letters I have received in connection with the publication of A Centenary of Justice acknowledged this need for transparency. Commission on Higher Education Chairperson Ester Albano Garcia eloquently puts it in her letter to me dated March 4, 2002: "it can only be helpful to the Supreme Court — and to the judiciary as a whole — for the public to be educated about its constitutionally apportioned task as well as role in a democratic state."

These twin policies of accountability and transparency are especially important in the light of the extensive and comprehensive judicial reform program being undertaken by the Supreme Court. Aside from A Centenary of Justice, two of my previous books also included several chapters on the judiciary's reform program. Readers who learned about the program through these books have written me words of encouragement for the "monumental task which Chief Justice Davide, you, and your honorable colleagues are undertaking to restore the faith and confidence of the public in the honor, integrity and credibility of our justice system." I am heartened, and I know my distinguished colleagues are also, by this show of public support for our reform program. For this reason, I am concentrating on the “Action Program for Judicial Reform 2001-2006" in the present book.

## A Window to the Court

A Centenary of Justice, as with my previous books, was not only a report on my work in the magistracy; it also strove to provide a "window to the Court." Specifically, it discussed controversial and landmark cases that had caused diversity in the opinions and legal philosophies of the members of the Court. It recounted how the justices had argued, deliberated, voted and decided on particular cases.

One of the landmark cases I discussed in A Centenary of Justice was Social Weather Stations v. Comelec. That chapter became the subject of a column by Dr. Mahar Mangahas in the January 7, 2001 issue of the Manila Standard. I am reproducing portions of his column entitled "The Right to Be Subjective":

"The Supreme Court answered that question ['Social Weather Stations v. Comelec: May Election Surveys Be Banned?, which served as the title of the chapter] with a resounding No in its decision (GR No. 147571), May 5, 2001, in time for the May 14th election) to strike off Sec. 5.4 of RA 9006, which had banned publication of election surveys in the last 15 days before a national election or seven days before local election, as unconstitutional.

"So now we also thank J. Panganiban, who had earlier penned the January 2000 decision (GR No. 133486) protecting exit for polling against Comelec interference, deeming the 2001 case deserving of a chapter in his book, and thus helping to teach lawyers and laymen alike about the full meaning of freedom of speech in the Philippines.

A Centenary of Justice also contains a chapter on Perez v. Estrada, an administrative case filed by Secretary of Justice Hernando Perez before the Court requesting live radio and television coverage of former President Joseph Ejercito Estrada’s Sandiganbayan trial for several counts of plunder. By a vote of 8-6, the Court denied the Petition and maintained the absolute prohibition of live radio and TV coverage. The Decision penned by Justice Jose C. Vitug ruled that, in weighing the conflicting right to information vis-à-vis of the press and of the public accused to a fair and an the fundamental right of the d must be impartial trial, "the right of the accused must be preferred to win."

I wrote a Dissent, arguing that ten years after the Court first banned live media coverage of trials in 1991, it was already "technologically possible to uphold the right of the people to public information without violating the right of the accused to due process e." without impeding the orderly administration of justice.” Ms Beth Day Romulo commented on this chapter in her column "The Supreme Court earns public confidence," which appeared in the January 17, 2002 issue of the Manila Bulletin. She wrote:

“One of his recent disappointments was the ruling by the court that the Estrada case not be televised - a decision in which Justice Panganiban considers that the court was 'turning its back' on modern technology and the people's right to know. When you consider the wide viewing audience for the Estrada and Lacson Senate hearings you realize that today people are conscious of the justice system, and because of reforms that have been made, no longer regard it cynically as pro-rich and against the common people."

Some of those who have written to me describe the experience of reading these chapters on controversial and landmark cases as "being privy to ponencias and separate opinions which have shaped and directed our society's quest for relevant and meaningful justice, thereby "offering us outsiders even deeper insight into the workings of the Supreme Court" and "make[ing] us understand the pulse of the black-robe[d] members of the High Court in deciding national issues.”

## One Hundred Years of Justice

While it contains reports on the judicial reform program and discussions of landmark cases, as well as the ponencias and the separate opinions that made these cases noteworthy, A Centenary of Justice — by its very title — was essentially about the celebration of the Supreme Court's centennial. I had the honor of chairing the Centenary Executive Committee (Execom), which the Court had formed to conceptualize and facilitate activities commemorating that momentous event. From that vantage point, I devoted the first twelve chapters of the book to articles, lectures and speeches on the more than 50 activities and projects spearheaded by the Execom.

Although the book did not purport to be the final word on the High Tribunal's centennial celebrations, a number of people who read the book, especially justices and judges of foreign courts, have written to me expressing their recognition of "the importance of recording the year and the century in this way.” Further, I would like to share some of the contents of the letter I received from Justice Dorit Beinisch of the Supreme Court of Israel in connection with our centenary celebrations.

"Thank you for sending me your book, A Centenary of Justice. I already reserved a special shelf in my library for your books, and I am waiting for the next one. I was lucky to have the opportunity to participate in your centenary celebrations,2 and I cherish the wonderful memories of this unforgettable visit. I was impressed by the work of your Supreme Court, which has managed to hold honorable and orderly procedures in the face of a difficult reality. I feel that I am already familiar with your legal system. As you yourself aptly state in your book, 'Law is territorial in scope. Nonetheless, its basic principles — especially those on human rights and civil liberties — are universal.'"
